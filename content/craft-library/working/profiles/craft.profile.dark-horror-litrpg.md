## What it is

Dark and horror LitRPG uses the genre's familiar mechanical scaffolding —
levels, stats, systems — but inverts its usual reassurance: the system
itself is hostile, indifferent, or actively cruel, and its numbers exist to
make danger legible rather than to promise survival. Structural signature:
permadeath or severe, durable penalties; antagonistic system design (a
system that punishes rather than helps); and morally compromised choices
forced by the mechanics themselves.

## Why readers may care

The promise is that the numbers do not mean safety — real menace, real cost.
Readers of this subgenre specifically want a LitRPG structure that doesn't
soften its stakes the way lighter, more comedic treatments often do; a
death mechanic or system voice that behaves reassuringly here betrays the
subgenre's core contract more than it would in almost any other LitRPG
variant.

## Common forms and variants

Some dark/horror LitRPG keeps the system itself neutral or mechanical while
the *world* is hostile (survival horror with game mechanics); other, more
distinctive variants make the system itself an antagonist — punishing
curiosity, offering corrupted choices, or lying about its own rules. The
degree of moral compromise forced on the protagonist (not just physical
danger) is often what separates "dark" from merely "difficult."

## What this profile modifies in other records

- **Death and respawn** is central here rather than a background design
  choice: permadeath or severe, durable penalties are conventionally core to
  this subgenre's promise, and a soft-respawn system undercuts its core
  appeal more here than in any other subgenre in this corpus.
- **Weak-to-OP honesty** (referenced pattern pool concept, P5) is
  especially load-bearing: this is the subgenre where the protagonist must
  actually lose things, and where readers most reliably notice when they
  don't.
- **Effort equals reward** (referenced pattern pool concept, P9) carries
  similar weight for the same reason — gains that arrive free or by luck are
  particularly damaging to trust in a subgenre built on cost.
- **Fake progression** is a specific risk here: if numeric growth doesn't
  correspond to genuinely rising danger and cost, the subgenre's core
  promise (numbers don't mean safety) collapses into ordinary LitRPG
  reassurance wearing dark aesthetics.

## What it can look like on the page

- A death or serious failure that costs the protagonist something durable —
  a resource, a relationship, a piece of themselves — rather than resetting
  cleanly.
- System messages or mechanics that are ambiguous, misleading, or actively
  adversarial, rather than helpfully informative.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: graphic violence or dark imagery paired
  with a death/failure mechanic that costs the protagonist nothing lasting —
  darkness as aesthetic without mechanical teeth.

## Characteristic failure mode

Gore substituting for consequence — brutality that costs the protagonist
nothing durable, so stakes are asserted through violent imagery rather than
actually felt through lasting cost.

## Revision or design experiments

1. Audit the manuscript's stated death or failure penalties against what
   actually happens to the protagonist when they fail; close any gap
   between stated and paid cost.
2. For a violent or graphic scene, check whether it changes anything lasting
   about the protagonist's capabilities, relationships, or psychology; if
   not, consider whether the violence is doing narrative work or only
   tonal work.
3. Test one system interaction for genuine ambiguity or hostility — does
   the system ever mislead, withhold, or extract a cost the protagonist
   didn't fully understand they were agreeing to?

## Questions for the author

- When the protagonist fails or dies, what do they actually lose, and does
  it last?
- Does this manuscript's system ever behave adversarially, or does it
  remain a helpful, reassuring presence despite the story's dark framing?
- Is the story's violence doing consequential narrative work, or primarily
  tonal work?

## When this profile does not apply

A LitRPG manuscript with dark aesthetic dressing but a fundamentally
reassuring, low-permanent-cost system should be evaluated under the
classic-LitRPG profile instead, with dark imagery treated as a tonal choice
rather than evidence this subgenre's structural conventions apply.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where a project explicitly tracks death, failure, or resource-loss
penalties, whether stated costs were actually applied is close to
deterministic. Whether violence in the prose does genuine consequential work
versus purely tonal work requires interpreting the manuscript and remains
model-assisted.

## Original micro-examples

*Costless darkness:* A character is graphically maimed in combat, described
across a full page of visceral detail, and is shown fully healed and
unaffected by the next chapter with no lasting physical, psychological, or
narrative trace.

*Costly darkness:* The same injury instead permanently reduces a tracked
stat, and three chapters later the character is shown adapting their combat
approach around the loss — the graphic moment bought something the story
keeps paying for.
