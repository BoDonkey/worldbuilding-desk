## What it is

Crafting and economy fiction advances the protagonist without primarily
relying on combat, structuring escalation instead through material tiers,
quality levels, trade routes, and non-combat advancement paths — recipes
discovered, goods produced, markets navigated. Progression here is measured
in what a character can make, source, or trade, not what they can defeat.

## Why readers may care

The promise is power without violence: the satisfaction of recipes,
discovery, and becoming genuinely indispensable to the people and systems
around you, offering an alternative to combat-driven power fantasy that
still delivers real stakes and real escalation. This makes the subgenre
distinctly non-combat-forward among progression fiction's variants, which
readers specifically seek out for that reason.

## Common forms and variants

Some crafting/economy stories keep the protagonist as an individual artisan
or trader; others scale up toward running a workshop, guild, or trading
operation with employees and competitors. Overlap with base building is
common at the larger end of that scale. The degree to which combat appears
at all (as background threat, occasional obstacle, or genuinely absent)
varies and is a significant tonal choice.

## What this profile modifies in other records

- **Crafting and economy loops** is this profile's companion system-
  mechanic record and should be read alongside it directly — its guidance
  on scarcity, sinks, and market consistency is the core technical
  toolkit for this subgenre.
- **Systemic social consequences** is central rather than peripheral here —
  per genre research, this subgenre lives or dies on whether society
  plausibly uses what it has; an economy that doesn't cohere with the
  surrounding world's institutions and daily life undermines the
  subgenre's core appeal more directly than it would in a combat-forward
  story.
- **Conflict-escalation patterns built around combat do not apply as
  written** — action-economy, death/respawn, and similar combat-specific
  records should generally be set aside unless the manuscript genuinely
  mixes crafting/economy with combat content; applying them by default
  risks flagging nonexistent problems.
- **Stakes** still applies at full strength, but its concrete-consequence
  framing should be read through economic and social costs (ruin,
  displacement, a ruined reputation) rather than physical danger by
  default.

## What it can look like on the page

- Consistent material scarcity, pricing, and trade logic honored across
  scenes, with escalating stakes tied to economic rather than combat
  outcomes.
- A protagonist's growing indispensability shown through specific
  relationships and dependencies (a village that now relies on their
  goods, a guild that can't function without their recipe) rather than only
  raw output numbers.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: an economy that behaves inconsistently
  across scenes, or advancement that reads as combat-forward progression
  with crafting vocabulary layered on top.

## Characteristic failure mode

An economy that doesn't cohere — prices, supply, and scarcity that exist
only when the plot needs them, breaking the internal consistency this
subgenre's non-combat stakes depend on to feel real.

## Revision or design experiments

1. List established material scarcity and prices across the manuscript and
   check each mention for consistency, per the companion crafting-and-
   economy-loops record's own experiments.
2. Identify one social or institutional consequence of the protagonist's
   economic activity (a guild's response, a market shift affecting other
   characters) and make sure at least one is dramatized.
3. Audit the manuscript for combat-escalation patterns applied by default;
   remove or reconsider any that don't actually fit a non-combat-forward
   story.

## Questions for the author

- Does this manuscript's economy behave consistently, or do prices and
  scarcity shift for plot convenience?
- Is the protagonist's growing indispensability shown through specific
  relationships, or only through abstract output numbers?
- If combat appears at all, is it genuinely load-bearing, or does it exist
  because combat escalation felt expected rather than because this story
  needs it?

## When this profile does not apply

A story where crafting or trade appears only as minor texture within an
otherwise combat-driven progression narrative should use the progression-
fantasy or classic-LitRPG profile as primary, with the crafting-and-economy-
loops system-mechanic record applied as a supporting element rather than
this profile's full modifier set.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a manuscript centers non-combat, economy-driven advancement is
directly observable from its structure. Whether the resulting economy is
internally consistent, and whether social consequences are adequately
engaged, requires interpreting the manuscript's worldbuilding and remains
model-assisted; where material tiers or prices are explicitly tracked as
structured data, consistency checks are closer to deterministic.

## Original micro-examples

*Incoherent economy, combat-forward default:* A crafting-focused
protagonist's story escalates almost entirely through fights with rival
crafters over stolen recipes, with the actual economic logic (why a recipe
is valuable, who else wants it and why) left vague.

*Coherent, non-combat escalation:* The same rivalry instead escalates
through undercut pricing, a guild contract dispute, and a supply blockade
that threatens the protagonist's workshop — real economic stakes,
dramatized without requiring physical confrontation to carry the tension.
