## What it is

Dungeon core casts the protagonist as the place itself — a dungeon core
generating rooms, monsters, and traps rather than a traveling adventurer.
Its resource loop is characteristically closed: mana in, rooms and monsters
out, stronger invaders drawn in response, more mana generated in turn.

## Why readers may care

The promise is an inverted power fantasy: base-building satisfaction in
narrative form, with the reader rooting for what would conventionally be the
"dungeon" antagonists of another story. It asks readers to relate to
architecture and strategy rather than a mobile, socially embedded
protagonist, which is a genuinely different pleasure from most progression
fiction.

## Common forms and variants

Some dungeon-core stories grant the core limited mobility or an avatar
partway through, shifting toward more conventional agency; others keep the
core fully immobile throughout, leaning further into the inversion. Overlap
with base-building is common, since both concern managing a growing
structure and its inhabitants.

## What this profile modifies in other records

- **Character agency** needs a dungeon-core variant: choices about what to
  build, who to trust among invaders, and how to interpret ambiguous
  threats carry real agency without physical mobility. Don't mistake
  immobility for a lack of choice.
- **Power as sole motivation** needs a variant: growth is legitimately the
  protagonist's condition and situation here, not an unexamined default —
  look instead for whether the core has any want beyond growth once growth
  stops being urgent.
- **Negative space: problems power cannot solve** is unusually strong here —
  a dungeon literally cannot leave, which is one of the richest built-in
  sets of problems power cannot solve in any subgenre in this corpus.
- **Side-cast obsolescence** (referenced pattern pool concept) inverts:
  adventurers are conventionally antagonists here, and the recurring cast
  may be the invaders rather than allies who need to keep pace.

## What it can look like on the page

- Scenes built around room design, monster placement, and resource
  allocation as the primary strategic activity, rather than travel or
  direct combat by the protagonist.
- A core's "choices" expressed through what it builds or which invader it
  spares, rather than through physical action.
- The inverse, as a signal this profile doesn't fit: a mobile protagonist
  who merely commands a dungeon from outside it — likely closer to a
  base-building or general progression-fantasy profile.

## Characteristic failure mode

A core with no goal beyond growing — the loop runs (mana in, rooms out,
stronger invaders, more mana) without a story attached to it, since growth
alone doesn't generate stakes once its initial novelty wears off.

## Revision or design experiments

1. Identify the core's want beyond survival and growth, and find a scene
   where that want, not growth, drives a decision.
2. List the built-in problems the core's immobility creates (can't leave,
   can't choose its neighbors, can't verify a threat's intentions from a
   safe distance) and check whether the manuscript engages at least one.
3. For a recurring "adventurer" cast, decide deliberately whether they
   function as invaders, potential allies, or something in between, and let
   that relationship develop rather than staying static.

## Questions for the author

- What does this core want, beyond continued growth and survival?
- Does the core's immobility create real, engaged narrative problems, or
  does the story route around the constraint?
- Is the recurring "invader" cast treated with individual stakes, or as an
  undifferentiated threat?

## When this profile does not apply

A story with a mobile protagonist who merely oversees a dungeon or
settlement from outside it should use the base-building profile instead;
applying dungeon-core's agency and motivation variants to a conventionally
mobile protagonist would misapply modifiers meant for immobility
specifically.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a protagonist is structurally immobile (a dungeon core rather than a
traveling character) is directly observable from the premise. Whether the
core has a want beyond growth, and whether its agency is meaningfully
exercised despite immobility, requires interpreting the manuscript and
remains model-assisted.

## Original micro-examples

*Growth with no attached story:* A dungeon core spends fifteen chapters
building progressively larger rooms and stronger monsters with no
articulated want beyond "grow stronger," and no built-in problem (isolation,
inability to verify intentions, forced proximity to a nearby village)
engaged directly.

*Growth with attached story:* The same core instead wants specifically to
protect a small village it has grown attached to, which creates a direct
tension between "grow stronger" (which requires drawing in dangerous
invaders) and "keep the village safe" (which growth itself endangers).
