## What it is

A class-based system assigns a character a role (Warrior, Mage, Ranger) that
constrains and shapes what abilities and growth are available to them,
usually chosen once (or occasionally changed at significant cost). A
skill-based or freeform system instead grows whatever a character actually
does — practicing archery improves archery, regardless of any assigned
role — with no formal role constraining what's available. Many systems blend
the two: a loose class that shapes tendencies without hard-gating options,
or a skill system with soft specialization pressure even without formal
classes.

## Why readers may care

A class system promises identity and specialization: choosing (or being
chosen by) a class is itself a character-defining moment, and a class's
constraints create genre-legible archetypes readers can immediately
recognize and root for or against. A skill-based system promises freedom and
responsiveness: a character's growth reflects their actual choices and
actions moment to moment, which can make the system feel more personally
earned and less like an externally imposed label — at the cost of the
immediate legibility a class name provides.

## Common forms and variants

- **Hard class-gating**, where a class strictly determines available
  abilities, with no cross-class access.
- **Soft class systems**, where a class provides bonuses or a starting
  direction without hard-blocking other paths.
- **Pure skill-based growth**, where any skill can be developed by any
  character purely through use, with no formal class structure at all.
- **Multiclassing / hybrid systems**, allowing a character to combine
  elements of multiple classes, often at a cost (slower growth in each,
  a unique but weaker hybrid path).
- **Respec or class-change mechanics**, allowing a character to abandon or
  change their class, usually at significant narrative or mechanical cost —
  a design decision with major stakes for identity-based storytelling.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character's identity and social role in the story explicitly tied to
  their chosen or assigned class.
- A character's growth shown as a direct response to their in-story actions
  and choices, with no formal role label attached.
- A scene where class constraints prevent a character from taking an
  otherwise-available action, creating a specific kind of narrative tension.

## Progression, failure behavior, and cross-mechanic interaction

Class systems interact strongly with genre archetype and reader
expectation — a "Rogue" signals a set of narrative possibilities before the
character does anything. Skill-based systems interact strongly with
character agency and the want/need pattern, since growth directly reflects
choices rather than an assigned category, which can make character arcs and
mechanical growth easier to align explicitly. Respec mechanics, where
present, interact with identity: a system that allows a character to
casually abandon a class undercuts any story treating class choice as
meaningful, while a system that makes respec impossible or extremely costly
raises the stakes of the original choice but can also trap a character in an
identity the story wants to complicate.

## Common failure modes

- **A class system where the class never actually constrains anything**,
  functioning as a label with no mechanical or narrative teeth.
- **A skill-based system with unlimited flexibility and no cost to
  specialization**, removing any meaningful tradeoff from character
  development choices.
- **Multiclass or hybrid options presented as strictly superior to
  single-class specialization** with no offsetting cost, undermining the
  appeal of committed specialization for any character who doesn't hybridize.
- **Respec mechanics used to erase consequences of an earlier narrative
  choice** rather than to enable deliberate character growth, weakening the
  stakes of the original decision.

## Questions for the author

- Does this story's chosen class or skill system reflect genuine identity
  stakes, or is the classification decorative?
- If class-gated, does the constraint ever actually prevent a character from
  doing something they'd otherwise want to do, creating real narrative
  tension?
- If skill-based, is there a meaningful cost to specialization, or can a
  character freely become good at everything?
- If respec or class-change exists, what does it cost narratively, and does
  that cost match the weight the story wants the original choice to carry?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Identify one moment where a character's class or skill specialization
   should plausibly prevent an easy solution, and check whether the current
   draft honors that constraint or quietly works around it.
2. For a skill-based system with no specialization cost, introduce a
   tradeoff (time, resource, or narrative opportunity cost) for spreading
   growth broadly rather than focusing it.
3. If class identity is meant to matter, find a scene where the class
   itself (not just the character) is challenged or tested, and see whether
   it currently exists.
4. For a respec mechanic, check whether its cost matches the narrative
   weight the story wants a class-change decision to carry.

## When this advice does not apply

- Base-building and other ensemble-focused subgenres may distribute class
  roles across a cast (a settlement of specialists) rather than concentrating
  the comparison in a single protagonist.
- GameLit and non-numeric progression fantasy may have no formal class or
  skill system at all, conveying specialization narratively instead.
- A story deliberately satirizing rigid class systems (or their absence) may
  use either extreme for effect rather than as an unexamined default.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where a project explicitly tracks a class or skill structure, a coach could
report which abilities are gated to which class deterministically. Whether
a class constraint or skill-growth cost is actually honored in the prose,
and whether it carries narrative weight, requires reading and interpreting
the manuscript and remains model-assisted.

## Original micro-examples

*Decorative class:* A character is introduced as a "Ranger" and proceeds to
cast fire spells, pick locks, and fight in heavy armor with no narrative or
mechanical friction — the class label does no work.

*Meaningful class constraint:* The same Ranger is explicitly barred from the
guild's arcane archives because Rangers aren't recognized spellcasters,
forcing them to find an unconventional, riskier path to the information they
need — the class constraint generates a plot problem rather than sitting
inert.
