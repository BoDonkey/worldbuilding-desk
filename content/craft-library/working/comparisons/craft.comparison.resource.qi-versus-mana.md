## What it is

"Mana" and "qi" (also transliterated chi, ki, or qì depending on tradition
and romanization system) are two of the most common vocabulary choices for a
magic or power system's fuel, and they are frequently treated in genre
discussion as interchangeable — a "Western" fuel bar and an "Eastern" one
doing the same job. This record treats that flattening as a mistake.

Mana has a documented route out of the Pacific. The word is Austronesian and
appears across languages spanning the three regions Europeans invented and
imposed — Polynesia, Melanesia, Micronesia — so calling it simply
"Polynesian" repeats a colonial division the concept crosses. Codrington's
1891 missionary ethnography introduced it to English-language scholarship as
a supernatural power attaching to people and things; Keesing later argued
that reading was a grammatical error, since in Proto-Oceanic mana was
canonically a stative verb — to be efficacious, to work — naming a condition
inferred from an outcome rather than a substance anyone holds. The substance
reading is the one that travelled: through comparative religion, where
Eliade anthologized Codrington for a mass American readership, into the
1960s counterculture, and from there into tabletop spell-point systems and
the video games that inherited them. What fantasy calls mana is therefore a
Victorian misreading of an Oceanic concept, repurposed twice more on its way
to the fuel bar.

Qi, in its historical and philosophical
sense within Chinese thought and Daoist internal alchemy (neidan), refers to
vital energy understood as constitutive of the body and cosmos alike —
not primarily a resource one spends, but a condition one cultivates and
refines. Modern xianxia and cultivation genre fiction uses "qi" in a further,
distinct sense: a game-adjacent, often quantifiable cultivation resource
that borrows the term's prestige and resonance without claiming to represent
the historical or religious concept directly. All three usages are
legitimate; conflating them is the error this record exists to prevent.

## Why readers may care

The resource label a story chooses signals a promise about the *texture* of
power: is it something a character possesses like a tool, or something a
character *becomes* through refinement? A mana system read as external and
tool-like promises acquisition, allocation, and management — a resource
economy. A qi/cultivation system read as internal and identity-constituting
promises transformation — power as a marker of who a character is becoming,
not just what they can currently spend. Readers of cultivation fiction
specifically are reported to care more about *why* a character advances
(the internal logic and cost of a breakthrough) than about the raw numbers
involved, the inverse of classic LitRPG's number-forward appeal.

## Common forms and variants

- **Ambient/external mana** — a force drawn from the environment, gods, or a
  cosmic field, existing independently of the caster and merely channeled;
  common in Western high fantasy and many LitRPG systems.
- **Internal, embodied qi** — vital energy cultivated within the body,
  refined through practice, and inseparable from the practitioner's own
  growth and moral/spiritual state; the historical Daoist neidan framing and
  the frame most cultivation fiction gestures toward, even when simplified.
  When using "qi," state which transliteration and tradition you are drawing
  from: Mandarin qì, Cantonese hei, Japanese ki, and Korean gi carry distinct
  histories that one spelling flattens.
- **Hybrid or renamed resources** — invented terms (aether, essence, spirit)
  that borrow structurally from either tradition without claiming either
  culture's vocabulary directly, a common and lower-risk choice for authors
  working outside either tradition's specific cultural context.
- **Dual-resource systems** — mana and qi (or equivalent internal/external
  resources) coexisting in one setting with genuinely different rules,
  useful for stories wanting both an acquisition economy and a cultivation
  arc.

## What it can look like on the page

- A mana system depicted as a meter that depletes with use and refills with
  rest or environmental access — spend, recover, spend again.
- A qi/cultivation system depicted as a state that deepens through
  disciplined practice, where "how much qi" matters less than "what stage of
  refinement," and where a breakthrough is a narrated event rather than a
  number ticking up.
- A story using "qi" as reskinned mana — a spendable meter with a different
  name and no cultivation-specific behavior. Not wrong; worth choosing
  deliberately rather than defaulting into.

## Common failure modes

- **Treating qi and mana as the same resource with different names**,
  discarding the narrative texture each tradition offers and delivering
  neither promise fully.
- **Using "qi" without any signal of which tradition or transliteration is
  meant**, when the setting otherwise gives no context, which can read as
  borrowing prestige from a real cultural and religious tradition without
  engaging its substance. The same applies to mana, whose distance from its
  Oceanic sense is now so wide that most readers do not know it has one.
- **Over-quantifying an internal-cultivation system** into a spendable meter,
  undercutting the "power as identity, not inventory" promise that draws
  cultivation readers to the subgenre in the first place.
- **Under-explaining an ambient mana system's sourcing**, leaving its
  economic and ecological implications unexamined — who else can reach this
  force, and what do they do with it.

## Questions for the author

- Is this story's power meant to feel like something a character has, or
  something a character is becoming? Does the chosen resource label support
  that distinction?
- If "qi" is used, which tradition or transliteration is it drawing from,
  and is that legible to the reader, even implicitly?
- Would this system's promise change if the resource were renamed to a
  fully invented term with no real-world cultural weight?
- Who else in this world has access to the same resource, and what does
  that imply about society, economy, and power structures?

## Progression, failure behavior, and visibility

The two framings also progress and fail differently, which is often where a
reskinned system gives itself away. Mana-shaped resources typically grow by
capacity — a larger pool, faster regeneration, cheaper costs — so failure
looks like running out at the wrong moment, and recovery is a matter of time
or access. Qi-shaped resources typically grow by refinement — the same
quantity made purer, or the practitioner made able to hold what would
previously have harmed them — so failure looks like a foundation built too
fast, a stalled stage, or damage taken from one's own power rather than from
an opponent. A story that says "qi" but fails only by running dry has
quietly chosen mana's shape — a legitimate choice, worth making
knowingly.

Visibility differs in the same direction. An ambient resource invites
instruments, wards, and specialists who measure it, and becomes something
institutions can price. An internal, cultivated resource is usually private —
legible to the practitioner, sometimes to a master or a spiritual sense the
setting defines, otherwise inferred from bearing. That privacy is what makes
concealed cultivation, underestimation, and the sudden reveal of a stage
such durable plots, and what makes an appraisal ability change a cultivation
setting more than a mana one.

## Common exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

An externally sourced, ambient resource (mana) raises questions a purely
internal one doesn't: can it be stolen, hoarded, or weaponized as a
resource denial tactic against others? Can it run out at a civilizational
scale? An internally cultivated resource (qi) raises different questions: can
cultivation be accelerated unsafely, is there a risk of "deviation" (a
common trope in cultivation fiction, drawn loosely from real qigong
deviation concerns) from rushing refinement, and does the resource interact
with morality or intent in a way a purely mechanical mana system typically
doesn't. Both interact with class/role systems, crafting (enchantment vs.
alchemy/pill refinement), and social status differently — worth mapping
explicitly rather than assuming either resource behaves like the other once
combined with other mechanics.

## Revision or design experiments

1. Rename the resource, temporarily, to a neutral invented term and reread a
   scene that leans on it. If nothing about the scene changes, the story is
   drawing on the label's borrowed resonance rather than on any behavior the
   system actually has — worth knowing before deciding whether to build the
   behavior or keep the neutral name.
2. Write the resource's failure state as a scene rather than a rule: what a
   character looks like when it runs out, or when refinement goes wrong.
   Whichever comes naturally is usually the tradition the draft actually
   operates in.
3. Draft one paragraph, not for the manuscript, stating which
   transliteration and tradition the term is drawn from and what the story
   does and does not claim about it. Keeping it in the notes tends to keep
   the prose consistent, and makes an eventual author's note easier to write
   honestly.
4. If the setting runs both resources, write the scene where a practitioner
   of one meets a practitioner of the other. Systems that were secretly one
   resource with two names reveal it here: neither character finds anything
   about the other strange.

## When this advice does not apply

- Stories using neither term, with a fully invented resource vocabulary, sidestep
  this comparison entirely and are not obligated to engage either tradition.
- A deliberate satire or genre mashup may intentionally flatten the
  distinction as its joke or its point — provided that flattening reads as
  authorial choice rather than unexamined default.
- Settings with an explicitly syncretic in-world justification (multiple
  real-world-inspired traditions colliding within the story's own history)
  can justify treating resources as convergent in-world, which is different
  from an author failing to distinguish them at the craft level.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a manuscript's resource system reads as ambient/external or
internal/embodied, and whether its vocabulary choices are used with
apparent cultural awareness, requires interpreting the text — no
deterministic check substitutes for this. A coach can surface this
comparison as a framework for the author's own review of a system already in
place, or as a design prompt before one is built, but cannot verify from a
system's stat sheet alone which narrative promise it is actually keeping in
the prose.

## Original micro-examples

*Reskinned mana:* A cultivator's "qi pool" depletes by a fixed number per
spell cast and refills after eight hours of rest, functioning identically to
a mana bar with no cultivation-specific behavior — a legitimate choice, but
one that trades away the "power as identity" promise cultivation readers
often want.

*Internal cultivation:* The same character instead measures progress by
named stages (Foundation, Core Formation) reached through sustained,
risk-bearing practice, where a breakthrough is a dramatized turning point
tied to the character's discipline and choices rather than a resource total
crossing a threshold.

doing something wrong, and is using a word with a history worth knowing.
