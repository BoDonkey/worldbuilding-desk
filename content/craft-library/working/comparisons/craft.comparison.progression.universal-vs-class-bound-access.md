## What it is

Universal access means a setting's power system is available, in
principle, to anyone who trains, tries, or meets the same conditions
regardless of birth or status — a farmer's child and a noble's child face
the same starting odds. Class-bound access means the system (or meaningful
parts of it) is gated behind something a character doesn't choose: a
bloodline, a caste, an institutionally granted class, a rare awakening.
This is a distinct design axis from the classes-versus-skill-based-growth
comparison, which addresses how progression is *structured* once a
character has access; this record addresses who *gets* access in the first
place, which has different and often larger implications for a setting's
social structure.

## Why readers may care

A universal-access system implies (whether or not the story engages it) a
society where merit and effort can plausibly overturn inherited status,
since anyone motivated enough can theoretically climb. A class-bound
system implies a society where power reinforces existing hierarchy, since
access itself is inherited or institutionally controlled — closer to real-
world structures of privilege, and often richer ground for stories
examining inequality directly. Readers register which kind of system a
story has built, consciously or not, and a story that treats a class-bound
system's access barriers as incidental rather than structurally
significant can read as having missed its own worldbuilding's
implications.

## Common forms and variants

- **Fully universal access**, where anyone who trains or attempts the
  system's methods can succeed, differing only in aptitude and effort —
  the closest to a pure meritocratic power fantasy.
- **Nominally universal, practically gated access**, where access is
  technically open but requires resources, training, or opportunity that
  aren't equally distributed — a more realistic and often more interesting
  middle ground, since it lets a story examine access inequality without
  making it an explicit magical rule.
- **Bloodline-gated access**, where only those born with a specific
  lineage can access some or all of the system, directly tying power to
  inherited status.
- **Institutionally gated access**, where a class or role must be granted
  by an authority (a guild, a government, a system's own arbitrary
  selection), making the gatekeepers themselves a source of narrative and
  political tension.
- **Awakening-gated access**, where the system selects individuals through
  an unpredictable or poorly understood process, creating a different kind
  of arbitrariness than birth-based gating — neither earned nor
  inherited, simply chosen.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character's access to power directly traceable to their birth,
  institutional standing, or an arbitrary selection process, with the
  story engaging what that implies about who else does and doesn't have
  access.
- A nominally universal system whose practical access is shown to be
  unequally distributed through resource or opportunity constraints,
  dramatized through specific characters who can't actually access what's
  technically available to them.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a class-bound or gated system where the
  gating criteria are never examined as socially significant, treated as
  pure narrative convenience rather than a fact about how this society
  actually works.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and cross-mechanic interaction

Access models interact directly with the systemic-social-consequences
pattern: a bloodline-gated system implies institutions (inheritance law,
aristocratic structures) a story can choose to engage; an
institutionally-gated system implies gatekeepers whose selection criteria
and biases become a source of political tension. They also interact with
the classes-versus-skill-based-growth comparison, since access and
structure are often conflated in casual worldbuilding but function as
genuinely separate design questions — a system can be universally
accessible and class-structured (anyone can become any class through
effort) or class-bound and skill-based (only certain bloodlines can use
the freeform skill system at all).

Whether access is *visible* matters as much as how it is distributed. If a
bloodline, awakening, or granted class leaves a legible mark — an eye color,
a brand, a public ceremony, a record only officials can query — then
exclusion can be enforced casually and continuously, and the story acquires
the texture of a society sorting people on sight. If access is invisible
until demonstrated, the same system produces passing, concealment, discovery
plots, and institutions that must test rather than look. Reader visibility
is again a separate choice: a reader told early that the protagonist carries
a gated bloodline reads their exclusion as dramatic irony, while a reader
who learns it alongside the character reads the same scene as revelation.
Neither is more honest; they promise different books.

## Common failure modes

- **Unexamined gating**, where a class-bound or bloodline-restricted
  system's social implications are never engaged, treated as a purely
  mechanical fact with no bearing on the setting's politics or inequality.
- **Nominal universality with no practical examination**, where a system
  is stated to be open to anyone but the story never shows or
  acknowledges the practical barriers (resources, opportunity, training
  access) that would realistically limit who actually succeeds.
- **Access criteria that shift for plot convenience**, where a
  bloodline-gated system suddenly grants access to a non-lineage character
  with no explanation, undermining the stated rule's credibility.

## Questions for the author

- Is access to this world's power system universal, class-bound, or
  nominally universal but practically gated — and does the story treat
  that distinction as meaningful?
- If access is gated by birth or institution, does the story engage what
  that implies about the setting's social structure, even briefly?
- If access is nominally universal, are there characters who are
  technically eligible but practically excluded by resource or opportunity
  constraints?
- Are the system's stated access rules honored consistently, or does an
  exception occur with no explanation?

## Revision or design experiments

1. State explicitly whether this world's power access is universal,
   gated, or nominally universal, and check whether the story's actual
   depicted cast reflects that choice consistently.
2. For a gated system with no examined social consequence, add a scene
   showing a character excluded by the gate, and let their exclusion
   matter to the plot.
3. For a nominally universal system, find or add a character who
   illustrates the gap between technical and practical access.
4. Audit any exception to the stated access rule for a credible
   explanation.

## When this advice does not apply

- Very lightweight settings where the power system is never load-bearing
  for social or political plot don't need the full examination this
  pattern describes.
- A story explicitly satirizing meritocratic myths (a "universal access"
  system revealed to be quietly gated in practice, as the story's actual
  point) is using the gap between stated and practical access
  deliberately.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a system's stated access rules are honored consistently across the
manuscript is close to deterministic where explicitly tracked. Whether the
story adequately engages the social implications of its access model
requires interpreting the worldbuilding and remains model-assisted.

## Original micro-examples

*Unexamined gating:* A magic system is established as available only to a
specific bloodline, and the story never shows or mentions anyone outside
that bloodline reacting to, resenting, or being affected by the
exclusion — the gate exists but does no social work.

*Examined gating:* The same bloodline-gated system instead is shown
sustaining an entrenched aristocracy, with a specific non-lineage character
whose resentment of the system (or whose discovery of an unexpected,
unexplained access to it) drives real plot tension.
