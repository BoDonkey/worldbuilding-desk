## What it is

A double is a duplicate — a twin, a shapeshifter, a copy, a mirror-self. An
impostor is someone occupying an identity that is not theirs. The two are
often treated as one convention because they produce the same structural
situation: a person in the story is not who the people around them believe,
and the story's tension comes from that gap.

It is adjacent to hidden identity, which this library treats separately.
There, a character conceals something true about themselves. Here, someone is
actively presenting as another specific person — which raises a different
question, since a real person is being displaced.

## Why readers may care

The convention is a pure information-control instrument, and it works in
either configuration. If the reader knows and the characters do not, every
ordinary scene acquires dread: watching someone confide in the wrong person
is uncomfortable in a way little else reproduces. If the reader does not
know, the eventual reveal reframes everything they have read, which is a
distinct and stronger single-use pleasure.

Beneath that sits the older appeal: the double asks what a person actually
consists of. If someone has your memories, your capabilities, and your face,
what is missing — and does the story think the answer matters?

## Common forms and variants

- **The replacement** — the original removed and someone installed in their
  place, with the question of what happened to them running underneath.
- **The infiltrator** — an impostor with an objective, where the tension is
  whether they will be caught before achieving it.
- **The unwitting double** — a duplicate who believes they are the original,
  which makes them sympathetic and the situation tragic rather than sinister.
- **The mirror self** — a double who represents what the protagonist could
  become, closer to a thematic device than a deception plot.
- **The system copy** — a genre-specific version where duplication is a
  mechanic rather than a magic, which raises the ownership and identity
  questions explicitly.
- **The suspected impostor** — nobody is a double, and the fear that someone
  might be does the work. Cheap to set up, and surprisingly durable.

## What it can look like on the page

- Small wrongnesses noticed by one character and dismissed by everyone else.
- A test someone devises, and what it costs to administer it.
- A scene the reader watches through their knowledge of the deception, where
  nothing overt happens.
- Conversely: an impostor who is never nearly caught, which converts the
  situation from tension into a waiting period.

## Common failure modes

- **The undetectable double.** A duplicate so perfect that no one could ever
  find out, which removes the possibility of near-misses and therefore most
  of the tension.
- **The idiot ball.** Characters failing to notice what the reader can see
  plainly, which makes them look foolish rather than deceived.
- **The reveal with no aftermath.** The impostor exposed, and the story
  moving on without the damage — the trust broken, the decisions made on
  false information.
- **The vanished original.** A person displaced and never accounted for,
  which the story usually owes an answer to.
- **Duplication without a question.** A copy that raises no issue about
  identity in a story where it plainly should.

## When straightforward execution is the right choice

The plain infiltrator plot is durable and needs no reinvention. Establish the
impostor, let the reader know before the characters do, give one person
suspicions nobody credits, and let the pressure build toward exposure.
Readers know the shape and the recognition is part of the pleasure.

The one requirement is that catching them be possible. A double who could be
found out — through a detail, a relationship, a test — generates tension in
every scene; a perfect one generates none, because nothing is at stake in any
individual moment. That is the difference between a working version and a
frustrating one, and it is a design decision more than a writing one.

## Questions for the author

- Who could find out, and what would it take?
- Does the reader know? Should they, and from when?
- What happened to the person being impersonated, and does the story answer?
- What does the impostor want that requires being this specific person?
- After the reveal, what is broken that cannot be repaired by knowing?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Give one character a genuine reason for suspicion and no way to prove it,
   and follow them for a chapter.
2. Write the near-miss: the impostor almost exposed, and what it costs them
   to recover.
3. Try the other information configuration — tell the reader early instead of
   late, or the reverse — for one scene, and see which story you would rather
   write.
4. Write the aftermath scene where someone realizes what they said to the
   wrong person.

## When this advice does not apply

- Comedy, where mistaken identity is farce and scrutinizing detection would
  break the machine.
- Thematic doubles that are not deception plots at all — a mirror-self used
  as a device about the protagonist's nature, where nobody is fooled.
- Settings where duplication is ordinary and untroubling, and the identity
  question genuinely does not arise.
- Very short work, where the tension has no room to accumulate.

## Evidence and detection limits

Almost nothing here is detectable. A coach cannot identify an impostor plot
from the text without understanding the story, and attempts to infer one from
surface features would be guessing.

Where an author raises the subject, the coach can reason about the questions
above: who could detect, what the reader knows and when, whether the
displaced original is accounted for. It should not diagnose an "idiot ball"
from the text — whether a character's failure to notice is plausible depends
on characterization the coach is reading interpretively, and readers
routinely disagree about exactly this.

## Original micro-examples

*Undetectable:* the copy had his memories, his mannerisms, and his mana
signature, and there was no test in the world that could tell them apart. The
next forty chapters are a wait.

*Detectable:* the copy had his memories up to the night of the fire, which
was enough for everyone except his sister, who had spent that night with him
and had never told anyone what he said.

## Sources and confidence notes

Vladimir Propp ([[src.book.propp-morphology-of-the-folktale]]) supplies the
structural observation that false-hero and unrecognized-hero functions recur
as distinct narrative roles, used here as a lens rather than a claim about
all traditions — the same limit this library applies to Propp elsewhere.
Orson Scott Card ([[src.book.card-characters-and-viewpoint]]) supplies the
reader-knowledge framing behind the dramatic-irony argument. The taxonomy,
the detectability requirement, and the failure modes are this drafting pass's
synthesis ([[src.synthesis.craft-library-editorial]]).

`source_confidence` is `limited`. The double has a substantial critical
literature — the Gothic doppelgänger, the uncanny, questions of identity and
duplication — none of which was available to this pass, and a later pass
should bring it in. This record reasons about the convention as a working
structure rather than analyzing it, and the identity questions it raises are
posed rather than answered.
