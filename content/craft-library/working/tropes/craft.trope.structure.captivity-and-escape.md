## What it is

A captivity structure confines the protagonist: a prison, a siege, a bonded
service, a system that will not let them leave, a household they cannot walk
out of. The escape structure is its resolution, and the two are usually
studied together because the second is what the first is for — though a
captivity story can also end in release, acceptance, or the discovery that
the cage was never the real constraint.

What distinguishes the form is the direction of its pressure. Most stories
expand: the protagonist's options grow as they do. Captivity narrows, and the
reader's attention goes to what remains available rather than to what is
being acquired.

## Why readers may care

Constraint concentrates. A protagonist with three possible actions is more
interesting per action than one with thirty, and a reader tracks a small set
of resources — a smuggled tool, a guard's routine, a fellow prisoner's
loyalty — with an intensity that an open world rarely produces.

The form also does something progression fiction structurally needs: it
supplies a reason for a powerful character to be unable to solve a problem
with power. A cage that holds someone strong has to be a real cage, and
building one is often more interesting than building a stronger opponent.

## Common forms and variants

- **The physical prison** — walls, guards, a route out; the escape is a plan
  and the story is its execution.
- **The systemic cage** — confinement enforced by the world's rules rather
  than its architecture: a binding, a debt, a curse, a floor that will not
  open.
- **The social captivity** — a household, marriage, sect, or station the
  protagonist cannot leave without losing everything, where the walls are
  other people.
- **The siege** — captivity from the outside in, where the protagonist is
  confined by what is waiting rather than by what holds them.
- **The willing cage** — confinement the protagonist chose or maintains, where
  the story's question is why they stay.
- **The escape that fails** — an attempt that costs, which is often what makes
  a later attempt matter.

## What it can look like on the page

- An inventory of what the protagonist actually has, revisited as it changes.
- A routine established in detail, so a deviation from it is legible as an
  event.
- Relationships formed under pressure with people the protagonist would not
  otherwise have chosen.
- Conversely: a captivity the protagonist could plainly end at any point, or
  a cage whose rules change whenever the plot needs an exit.

## Common failure modes

- **The soft cage.** Confinement that never actually prevents anything the
  story wants to happen.
- **The convenient key.** An escape route that appears rather than being
  built, discarding the accumulated tension of the confinement.
- **Captivity as a pause.** Chapters in which nothing changes, on the
  assumption that constraint is inherently tense. It is not; narrowing is.
- **Unexamined recovery.** Release followed immediately by a protagonist
  unaffected by what happened to them.
- **The cage that only holds the protagonist.** Confinement whose rules
  evidently do not apply to anyone else, which reads as authorial rather than
  structural.

## When straightforward execution is the right choice

The prison break is one of the most reliably satisfying plain structures
available. Establish the cage, establish its rules, let the protagonist
accumulate small advantages, and pay it off. Readers know the shape and enjoy
it precisely because they know it — the pleasure is in the specifics of this
cage and this plan, not in surprise about the form.

The one thing straightforward execution requires is that the rules hold. A
cage the author respects is tense; a cage the author bends is a delay. Every
other virtue of the plain version depends on that.

## Questions for the author

- What exactly holds this character, and has the reader been shown it
  working?
- What do they have? What have they lost since the last chapter?
- Why can they not simply use their power here — and is that reason as strong
  as their power is?
- Who else is confined, and what do the rules do to them?
- What does release cost, and what does it leave behind?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write the rules of the cage as a short list, then find the scene where the
   plot wanted one bent.
2. Inventory what the protagonist has at three points in the confinement. If
   the list does not change, the captivity is a pause rather than a
   narrowing.
3. Give the first escape attempt a real cost and see what it does to the
   second.
4. Write one page from the perspective of someone who has been here longer
   and stopped trying.

## When this advice does not apply

- Brief confinements used as a beat rather than a structure, where working
  out the cage's economy would be disproportionate.
- Stories where the captivity is metaphorical — a life, a role, an addiction —
  and physical-cage questions would misread the subject.
- Heist structures, which invert this one: breaking in rather than out, and
  treated in their own record.
- Depictions of real imprisonment, where this record's structural questions
  are not the relevant ones and the subject deserves sources this library
  does not hold.

## Evidence and detection limits

A coach can note when a setting persists across many consecutive chapters,
whether the same location dominates, and whether stated constraints recur
consistently. Contradictions between a stated rule and a later event are the
closest thing to a deterministic check available here.

Whether a cage is tense, whether an escape is earned, and whether release has
been processed are semantic judgments. A long confinement is not evidence of
a stalled book, and a quick escape is not evidence of a soft cage. The coach
should surface the rules the text has stated and ask whether the story has
kept them.

## Original micro-examples

*Soft cage:* the collar suppressed his mana, except when he needed it, and
the door was locked, except that Sera had a key.

*Cage with rules:* the collar suppressed his mana entirely, and had done for
eleven weeks, and the only thing he had gained in that time was the knowledge
that the third guard could not read the duty roster and had to ask.

## Sources and confidence notes

Christopher Booker ([[src.book.booker-seven-basic-plots]]) supplies the
general treatment of confinement-and-release as a recurring narrative shape;
Janet Burroway ([[src.book.burroway-writing-fiction]]) supplies the
principles about constraint, scene pressure, and setting this record applies.
The taxonomy of cage types, the narrowing-versus-expanding framing, the
failure modes, and the diagnostics are this drafting pass's synthesis
([[src.synthesis.craft-library-editorial]]).

`source_confidence` is `limited`. Booker's taxonomy is a contested framework —
widely read, widely criticized as over-schematic — and is used here only for
the observation that the shape recurs, not as an authority on how it should
be executed. No dedicated craft treatment of captivity structure was
available to this drafting pass; this is a revision-pass target, and a later
pass should also check the prison-narrative literature, which exists and
would sharpen the boundary this record draws in its final exclusion.
