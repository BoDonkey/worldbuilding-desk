## What it is

A companion travels or works alongside the protagonist across a sustained
stretch of story without occupying one of the more specifically defined
supporting roles this library covers elsewhere — not a mentor (who
teaches), not a rival (who competes), not a romantic interest (whose
relationship carries its own distinct arc), not necessarily part of a
found-family structure (which implies a fuller ensemble). A companion's
defining feature is sustained proximity without a sharply defined
function, which makes the role simultaneously easy to include and easy to
leave underdeveloped — this library's general supporting-cast-purpose
pattern applies to the companion role with particular force, since
"someone who's around" is the least automatically purposeful function a
character can have.

## Why readers may care

A well-developed companion gives a protagonist someone to talk to, react
against, and be witnessed by across the story's full length, which can
carry significant narrative weight even without a sharply defined
function — companionship itself, sustained over time, is a real and
valued relationship readers respond to. The risk is specific to this
role's lack of built-in definition: a companion with no independent want,
perspective, or arc can become furniture, present in every scene but
contributing nothing a reader would miss if they were replaced by a
different character entirely.

## Common forms and variants

- **Contrast companion**, whose personality or worldview differs
  meaningfully from the protagonist's, generating low-grade ongoing
  friction or complementary balance.
- **Similar companion**, sharing the protagonist's outlook or background,
  offering solidarity and shared perspective rather than contrast — a
  different but legitimate register.
- **Skill-complementary companion**, whose distinct practical capabilities
  fill a gap the protagonist has, giving the role a functional as well as
  relational purpose.
- **Witness companion**, whose primary narrative function is to observe and
  react to the protagonist, sometimes serving as an audience surrogate —
  effective when the reactions themselves reveal character, weaker when
  they only exist to prompt exposition.
- **Independent-arc companion**, who has their own developing want and
  storyline running alongside the protagonist's, distinct from a companion
  whose entire arc is defined by proximity to the protagonist.

## What it can look like on the page

- A companion whose specific perspective, skill, or want shapes a scene's
  outcome independent of the protagonist's own choices.
- A companion's absence from a scene creating a specific, nameable gap —
  information, skill, or perspective only they would have provided.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a companion present in scene after scene
  who could be silently removed with no effect on plot, dialogue, or the
  protagonist's decisions.

## Common failure modes

- **The furniture companion**, present without independent want, skill, or
  perspective, functioning as company rather than character.
- **The exposition-only companion**, whose primary function is prompting
  the protagonist (or being prompted) to explain things to the reader, with
  no other narrative purpose.
- **An independent arc introduced late and never developed**, where a
  companion is given a hint of their own story that never actually
  receives page time or resolution.

## Questions for the author

- What does this companion want, independent of their proximity to the
  protagonist?
- If this companion were removed from a chapter range, what specifically
  would be lost — information, skill, perspective, or nothing?
- Does the companion's presence in scenes serve a function beyond company,
  or could their dialogue be reassigned to any other character with no
  loss?
- If the companion has an independent arc, does it receive dedicated page
  time, or does it remain a gestured-at but undeveloped thread?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write one sentence describing the companion's want that has nothing to
   do with the protagonist, and find a scene where it surfaces.
2. Test removing the companion from a chapter range; if nothing changes,
   identify what specific function (skill, perspective, information) could
   be added to make their presence necessary.
3. For an exposition-only companion, replace at least one instance of
   prompted exposition with a moment revealing the companion's own
   perspective or stake in what's happening.
4. If an independent arc is gestured at but undeveloped, either commit
   page time to it or scale back the gesture to match what the story
   actually delivers.

## When this advice does not apply

- Very minor, single-scene companions don't need full independent
  development.
- A story deliberately depicting a protagonist's isolation, where a
  companion's flatness reflects the protagonist's own failure to really
  see the people around them, may use an underdeveloped companion as
  characterization of the protagonist — provided that's a legible,
  examined choice.

## When straightforward execution is the right choice

A loyal, consistent companion whose primary narrative function really is
steady presence and support — without a fully independent arc — remains a
satisfying and reliable structure in many registers, particularly
lighter or more episodic ones, as long as they have at least one
distinguishing want or perspective that keeps them from reading as
interchangeable.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a companion has independent narrative function, or exists purely
as proximity, requires reading and interpreting characterization across
the manuscript — a judgment no structured data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Furniture companion:* A character travels alongside the protagonist for
twenty chapters, speaking only to ask questions that let the protagonist
explain the plot, with no stated want, skill, or reaction that isn't
directly prompted by the protagonist's own concerns.

*Purposeful companion:* The same character instead has a specific,
established skill (reading terrain) that solves a problem the protagonist
couldn't have solved alone, and a private worry about a family member back
home that surfaces in a quiet scene, unprompted by the protagonist — the
companion exists as a person, not a presence.
