## What it is

A working LitRPG author, writing candidly for peers about his own
observations of the genre, ranked five conventions as the ones he sees
most often drawing reader fatigue: an overpowered main character who never
struggles meaningfully, a harem or large ensemble romantic cast, a secret
lost class or item no one else in the setting has heard of in centuries, an
exposition-dispensing comic-relief companion, and a supporting cast of
uniformly attractive women. This is one author's personal, ranked opinion
about his own reading experience — not a survey, not a study, and not
this library's judgment about any of these conventions' inherent worth.
Every item on this list is also, demonstrably, commercially successful:
these conventions appear constantly in the genre's bestselling work.
Holding "readers report fatigue with this" and "this sells extremely
well" as simultaneously, unresolvedly true is this record's actual
position — a coach that tells an author not to write what sells is wrong;
a coach that pretends there's no fatigue risk at all is useless. The goal
here is descriptive: naming what each convention promises, what produces
fatigue when it's handled unexamined, and what freshens it when an author
wants to use it deliberately.

## Why readers may care

Each convention below is popular for a specific, real reason — readers
seek out and enjoy all five, and this genre's bestselling work proves it.
Fatigue, where it's reported, comes from a specific mechanism particular
to each convention (named per item below), not from the convention's mere
presence. A coach that only names "this is popular" or only names "this
tires some readers" gives an author half the picture; both facts matter to
a deliberate choice.

## What it can look like on the page

Common forms and variants for each of the five conventions, and the
specific mechanism that produces reader fatigue when the convention is
handled without individual development, are addressed together below,
convention by convention, rather than as separate generic sections — the
five items are different enough from each other that a single shared
"forms and variants" list would blur distinctions worth keeping separate.

## The overpowered protagonist with no real struggle

**What it promises:** an uncomplicated power fantasy — competence without
anxiety, a reader who gets to relax into watching the protagonist win.
**What produces fatigue:** when "no real struggle" extends to no stakes at
all, removing the tension this library's stakes and agency patterns
depend on; readers describe this specifically as boredom, not offense.
**What freshens it:** relocating the story's actual tension to a different
axis — social, moral, relational — so the character's overwhelming combat
or magical competence doesn't have to be the source of drama the plot
needs (see this library's negative-space-problems-power-cannot-solve
pattern).

## Harems and large ensemble romantic casts

**What it promises:** abundance, and for some readers, a lower-pressure
alternative to a single, exclusive romantic arc's usual obstacles. **What
produces fatigue:** when the ensemble functions as interchangeable
romantic options with underdeveloped individual identity, undercutting the
character-development stakes this library's supporting-cast-purpose and
romantic-subplot-conventions patterns describe. This is a structural
critique about characterization depth, not a judgment about the
relationship model itself — a large ensemble cast where each member has a
distinct, developed want and relationship to the protagonist doesn't
generate the same fatigue as one where they don't. **What freshens it:**
applying this library's supporting-cast-purpose pattern rigorously to each
member of the ensemble, so the cast's size doesn't come at the cost of
individual development.

## The secret lost class or item no one has heard of in centuries

**What it promises:** specialness and destiny, tying into the chosen-one
trope's core appeal. **What produces fatigue:** when the secrecy and
rarity are asserted rather than earned, and when "no one has heard of it
in centuries" starts to feel like a convenient absence of competition
rather than a meaningful worldbuilding fact — closely related to this
library's chosen-one and competent-others patterns. **What freshens it:**
giving the secrecy an in-world cause (the knowledge was deliberately
suppressed, lost in a specific catastrophe, or requires an unusual
circumstance to rediscover) rather than leaving it as an unexplained
absence, and letting the discovery cost something.

## The exposition-dispensing comic-relief companion

**What it promises:** levity and accessible information delivery. **What
produces fatigue:** when the character has no function beyond those two
roles, exactly the failure this library's companion and mentor records
describe as "the exposition dispenser" — present to explain things and
lighten the mood, with no independent want or arc. **What freshens it:**
giving the character a want unrelated to their functional role, per this
library's supporting-cast-purpose pattern, so their comic timing and
explanatory usefulness sit on top of an actual person rather than
substituting for one.

## A supporting cast of uniformly attractive women

**What it promises:** a specific aesthetic register some readers
explicitly enjoy. **What produces fatigue:** when physical uniformity
substitutes for the differentiated characterization this library's voice
and supporting-cast-purpose patterns describe — readers report fatigue
specifically when a cast reads as interchangeable, and physical uniformity
is one visible symptom of that deeper interchangeability, not
necessarily its cause. This record makes no claim about what any
individual reader finds appealing in a cast's appearance; the craft
concern is specifically whether appearance-level similarity correlates
with — or substitutes for — deeper character-level similarity. **What
freshens it:** the same fix as the harem item above — distinct wants,
voices, and relationships to the protagonist, independent of any
description of appearance.

## Questions for the author

- For any of these five conventions present in this manuscript, does the
  fatigue-producing mechanism (no real stakes, interchangeable cast
  members, unearned secrecy, function without personhood) actually apply
  here, or has the convention already been handled with the kind of
  individual development that avoids it?
- Is this convention being used because it serves this specific story, or
  because it's expected of the genre?
- If a convention here is core to what this book is trying to be, would a
  reader who's fatigued by the convention in its unexamined form still
  find something distinct in this particular execution?

## Revision or design experiments

1. For any convention from this cluster present in the manuscript, apply
   this library's relevant cross-referenced pattern (supporting-cast-
   purpose, negative-space, chosen-one, companion) directly and check
   whether the specific fatigue mechanism named above is actually present.
2. If a large ensemble cast is present, write one distinct want for each
   member independent of their relationship to the protagonist.
3. If a secret lost class or item is present, add an in-world reason for
   its obscurity rather than leaving the rarity unexplained.

## When this advice does not apply

- Every convention in this cluster has a straightforward, well-executed
  form that doesn't require subversion — this record's purpose is naming
  the fatigue mechanism precisely enough that an author can choose
  deliberately, not steering authors away from commercially proven,
  reader-loved conventions.
- A story deliberately and knowingly leaning into one of these conventions
  for its established audience, with full awareness of both its appeal
  and its fatigue risk, is making an informed choice this record supports
  rather than overrides.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a specific instance of any of these conventions triggers the
fatigue mechanism described, or avoids it through individual
characterization, requires reading and interpreting the manuscript
directly — a judgment no structured data substitutes for, and one this
library is careful not to present as a verdict on the convention's
inherent worth.
