## What it is

A revenge plot organizes a protagonist's central drive around retribution
for a specific wrong done to them or someone they care about. Structurally,
revenge plots often double as want/need stories: the want (retribution) is
usually clear and trackable, while the need (what would actually resolve the
underlying grief, anger, or loss) frequently diverges from it — the
strongest revenge stories dramatize that gap rather than treating
successful retribution as automatically satisfying.

## Why readers may care

Revenge offers clean, legible motivation and stakes — a reader understands
immediately why the protagonist is pursuing their goal, and can track
progress toward it concretely. The trope's deeper interest, though, usually
lies in what pursuing revenge costs the protagonist along the way:
relationships strained or lost, moral lines crossed, an identity reshaped
around the pursuit. A revenge story that shows no cost tends to read as
simple wish fulfillment; one that dramatizes the cost engages the reader in
a real question about whether the pursuit is worth it.

## Common forms and variants

- **Cost-free revenge**, where the protagonist pursues and achieves
  retribution with minimal personal cost — a legitimate, satisfying
  register for straightforward power-fantasy or pulp adventure, but a
  different (thinner) promise than cost-examined revenge.
- **Costly revenge**, where the pursuit exacts a real, dramatized price —
  relationships, morality, identity — raising the story's stakes beyond the
  external plot.
- **Hollow-victory revenge**, where achieving retribution doesn't provide
  the expected relief, directly dramatizing the want/need gap.
- **Abandoned revenge**, where the protagonist chooses to give up the
  pursuit before completing it, usually because something else (a
  relationship, a realization) has become more important — effective when
  the choice is dramatized as a real cost, not a convenient off-ramp.
- **Revenge redirected into justice**, where personal retribution evolves
  into a broader, less personally consuming pursuit of accountability —
  a common and often more mature evolution of the trope.

## What it can look like on the page

- A specific, concrete cost the pursuit of revenge exacts from the
  protagonist, dramatized rather than only referenced.
- A moment where achieving retribution (or the chance to achieve it) forces
  the protagonist to confront whether it's actually what they wanted.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a revenge plot resolved with the
  protagonist fully satisfied and entirely unchanged, with no cost
  attached to the pursuit anywhere in the story.

## Common failure modes

- **Consequence-free revenge**, where the pursuit costs the protagonist
  nothing, undermining any deeper engagement the trope could offer beyond
  simple wish fulfillment.
- **An unexamined hollow victory**, where retribution's failure to satisfy
  is stated but not actually dramatized or explored.
- **Revenge motivation that never develops**, remaining exactly as simple
  and singular at the story's end as at its start, with no complication or
  deepening across the manuscript.

## Questions for the author

- What has pursuing revenge cost this protagonist by the story's end?
- If retribution is achieved, does the story show whether it actually
  satisfies the underlying want, or does it move past the question quickly?
- Has the protagonist's relationship to their own revenge motivation
  changed or deepened at any point across the story?
- Is a cost-free, purely satisfying revenge the deliberate tonal register
  this story is going for, or does the manuscript's tone suggest it wants
  more complexity than it's currently delivering?

## Revision or design experiments

1. List what the pursuit of revenge has cost the protagonist by the
   manuscript's midpoint and end; if the list is empty, consider adding a
   specific, dramatized cost.
2. For a completed revenge, add or strengthen a scene examining whether the
   retribution actually satisfies the protagonist, rather than assuming it
   does.
3. Trace the protagonist's stated reason for revenge from the story's
   opening to its climax and check whether it deepens or complicates at
   any point.
4. If abandoning the pursuit is part of the arc, make sure the choice
   carries a real, dramatized cost rather than functioning as a convenient
   resolution.

## When this advice does not apply

- Pulp, action-adventure, or power-fantasy registers may deliberately keep
  revenge cost-free as part of their intended, uncomplicated satisfaction —
  a legitimate choice distinct from an unexamined default.
- A very brief revenge subplot within a larger story may not need the full
  cost-and-reckoning treatment this pattern describes.

## When straightforward execution is the right choice

A classical revenge plot resolved with clean, satisfying retribution and
minimal cost is a reliable and legitimate structure for stories where
uncomplicated catharsis is the actual point — not every revenge story needs
to interrogate whether revenge is "worth it" to succeed on its own terms.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a revenge plot's costs are dramatized, and whether the story
examines the want/need gap between retribution and satisfaction, requires
reading and interpreting the manuscript's characterization and plot — a
judgment no structured data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Cost-free revenge:* A protagonist tracks down and defeats the person
responsible for their family's death, and the story ends on their
satisfaction, with no relationship strained, no line crossed, and no
question raised about whether the pursuit changed them.

*Costly revenge:* The same pursuit instead costs the protagonist a
long-standing friendship with someone who begged them to let the matter
go, and upon finally confronting their target, the protagonist finds the
victory hollow — grief remains exactly as present as before, and the
friendship is not easily repaired.
