## What it is

A heist structures a story around a planned theft, infiltration, or
con, typically involving a team with specialized roles, a stated plan, and a
high-stakes target protected by obstacles the plan must account for. The
trope's characteristic pleasure comes from the gap between what the audience
is shown of the plan and what actually happens during execution — a gap
that can run in either direction: the plan working exactly as stated
(satisfying competence) or deviating in ways later revealed to have been
anticipated (satisfying misdirection).

## Why readers may care

A well-executed heist rewards attention: readers who track the stated plan
closely and notice where execution diverges get a specific pleasure a less
attentive read wouldn't. This makes the heist one of the trope forms most
dependent on fair-play information control — the reader needs enough
information to follow the plan, and any later reveal about what was
withheld needs to hold up against what was actually shown, not depend on
information the reader had no access to.

## Common forms and variants

- **Plan-then-execution structure**, where the plan is shown to the reader
  in advance, and execution either follows it or deviates in ways the
  reader can track in real time.
- **Withheld-plan structure**, where the plan is deliberately hidden from
  the reader until execution reveals it retroactively — riskier, since the
  later reveal must survive a fair-play check.
- **Team-based heist**, built around specialized roles (the planner, the
  infiltrator, the technical specialist), where each character's specialty
  creates a distinct obstacle and solution.
- **Solo heist**, compressing the team dynamic into a single character's
  resourcefulness, shifting the pleasure toward improvisation rather than
  team coordination.
- **The heist gone wrong**, where the plan fails and the story becomes about
  improvised recovery rather than execution of a stated plan — a legitimate
  and common variant, provided the improvisation still follows the story's
  own established rules.

## What it can look like on the page

- A stated plan followed by execution that either matches it closely or
  diverges in ways the reader can trace back to something established
  earlier.
- Team members whose specific skills directly solve a specific obstacle,
  making the team's composition meaningful rather than decorative.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a "twist" resolution that depends on a
  capability, resource, or piece of information never established before
  the moment it's needed.

## Common failure modes

- **The unfair twist**, resolving a heist's complication using information
  or a capability the reader had no way to know about, breaking the
  fair-play contract this trope depends on more than most.
- **Decorative team roles**, where a team member's stated specialty never
  actually matters to solving an obstacle.
- **A plan with no real obstacle**, where the target's defenses are
  underdeveloped enough that the heist never seriously risks failure.

## Questions for the author

- If a reader traced backward from the heist's resolution, could they find
  the earlier setup that made it possible, or does the resolution depend on
  something introduced only at the moment it's needed?
- Does each team member's stated specialty actually solve a specific
  obstacle in the story, or could their role be swapped with no effect?
- Is the target's defense substantial enough that the heist carries genuine
  risk of failure?
- If the plan goes wrong, does the improvised recovery still follow the
  story's own established rules, or does it introduce new capabilities with
  no setup?

## Revision or design experiments

1. For the heist's key resolution or twist, trace backward to find its
   setup; if none exists, plant one earlier in the manuscript.
2. Audit each team member's stated specialty against the actual obstacles
   they solve; assign a specific obstacle to any specialty that's currently
   decorative.
3. Strengthen the target's established defenses if the heist doesn't
   currently carry real risk of failure.
4. For a heist-gone-wrong structure, check that the improvised solution
   uses only capabilities and information already established for the
   characters involved.

## When this advice does not apply

- A comedic or deliberately absurd heist may break fair-play conventions for
  effect, where the joke is the implausibility itself rather than a
  fair-play violation.
- Very short-form heists (a single scene within a larger story) may
  compress or omit the full plan-and-execution structure without losing
  effectiveness.

## When straightforward execution is the right choice

A classical plan-then-execution heist, played straight with a well-matched
plan and outcome, remains a satisfying and reliable structure — the pleasure
of competence executed as stated doesn't require a twist to work, provided
the obstacles were genuinely difficult enough to make the competence worth
watching.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a heist's resolution is fairly set up, and whether team members'
specialties meaningfully solve specific obstacles, requires reading and
interpreting the manuscript's causal structure — a judgment no structured
data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Unfair twist:* A heist's resolution depends on a hidden passage the team
discovers only in the final scene, never hinted at or established
beforehand.

*Fair setup:* The same passage is instead glimpsed on an earlier
reconnaissance scene, dismissed at the time as a maintenance shaft too small
to use — its actual use in the climax recontextualizes that earlier,
seemingly minor detail.
