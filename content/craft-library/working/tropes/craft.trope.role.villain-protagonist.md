## What it is

A villain protagonist is a viewpoint character whose aims the story does not
present as good, and does not expect the reader to endorse. This is a
stronger position than the antihero, which this library treats separately:
an antihero is unadmirable in method while broadly aligned with something the
reader wants, whereas a villain protagonist may want what the reader hopes
they do not get.

The distinction that matters in practice is between *sympathy* and
*investment*. A reader does not need to approve of a character to want to
know what they do next, but the story has to supply something in place of
approval — competence, coherence, honesty about what the character is, or
the specific pleasure of watching a plan work.

## Why readers may care

Approval is only one of several things that holds a reader. Craft writing on
character tends to identify others: a character who wants something intensely
is interesting nearly regardless of what it is; a character who is good at
something is compelling to watch; a character whose logic is internally
consistent invites the reader to follow it even while disagreeing.

There is also a specific pleasure in being allowed inside a mind the reader
would ordinarily be protected from, and in a story that does not pretend to
be shocked by its own protagonist. Progression fiction has its own version
of this — the dungeon core, the monster protagonist, the ruthless optimizer —
where the appeal often lies in a frank amorality the genre otherwise avoids.

## Common forms and variants

- **The competent villain** — held by skill and planning; the reader watches
  a difficult thing done well.
- **The sympathetic-cause villain** — wrong methods, comprehensible aim,
  where the reader's discomfort is the design.
- **The unrepentant villain** — no arc toward redemption offered, which is
  the hardest version and the most honest about what it is.
- **The monstrous viewpoint** — a non-human protagonist whose values are
  genuinely alien rather than merely wicked.
- **The villain in decline** — a character whose logic is coherent early and
  visibly deteriorates, which supplies an arc without a redemption.

## What it can look like on the page

- A protagonist whose plans work, narrated with the same satisfaction a
  heroic story gives to a rescue.
- Victims who are specific people rather than obstacles — a choice that
  makes the book harder and usually better.
- A narration that does not editorialize, letting the reader supply the
  judgment the character will not.
- Conversely: a villain protagonist who is repeatedly excused by
  circumstance, which usually indicates a story that wants the aesthetics of
  villainy without its cost.

## Common failure modes

- **The softened villain.** Cruelty established and then continuously
  mitigated, so the reader is asked to enjoy the transgression and forgive it
  simultaneously.
- **Victims as furniture.** Harm done to unnamed, uncharacterized people,
  which makes the transgression weightless and the story less interesting
  rather than more comfortable.
- **Incoherent values.** A character whose ruthlessness is inconsistent,
  applied when the plot needs menace and abandoned when it needs sympathy.
- **Nothing to watch.** A protagonist who is unpleasant without being
  competent, interesting, or coherent — cruelty as characterization.
- **The unearned redemption.** A late turn toward goodness that the book has
  not paid for, which readers often experience as the story losing its nerve.

## When straightforward execution is the right choice

This convention needs no inversion. A story about a genuinely villainous
person, executed plainly and without apology, is a legitimate and durable
form — it does not require an ironic frame, a redemptive arc, or a
sympathetic backstory to justify itself, and adding those defensively is one
of the more common ways the form is weakened.

The one thing straightforward execution does require is that the story mean
it. A villain protagonist whose villainy is real, whose harm lands on people
the book has bothered to make real, and who is not quietly excused, gives the
reader a clear contract. It is the half-committed version — transgression
with the costs edited out — that tends to disappoint.

## Questions for the author

- What holds a reader here in place of sympathy, and is it on the page in
  chapter one?
- Does the story ever pretend to disapprove of something it is inviting the
  reader to enjoy?
- Are the people this character harms specific enough that the harm registers?
- Is this character's logic consistent when it is inconvenient for the plot?
- Does the book intend a redemption, and has it been paid for — or is it
  arriving because the ending felt uncomfortable?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Name what the reader is being offered besides approval — competence,
   insight, spectacle, honesty. If it is hard to name, that is the work.
2. Give one victim a page of their own before the harm, and see what the
   scene costs afterward.
3. Remove the story's most recent excuse for the protagonist and read the
   chapter again. If it becomes unreadable, the excuses may be carrying the
   book.
4. Write the protagonist's values as three rules, then find the scene where
   the plot asked them to break one for convenience.

## When this advice does not apply

- Comedy and satire, where a monstrous protagonist's harm is deliberately
  weightless and making victims real would break the register.
- Stories where the protagonist is villainous by circumstance rather than
  disposition, which are usually antihero or redemption structures and are
  treated in the related record.
- Non-human viewpoints whose alien values are the subject rather than a
  moral position — the dungeon-core profile covers the genre's common case.
- Any story whose intent is to make the reader complicit rather than
  comfortable, where several of the failure modes above may be deliberate
  effects.

## Evidence and detection limits

Very little of this is computable. A coach can observe whether harmed
characters are named and given dialogue, whether the narration applies
evaluative language to the protagonist's acts, and whether a redemptive turn
occurs late relative to the book's length. None of these establishes the
record's central question.

Whether a reader has been given something in place of sympathy is a semantic
judgment and a reader-dependent one — the same book can be gripping or
repellent to two readers with no disagreement about its content. A coach
should describe what the text does and avoid concluding that a protagonist
is unsympathetic, which is a response rather than a property.

## Original micro-examples

*Softened:* he had no choice; they would have killed him first; the guard was
cruel to prisoners anyway. Three excuses in one paragraph, and the reader is
being asked not to notice.

*Committed:* the guard had a daughter at the barracks school and talked about
her while he worked. Kel let him finish the sentence before he did it,
because interrupting would have been rude.

## Sources and confidence notes

Orson Scott Card's treatment of character and viewpoint
([[src.book.card-characters-and-viewpoint]]) supplies the underlying
distinction between reader sympathy and reader interest, and the argument
that attachment can be built on curiosity and competence rather than
approval; Janet Burroway ([[src.book.burroway-writing-fiction]]) supplies the
general principles about characterization and narrative distance this record
relies on. This library's genre research
([[src.internal.litrpg-genre-research]]) supplies the observation about
monstrous and amoral protagonists in dungeon-core and dark subgenres.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the handoff's second sense: the
character-craft principles are supported by established sources, while the
application to villain protagonists specifically — the failure taxonomy, the
"softened villain" pattern, the argument about specific victims — is this
drafting pass's synthesis. Neither craft source addresses villain
protagonists directly. No claim is made about how readers in aggregate
respond to morally repellent viewpoints; that varies widely and no study is
cited.
