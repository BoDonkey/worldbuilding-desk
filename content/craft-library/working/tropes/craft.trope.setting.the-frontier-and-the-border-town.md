## What it is

A frontier settlement sits at the boundary of what the story's civilization
controls: the last town before the wilds, the camp at the dungeon mouth, the
outpost on the border, the shantytown that grew around a newly opened gate.
Its defining features are proximity to danger, thin or improvised authority,
and a population that arrived for reasons.

The convention is old and travels across genres. In progression fiction it
usually attaches to a resource — a dungeon, a rift, a hunting ground — which
gives it an economic engine as well as a geographic one.

## Why readers may care

A frontier answers the question of why these particular people are in the
same place, which is otherwise one of the more awkward things a setting has
to arrange. Everyone here came for something, and that motive is a
characterization shortcut for an entire population.

It also supplies conflict without villains. Thin law means disputes must be
settled some other way; proximity to danger means ordinary decisions carry
risk; a mixed population means incompatible customs meeting daily. A frontier
town generates scenes the way a hub provides rest.

## Common forms and variants

- **The resource frontier** — a settlement grown around something valuable
  and dangerous, with a boom-town economy and everyone's arrival dated by it.
- **The military border** — a garrison town where authority is present but
  martial, and civilians live inside someone else's priorities.
- **The receding frontier** — a settlement that used to be the edge and no
  longer is, which produces decline, nostalgia, and people who stayed.
- **The advancing frontier** — a place being pushed outward, with all the
  displacement that implies for whoever was already there.
- **The permanent siege** — a settlement that cannot expand and cannot leave,
  where the boundary is fixed and the pressure constant.

## What it can look like on the page

- Prices that reflect distance from supply, and characters who notice them.
- A dispute settled by a local arrangement rather than by law, and everyone
  understanding the arrangement.
- People who are obviously not from here, and people who obviously have
  nowhere else to go.
- Conversely: a frontier town with a functioning constabulary, stable prices,
  and no evidence of danger except when the plot needs some.

## Common failure modes

- **The safe frontier.** All the aesthetics of danger with none of its
  effects on daily life.
- **Lawlessness as licence.** Thin authority used only to justify violence,
  never to produce the improvised institutions that actually arise in its
  absence.
- **The empty backdrop.** A town with an inn, a smith, and a quest-giver,
  which readers recognize as a menu rather than a place.
- **Nobody's home.** A frontier defined entirely by transients, with no one
  whose life is here, which removes the stakes of the place itself.
- **The unexamined displacement.** A settlement expanding into somewhere, with
  no acknowledgment that somewhere was occupied — a real historical dynamic
  the convention inherits and often leaves as scenery.

## When straightforward execution is the right choice

The plain frontier town works and needs no reinvention. A dusty, dangerous,
mixed settlement where the protagonist can arrive as a stranger and be
plausibly accepted is doing several structural jobs at once, and readers
recognize it immediately, which is a feature — the recognition means the
author can spend their attention on what is specific here rather than on
establishing the type.

Straightforward execution asks only that the danger be real in ordinary life,
not just during plot events. A town where the price of iron reflects the
road's risk, where people have opinions about the militia, and where somebody
has a scheme for getting rich is a plain frontier town executed well, and it
will carry a hundred chapters.

## Questions for the author

- Why did each significant character come here, and what would make them
  leave?
- Who settles disputes in this town, and by what authority?
- What does danger do to ordinary life — prices, hours, marriage, insurance,
  where children are allowed to go?
- Who was here before this settlement, and what does the story say about
  that?
- If the danger ended tomorrow, what would happen to this place?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write the price of three ordinary goods and why. Frontier economics
   usually generate more texture per sentence than frontier description.
2. Give the town one improvised institution — a bounty board, a widows'
   fund, a rule about the gate at nightfall — and let a scene run through it.
3. Write one character whose family has been here three generations, and see
   how the transients look from that angle.
4. Draft the scene where the danger arrives during something ordinary rather
   than during a set piece.

## When this advice does not apply

- Stories where the frontier is passed through in a chapter and does not need
  its economy worked out.
- Settled, urban, or courtly settings where this convention simply is not in
  play.
- Deliberately mythic or fabular treatments where a border town is an image
  rather than a functioning place.
- Base-building premises, where the settlement is the subject and its profile
  record governs the detail; this record covers the frontier as a place
  others arrive at.

## Evidence and detection limits

A coach can note recurring locations and how many chapters they appear in,
and can observe whether prices, laws, or dangers are mentioned consistently
across appearances. Nothing here is strongly detectable.

Whether a frontier feels real is a semantic judgment about accumulated
detail, and its absence is not evidence of a fault — plenty of good books
keep their settlements thin on purpose. The displacement question in
particular is a matter for the author's intent and not something a coach
should raise as a defect; it is offered here as a question worth having
answered, not a standard to be enforced.

## Original micro-examples

*Safe frontier:* Ridgewatch was the last town before the Blight, and its
market was well stocked, its streets were patrolled, and its prices were
reasonable.

*Frontier with consequences:* Ridgewatch charged four times the capital's
price for lamp oil, because the road came through the Blight and one carter
in five did not. Everyone went to bed early and nobody called it fear.
