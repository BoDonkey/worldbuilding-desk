## What it is

Science fiction as a genre convention promises that its speculative
elements — a technology, an alien contact, a changed physical law — will be
extrapolated with some rigor rather than treated as arbitrary set dressing.
Readers expect the story to actually think through a premise's
implications: if faster-than-light travel exists, what does that do to
politics, economics, and warfare; if a technology can read minds, what
does that do to law, privacy, and relationships. This is the genre's
version of the internal-consistency contract fantasy readers hold stories
to, applied specifically to extrapolated implication rather than
established magical rules — the difference being that science fiction's
promise is less about never breaking a stated rule and more about actually
following a premise's logic where it leads, including into places the
author might not have originally intended to go.

## Why readers may care

A science fiction premise that's introduced but never followed through —
a technology that changes nothing about how society functions, an alien
contact that has no lasting effect on humanity's self-understanding —
reads as a missed opportunity specifically because science fiction readers
are primed to expect and value that follow-through as the genre's central
pleasure. This is distinct from fantasy's consistency expectation: a
science fiction story isn't just avoiding contradiction, it's actively
expected to explore consequence.

## Common forms and variants

- **Hard science fiction**, extrapolating with close attention to
  established physical science, holding itself to a high standard of
  technical plausibility.
- **Soft science fiction**, prioritizing social, psychological, or
  political extrapolation over strict technical rigor, still expected to
  follow its premises' implications even without hard-science grounding.
- **Space opera**, often treating some technological elements as
  convention rather than rigorously extrapolated, while still expected to
  follow through on its story's actual central premises (a galactic
  empire's politics, a specific technology's social effect).
- **Near-future or speculative fiction**, extrapolating from current
  trends or technologies, where readers may hold the story to an even
  higher standard of plausible consequence given the smaller conceptual
  distance from the present.
- **Science-fantasy hybrids**, blending SF's extrapolation-driven promise
  with fantasy's internal-consistency promise, requiring the story to
  honor both conventions where they apply.

## What it can look like on the page

- A central speculative element whose social, political, or personal
  implications are traced through multiple scenes rather than remaining
  static background.
- A technology or premise's introduction followed by the story actually
  grappling with unintended or complicated consequences, not just the
  intended, convenient ones.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a technology or premise established
  early with dramatic implication, that the story then treats as static
  set dressing for its remaining length, never engaging what it would
  actually change.

## Common failure modes

- **Premise as decoration**, introducing a speculative element for
  atmosphere or plot convenience without extrapolating its implications.
- **Consequence-free technology**, where a stated capability (mind-
  reading, instant travel, resurrection-adjacent tech) never actually
  reshapes the plot, society, or relationships around it.
- **Inconsistent extrapolation**, where a technology's implications are
  followed through in one scene and ignored in another with no
  explanation.

## Questions for the author

- What does this story's central speculative element actually change about
  society, politics, or daily life — and is that change shown, not just
  asserted?
- Is there an unintended or complicated consequence of the story's premise
  that hasn't been explored yet?
- Does the extrapolation stay consistent across the manuscript, or does
  the technology's implications get followed through in some scenes and
  ignored in others?

## Revision or design experiments

1. For the story's central speculative element, list three social,
   political, or personal consequences it would plausibly cause, and check
   how many are actually shown in the manuscript.
2. Find a convenient use of the premise and consider adding an
   inconvenient, complicating consequence nearby to balance it.
3. Audit the premise's application across the manuscript for consistency
   in how thoroughly its implications are followed.

## When this advice does not apply

- Space opera and other convention-forward SF subgenres may deliberately
  treat some background elements (interstellar travel, translator
  technology) as genre furniture without extrapolation, provided the
  story's actual core premise still receives real follow-through.
- Very brief or minor speculative elements, not central to the story,
  don't need the full extrapolation treatment this pattern describes.

## When straightforward execution is the right choice

A classical hard-SF or soft-SF structure that rigorously follows its
premise's implications to a well-reasoned conclusion is a reliable and
satisfying structure that doesn't need to subvert genre expectations —
delivering on the extrapolation promise cleanly is often exactly what
science fiction readers are there for.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a speculative element's implications are adequately extrapolated
requires reading and interpreting the manuscript's worldbuilding and plot
as a whole — a judgment no structured data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Premise as decoration:* A story establishes that a device can transfer
memories between people, uses it once for a plot-convenient reveal, and
never explores what such a technology would do to law, identity, grief, or
relationships more broadly.

*Followed-through premise:* The same device instead drives a subplot about
a black market for stolen memories, a legal case over memory-based
inheritance disputes, and a character's crisis about whether a memory they
now hold is genuinely "theirs" — the premise's implications ripple outward
rather than staying contained to a single plot beat.
