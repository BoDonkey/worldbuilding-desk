## What it is

Horror as a genre convention promises managed fear: dread that builds
through withheld or ambiguous information, releases through confrontation
or revelation, and ideally carries meaning beyond the fear itself — a
monster that represents something, a violation of safety that says
something about vulnerability, a survival ordeal that tests character.
Readers bring genre-specific expectations to horror distinct from a
general story's suspense: they expect the threat to feel genuinely
dangerous (not defeatable through simple competence), they expect some
ambiguity or unknowability to persist rather than being fully resolved
and explained, and they expect the story to be willing to let bad things
actually happen rather than rescuing every character from meaningful
harm.

## Why readers may care

A reader who opens a story signaled as horror and finds a threat that's
fully explained, easily defeated, and costs nothing real feels the genre's
central promise broken, even if the resulting story is well executed on
its own separate terms. Conversely, applying horror conventions
(sustained dread, ambiguous threat, real cost) to a story that hasn't
signaled itself as horror can feel like a tonal betrayal in the other
direction. The convention exists because it names a real, distinct reader
expectation, not because horror is inherently "about" any specific content
like gore or jump-scares.

## Common forms and variants

- **Psychological horror**, where dread comes primarily from uncertainty,
  perception, and internal experience rather than physical threat.
- **Visceral or body horror**, where dread comes from physical violation,
  transformation, or vulnerability made concrete and graphic.
- **Cosmic or unknowable horror**, where dread comes from confronting
  something beyond human understanding or control, often deliberately
  resisting full explanation.
- **Survival horror**, where the threat is escapable or defeatable in
  principle but genuinely, consistently dangerous, emphasizing resource
  management and vulnerability.
- **Horror-adjacent hybrids**, combining horror conventions with another
  genre's structure (horror-mystery, horror-LitRPG) — workable when the
  hybrid genre's other conventions don't undercut horror's core promise of
  genuine, sustained danger and some irreducible ambiguity.

## What it can look like on the page

- A threat that remains genuinely dangerous across the manuscript, with
  competence and preparation mitigating but not eliminating risk.
- Some element of the story's central threat or mystery that resists full,
  tidy explanation, preserving irreducible unease even after a climax.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a story signaled as horror whose central
  threat is fully explained, fully defeated, and imposes no lasting cost
  by the story's end.

## Common failure modes

- **Over-explained threats**, where a horror story's central menace is
  fully rationalized and demystified, removing the ambiguity that
  sustains dread.
- **A threat rendered toothless by competence**, where the protagonist's
  established capability makes the danger feel manageable rather than
  genuinely threatening, undercutting horror's promise.
- **Borrowed conventions that compete with horror's promise**, where a
  hybrid genre's own conventions (a LitRPG system's helpful, reassuring
  interface; a mystery's tidy, fully-resolved reveal) work against
  sustained dread and ambiguity rather than supporting them.

## Questions for the author

- Does this story's central threat remain genuinely dangerous throughout,
  or does the protagonist's growing competence make it feel manageable
  well before the climax?
- Is there some element of the story's central threat or mystery that
  resists full explanation, preserving unease even after resolution?
- If this story hybridizes horror with another genre's conventions, do
  those conventions support or compete with horror's core promise?
- Has the story signaled itself as horror clearly enough that a reader's
  genre expectations are correctly calibrated from the opening pages?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Audit the central threat's danger level across the manuscript; if it
   becomes fully manageable well before the climax, consider whether that
   serves the story's actual promise.
2. Identify one element of the central threat or mystery and test leaving
   it deliberately unresolved or ambiguous rather than fully explained.
3. For a horror-hybrid story, check whether the non-horror genre's
   conventions (a reassuring system voice, a fully tidy resolution)
   undercut sustained dread, and adjust if so.

## When this advice does not apply

- A story using horror imagery or aesthetics without pursuing the genre's
  core promise of managed, sustained dread (a comedic or ironic use of
  horror trappings) is using the convention differently, not failing to
  achieve it.
- Very brief horror interludes within a non-horror story don't need the
  sustained ambiguity and irreducible danger this pattern describes for a
  full-length treatment.

## When straightforward execution is the right choice

A classical horror structure — sustained dread, a genuinely dangerous and
not-fully-explained threat, real cost paid by the story's end — remains a
satisfying and reliable structure that doesn't require subversion to
deliver its core promise effectively.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a threat remains genuinely dangerous throughout, and whether
enough ambiguity is preserved to sustain dread, requires reading and
interpreting the manuscript's plot and pacing — a judgment no structured
data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Toothless threat:* A horror story's central menace is fully explained by
the midpoint and defeated through the protagonist's established combat
skill with no cost, leaving the remaining chapters without genuine danger.

*Sustained dread:* The same menace instead remains only partially
understood even after a costly confrontation, and the story's final
chapter includes a small, unresolved detail suggesting the threat — or
something like it — persists beyond the story's ending.
