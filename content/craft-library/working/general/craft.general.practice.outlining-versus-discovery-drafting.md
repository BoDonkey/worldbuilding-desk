## What it is

Some writers plan a book before drafting it; some find it by writing. Most
work somewhere between, and the useful question is not which camp an author
belongs to but what each approach costs them in their particular
circumstances.

This record is a `practice` record: it describes an author's working method,
not a property of a manuscript. Nothing here can be inspected in a draft, and
a coach should never infer from a text how it was planned.

## Why readers may care

Readers do not encounter method, but they encounter its consequences.
Discovery drafting tends to produce livelier scenes and structural problems
that surface late; outlining tends to produce sound structure and drafts that
can feel dutiful where the author was executing a plan rather than
discovering anything. Both are fixable, and they are fixed at different
stages, which is the practical point.

The serial case is different in kind rather than degree. A published chapter
cannot be revised backward. Discovery drafting in public means every
improvisation becomes canon, and a plot the author has not thought through
becomes a promise the audience is holding them to. That does not make it
wrong — a great deal of serial fiction is written this way — but the cost
lands differently than it does for a novelist with a drawer.

## Common forms and variants

- **Full outlining** — the book planned to scene level before drafting.
- **Signpost planning** — a handful of fixed points with the route between
  them left open; the most common working compromise.
- **Discovery drafting** — writing forward to find out what happens, with
  structure imposed in revision.
- **Iterative outlining** — outlining the next stretch only, repeatedly, so
  planning stays a chapter or two ahead of the draft.
- **Retrospective outlining** — drafting freely, then outlining what was
  actually written in order to see its shape.

## What it can look like in an author's process

- A draft that stalls at the same proportional point each time — often a sign
  that the method is failing at a specific structural moment rather than in
  general.
- Notes that accumulate faster than pages.
- A serial author writing themselves into a corner in public and having to
  build a plausible exit out of established material.
- Conversely: an outline followed so faithfully that discoveries made during
  drafting are discarded because they were not in the plan.

## Common failure modes

- **Outlining as avoidance.** Planning indefinitely because a plan cannot
  fail the way a draft can.
- **Discovery drafting a serial without a buffer.** The combination most
  likely to produce a corner with no exit — related to the buffer-discipline
  record.
- **Method loyalty.** Holding to an approach that is not working because it
  is the author's identity rather than their tool.
- **Confusing method with structure.** Believing a discovery draft cannot be
  structurally sound, or that an outline guarantees it. Neither is true.
- **Abandoning the outline silently.** Diverging from the plan without
  revisiting it, so the plan quietly stops describing the book.

## Questions for the author

- Where do drafts usually stall, and is it the same place each time?
- Which parts of the story are you willing to discover, and which need to be
  known in advance?
- If this is a serial, what have you already promised in public that you have
  not yet worked out?
- When the draft diverges from the plan, does the plan get updated?
- What does the current method cost you — in rewriting, in stalls, in
  chapters you would rather not have published?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Outline the next three chapters only, then draft them. Iterative planning
   suits many writers who dislike full outlines and find pure discovery
   costly.
2. Retrospectively outline what you have written. The shape of a discovery
   draft is often clearer to its author in summary than in prose.
3. For a serial, list every open promise made to readers, then check which
   have an answer. That list is the real exposure of drafting in public.
4. Try the other method for one chapter, deliberately, and note what it felt
   like rather than whether the chapter was better.

## When this advice does not apply

- Writers with a settled, working method and no symptoms. Process advice is
  for friction; changing an approach that works is a cost with no benefit.
- Collaborative or commissioned work, where planning is a contractual
  requirement rather than a preference.
- Short work, where the difference largely dissolves.
- Serials already deep in publication, where the practical question is not
  method but how to manage the promises already made.

## Evidence and detection limits

Nothing in this record is detectable from a manuscript, and that is the
point of the `practice` mark. A coach cannot tell an outlined draft from a
discovered one; structural problems occur in both, and their presence says
nothing about method.

The one legitimate use is conversational. If an author raises a process
problem — repeated stalls, a serial corner, planning that never ends — the
coach can offer these distinctions. It should never open the subject by
inferring from the text how the book was written, which would be a claim it
cannot support.

## Original micro-examples

*Serial exposure:* chapter 40 established that the Ash Rite cannot be
performed twice. Chapter 90's plan requires performing it twice. Both
chapters are published.

*Method as identity:* "I'm a discovery writer" — said by someone whose last
three drafts each stalled at the sixty-percent mark, which is a fact about
where their method breaks rather than about who they are.

## Sources and confidence notes

Stephen King ([[src.book.king-on-writing]]) supplies the well-known
practitioner case for discovery drafting and against outlining; James Scott
Bell ([[src.book.bell-super-structure]]) supplies the case for structural
planning; Janet Burroway ([[src.book.burroway-writing-fiction]]) supplies the
textbook's more neutral treatment of process variation. The disagreement
between the first two is real and is preserved here rather than resolved —
this record deliberately takes no side, because the sources do not settle it
and neither does the evidence.

`source_confidence` is `mixed`. The methods themselves are well documented as
practitioner positions rather than studied outcomes; no source cited here
demonstrates that either produces better books, and this record should not be
read as implying one does. The serial-publishing analysis — that public
drafting converts improvisation into binding canon — is this drafting pass's
synthesis from this library's genre research, and is the part of the record
most specific to the audience it is being written for.
