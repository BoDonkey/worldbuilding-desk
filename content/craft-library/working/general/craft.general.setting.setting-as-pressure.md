## What it is

Setting can operate at two very different levels of involvement. At one, it
is the place a story happens: rendered, atmospheric, and essentially
neutral toward events. At the other, it is a source of constraint — the
weather, the distance, the season, the law, the architecture, the customs
make some things impossible and others expensive, and the story's problems
are harder because of where they occur.

This record is about the second. It is distinct from the description and
specificity record, which is about how vividly a place is rendered on the
page. A setting can be beautifully described and exert no pressure at all,
and a setting can be sketched in a sentence and constrain every scene.

## Why readers may care

Pressure from setting makes a world feel real in a way description alone
does not. When a place costs the characters something — a journey that takes
weeks, a city where their accent is a liability, a winter that ends the
campaigning season — the reader infers that the world exists independently
of the plot's convenience. Conversely, a world that supplies whatever a
scene needs and withholds nothing tends to feel like a backdrop even when
it is richly painted.

Setting-as-pressure also generates plot cheaply. A constraint the author has
established can complicate a scene without any new character or coincidence,
which is often the difference between a story that escalates through events
and one that escalates through invention.

## Common forms and variants

- **Physical constraint** — distance, terrain, climate, season, scarcity.
  The oldest and most reliable form: things take time, and time costs.
- **Social constraint** — law, custom, class, reputation, obligation. What a
  character *may* do in this place, and what it costs them to do otherwise.
- **Institutional constraint** — the schedule, hierarchy, or rules of an
  enclosing structure: a school, a ship, an army, a hospital.
- **Setting as antagonist** — the place itself as the primary opposing
  force, common in survival and expedition stories.
- **Setting as relief** — a place whose function is to be safe, which works
  as pressure in reverse: it matters because leaving it is costly.

## What it can look like on the page

- A plan that has to change because of where it must happen.
- Travel that takes a stated, felt amount of time, and characters who make
  decisions around it.
- Characters behaving differently in different places — quieter in one city,
  franker in another — because the setting is exerting something on them.
- Conversely: a journey of unspecified duration; a season that never
  constrains anything; a city that is a name and a marketplace.

## Common failure modes

- **Scenery without cost.** Extensive description of a place that never
  makes anything harder, which readers often experience as a slowed pace
  without a clear reason.
- **Inconsistent constraint.** A distance that takes weeks when the plot
  needs delay and days when it needs speed, which teaches the reader not to
  believe the map.
- **Constraint stated but not felt.** The narration says the winter is
  brutal; no scene is shaped by it.
- **Setting as exposition delivery.** A place introduced primarily so
  someone can explain the world in it.
- **Over-constraint.** So many limits that the characters cannot act, and
  the story becomes a description of impossibility.

## Questions for the author

- What does this place make harder, and for whom?
- If this scene were moved somewhere else in the same world, what would
  actually change?
- What can the characters not do here that they could do elsewhere — and has
  the reader been told, or shown?
- Which constraints has the story established, and has it kept faith with
  them under plot pressure?
- Is there a place in this world the characters are trying to reach or
  avoid, and does the reader understand why?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Pick a scene and move it to a different location in the same world. If
   nothing about the scene changes, the setting is currently decorative
   there — which may be fine, or may be a missed source of difficulty.
2. List the three constraints this world imposes most often, and check
   whether any scene in the draft violates one for convenience.
3. Take one long description and convert half its detail into difficulty:
   the same features, but as things characters must work around.
4. Write the journey times, seasons, or laws down as a short reference, then
   read the draft against it. Inconsistencies usually cluster where the plot
   was in a hurry.

## When this advice does not apply

- Interior and psychological fiction where the setting is deliberately thin
  because the pressure is internal.
- Stories whose promise is comfort or wish-fulfilment, where a frictionless
  world is the point and adding constraint would break the contract.
- Allegorical, fabular, and dreamlike modes, where a place that behaves
  according to meaning rather than physics is the form.
- Scenes that deliberately provide relief. Not every scene should be made
  harder by where it happens.

## Evidence and detection limits

A coach can observe some of this: whether journey durations are stated
consistently, whether described features recur as obstacles, how much of a
chapter is description versus action, whether a stated constraint is ever
referenced again. Contradictions in stated distances or times are the
closest thing here to a deterministic check, and even those require reading
context to distinguish an error from a character's mistaken belief.

What cannot be determined is whether a setting *should* exert more pressure.
That depends on the book's intent, and a low-friction world may be exactly
right. A coach can say that a place has been described at length and has not
yet constrained anything, and ask whether that is intended. It cannot
conclude that the setting is failing.

## Original micro-examples

*Setting as scenery:* three paragraphs on the salt flats — the light, the
crust underfoot, the distant heat shimmer. The characters cross them in a
sentence and arrive unaffected.

*Setting as pressure:* the same salt flats, where water must be carried, the
crust will not hold a cart, and travelling by day is impossible. The
crossing now costs a decision about what to leave behind.

## Sources and confidence notes

Janet Burroway's textbook treatment of setting and atmosphere
([[src.book.burroway-writing-fiction]]) supplies the core distinction between
setting as backdrop and setting as an active element of conflict; John
Gardner ([[src.book.gardner-art-of-fiction]]) supplies the argument about
concrete detail and the vividness of the fictional world; Ursula K. Le Guin
([[src.book.leguin-steering-the-craft]]) supplies the treatment of description
as something to be integrated into action rather than delivered in blocks.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the handoff's second sense: the principle
that setting can be active rather than decorative is well supported by
multiple independent craft sources, while this record's specific framing —
setting as a constraint economy, the location-swap test, the taxonomy of
constraint types — is this drafting pass's synthesis. The related record on
systemic social consequences extends the same reasoning to power systems
specifically; readers of that record should treat this one as the general
case rather than a duplicate of it.
