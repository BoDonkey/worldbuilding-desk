## What it is

Most novels run more than one storyline. Braiding is the management of those
threads: what each one is for, how often it surfaces, how long it can be
absent before a reader loses hold of it, and whether it eventually connects
to the main line or simply runs alongside it.

This is distinct from complications, which are events that change the shape
of a problem inside one line, and from causal escalation, which is about how
events drive each other. Braiding is the level above both: the arrangement of
whole storylines against each other across a book.

## Why readers may care

Threads let a book breathe. A single line at constant pressure exhausts a
reader; cutting away to a second concern creates relief without stopping the
story, and returning to the first after an absence often makes it feel more
urgent than it did when the reader left. Threads also let a book say more
than one thing — a subplot can examine the same question the main plot is
asking, from a position the protagonist cannot occupy.

The cost is attention. Every thread a reader is asked to hold is a small
ongoing tax, and a thread that never repays it becomes something the reader
waits out.

## Common forms and variants

- **The mirroring subplot** — a secondary storyline that poses the main
  plot's question in a different key, so that the two comment on each other
  without either explaining the other.
- **The contrasting subplot** — a line that shows what the protagonist's
  choice would look like made differently, often through a character who
  chose otherwise.
- **The convergent thread** — a line that runs separately and then collides
  with the main plot, usually near the climax.
- **The standing thread** — an ongoing relationship, obligation, or
  condition that surfaces regularly without ever becoming the book's centre.
- **The runner** — a small recurring element, sometimes comic, that provides
  rhythm and familiarity more than plot.

## What it can look like on the page

- Chapters that alternate between threads at a rhythm the reader learns.
- A thread that is set up in the first quarter, disappears for two hundred
  pages, and returns needing reintroduction.
- Two lines that share a scene for the first time, and the scene's tension
  comes largely from that convergence.
- A subplot that ends quietly a third of the way in and is never referenced
  again — sometimes a dropped thread, sometimes a deliberate closure the
  reader reads as dropped.

## Common failure modes

- **The unconnected thread.** A well-written line that never touches
  anything else and could be lifted out cleanly, which readers often
  describe as "the parts I skipped."
- **Thread starvation.** A line surfacing so rarely that each return spends
  its space on reminding the reader who these people are.
- **Competition rather than braiding.** Two threads of equal weight and
  similar concern, so the book reads as two half-books.
- **The subplot that eats the book.** A secondary line more alive than the
  main one — worth noticing rather than fixing, since it sometimes indicates
  which book the author actually wants to write.
- **Convergence by coincidence.** Threads that meet because the plot needs
  them to rather than because anything in either made it likely.

## Questions for the author

- What is each thread for — what does the book get from it that it could
  not get otherwise?
- Which threads eventually touch, and which are content to run in parallel?
- What is the longest gap in the book for each thread, and does the reader
  need reminding when it returns?
- If a thread were removed entirely, what else in the book would have to
  change? If the answer is nothing, that is information.
- Does any thread ask the same question as the main line, and would the book
  be stronger if one of them asked a different one?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Chart the threads by chapter — a simple grid of which line each chapter
   advances. Gaps, clumps, and the shape of the convergence usually become
   visible immediately.
2. Write one sentence per thread saying what it is for. A thread whose
   sentence is only a description of its events, not its function, may not
   have one yet.
3. Try removing the weakest thread in outline and see what collapses. What
   collapses is the thread's real contribution; if nothing does, consider
   whether its material belongs to another line.
4. Move a convergence earlier and see what it costs. Threads often meet
   later than they need to because the author is saving them.

## When this advice does not apply

- Deliberately episodic or picaresque structures, where discrete,
  unconnected episodes are the form rather than a failure of it.
- Short works, where a single line is often the right amount of book.
- Serialized fiction with a long arc structure, where a thread may
  legitimately sleep for a hundred chapters because the reader is reading
  across a much longer span than a novel's.
- Stories whose subject is disconnection, where threads that never meet may
  be the point — provided the book gives the reader a reason to believe that.

## Evidence and detection limits

A coach can, given a chaptered manuscript, approximate which thread each
chapter advances by tracking which characters and concerns appear, and from
that produce gap lengths, distribution, and the location of convergences.
These are useful structural observations, and they are approximations: which
thread a chapter "belongs to" is an interpretive call, and chapters often
advance two.

What cannot be determined is whether a thread earns its place. That is a
judgment about what the book is for. A thread with long gaps and no
convergence is not thereby a failure — the coach should show the shape and
ask, rather than name a fault.

## Original micro-examples

*A mirroring subplot:* the protagonist refuses to give up a dangerous
inheritance; in the second thread, her cousin accepts an equivalent one and
is slowly consumed by it. Neither line explains the other, and the book's
question is sharper for having two answers in it.

*An unconnected thread:* eight chapters follow a cartographer mapping the
northern roads. The chapters are good. Nothing in the book requires the map,
and no character ever consults it.

## Sources and confidence notes

John Truby ([[src.book.truby-anatomy-of-story]]) supplies the argument that
subplots work best when they pose the main story's moral question
differently rather than merely adding incident; Donald Maass
([[src.book.maass-writing-breakout-novel]]) supplies the treatment of
multiple storylines as a source of tension and momentum; Janet Burroway
([[src.book.burroway-writing-fiction]]) supplies the general structural
vocabulary this record uses.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the handoff's second sense: the underlying
principle — that a subplot should do work the main line cannot — is supported
independently by two of the three sources, while the specific diagnostics
here (thread charting, gap length, the removal test, the taxonomy of forms)
are this drafting pass's synthesis. The serialized-fiction exception is drawn
from this library's own genre research rather than from any of the three
craft texts, none of which address serial publication.
