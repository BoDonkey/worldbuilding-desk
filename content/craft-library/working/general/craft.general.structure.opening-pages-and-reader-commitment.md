## What it is

An opening is the stretch of a story — often a page, sometimes a chapter —
during which a reader is deciding whether to continue, and is doing so
without any accumulated investment to draw on. Everything the story will
later be able to rely on (attachment to a character, curiosity about a
question, trust that setups will pay off) does not exist yet. The opening's
job is to create enough of it to buy the next few pages, and then the next.

This record covers that stretch and the pressure it operates under. It is
distinct from the promise and genre-signaling patterns treated separately
here: those concern what a story commits to across a whole book. An opening
is where those commitments are first made — quickly, and usually
implicitly.

## Why readers may care

A reader arriving at page one is deciding, mostly below conscious thought,
what kind of experience is on offer and whether they want it. Craft
discussion converges on a few things a reader seems to want early: someone
to attach to, an indication of what trouble is in play, and a reason to
believe the writing will repay attention. A reader given none of these
rarely articulates a complaint — they simply stop.

Serialized publishing sharpens this considerably. A reader browsing a
platform's list is comparing openings, not books, and a story whose appeal
begins in chapter four is competing against stories whose appeal begins in
paragraph one. That is a market fact rather than a craft law, but it changes
what an opening has to do.

## Common forms and variants

- **Opening in motion** — beginning during an event already underway, so
  that orientation arrives as the reader watches rather than before.
- **Opening on a character in ordinary difficulty** — establishing who this
  is and what their life costs them before the story disrupts it.
- **Opening on a question** — a situation whose explanation is withheld,
  buying attention against the promise of an answer.
- **Opening on voice** — carrying the first pages primarily on the
  distinctiveness of the narration, most viable where voice is the book's
  central pleasure.

Edgerton's craft treatment of openings distinguishes the *surface problem*
a scene presents from the deeper *story-worthy problem* the book is actually
about, and argues that an opening works better when the first gestures
toward the second rather than standing alone.

## What it can look like on the page

- A first paragraph establishing a specific person in a specific situation,
  rather than a landscape, a history, or the weather.
- Orientation delivered inside action — who, where, and what is at stake
  emerging from what characters do and say rather than from a preamble.
- A first chapter that ends on a reason to continue rather than on a
  resolution.
- Conversely: several pages of world history, a dream sequence revealed as a
  dream, or a character waking up and describing themselves — all common
  enough that many readers recognize them as openings that have not yet
  started.

None of these signals proves anything on its own. A prologue of world
history is a real choice with precedent; it simply spends reader patience
early, and should be made knowingly.

## Common failure modes

- **Front-loading orientation.** Explaining the world so the reader will
  understand chapter three, at the cost of chapter one being interesting.
- **Opening before the story starts.** Beginning at the last ordinary
  moment rather than the first consequential one, so the reader waits
  through a preamble for the book they were promised.
- **A hook with nothing behind it.** An arresting first line that connects
  to nothing, which buys attention and then spends the credit immediately.
- **Withholding to create false mystery.** Concealing information the POV
  character plainly knows, which reads as evasion rather than suspense.
- **Signalling the wrong book.** An opening whose tone promises a different
  experience from the one that follows — the promise-consistency problem,
  seeded at page one.

## Questions for the author

- If a reader stopped at the bottom of page one, what would they be able to
  say this book is about?
- What is the first thing on the page that a reader might actually want to
  know the answer to?
- Is the opening scene the first moment when something changes, or the last
  moment before it does?
- What does the opening promise about tone, pace, and subject — and does
  chapter ten still honor it?
- If the orientation in the first pages were cut entirely, what would a
  reader genuinely fail to follow?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Delete the first page and read from page two. If nothing is lost, the
   book was clearing its throat; if something is lost, note precisely what —
   it is usually smaller than a page.
2. Write a one-sentence statement of the story-worthy problem, then check
   whether anything in the first scene gestures toward it. If not, consider
   whether the opening is doing the book's work or its own.
3. Ask a first reader to stop at the end of chapter one and say what they
   expect from the book. The gap between that and the actual book is the
   promise the opening is really making.
4. For a serialized draft, compare the first chapter against the opening
   chapters of three stories in the same category — not to imitate them, but
   to see what job those openings do that yours may be deferring.

## When this advice does not apply

- Literary and experimental traditions where a slow, ambient, or
  disorienting opening is the intended effect and the readership expects it.
- Later books in a series, where the reader arrives with attachment already
  built and an opening may reasonably spend time re-establishing rather than
  hooking.
- Any case where the intent is to filter for a particular reader rather than
  retain the widest one — a legitimate strategy with a known cost.

## Evidence and detection limits

A coach can observe structural facts: how far into the first chapter the
first conflict appears, how much of the first page is exposition or
backstory, whether a named character is present in the first paragraph,
whether the chapter ends open or closed. These are countable from the text.

Whether an opening *works* cannot be determined. Reader commitment is a
response, not a property of the page, and it varies by reader, platform, and
expectation. The absence of a conventional hook is not evidence of failure,
and its presence is not evidence of success. A coach should describe what
the opening is doing and what it appears to promise, and leave the judgment
of effect to the author and their readers.

## Original micro-examples

*Opening that defers:* "The Kesarin Empire had stood nine hundred years. Its
founding is recorded in the Third Chronicle..." — accurate, and the reader
has not yet met anyone.

*Opening in motion:* "Tomas had been told the vault door could not be opened
from the inside. He had eleven minutes to find out who had been wrong." —
a person, a situation, a limit, and a question, in two sentences.

*Hook with nothing behind it:* "I died on a Tuesday." — followed by four
chapters of school routine in which nobody dies and the line is never
explained. The attention it bought is gone by page three.

## Sources and confidence notes

Opening craft is well covered by practitioner literature. Les Edgerton's
dedicated treatment ([[src.book.edgerton-hooked]]) supplies the
surface-problem versus story-worthy-problem distinction and the catalogue of
recognizable weak openings; Donald Maass
([[src.book.maass-writing-breakout-novel]]) supplies the argument about early
tension and reader investment; Janet Burroway
([[src.book.burroway-writing-fiction]]) supplies the scene-construction and
orientation principles. The three agree broadly on what openings do and
disagree on how prescriptive to be, which this record preserves rather than
resolves.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the sense the handoff defines: the craft
principles are well supported by multiple independent sources, while the
serialized-publishing application — that platform browsing makes openings
compete directly — is this drafting pass's synthesis from the genre research
and is marked as such rather than attributed to any of the three craft texts,
none of which discuss serial platforms. The claim that readers "decide" on
page one is a widely repeated practitioner belief; no reader-response study
is cited here, and none should be inferred.
