## What it is

A character's introduction is the passage where a reader first meets them,
and it does more work than its length suggests. It tells the reader how much
attention this person deserves, what kind of person they are likely to be,
and — often decisively — whether to bother remembering their name.

Craft writing on character treats the entrance as a signalling problem: a
reader budgets attention across a cast, and takes their cues from how the
book presents each arrival. A major character introduced in passing may be
forgotten; a minor one introduced with ceremony may be waited for.

## Why readers may care

Cast management is one of the quieter reasons readers abandon books. Too many
people arriving too fast, insufficiently distinguished, produces the
experience of names without faces — and once a reader has stopped tracking
who is who, every subsequent scene is harder.

A good entrance also does characterization economically. A person shown once
doing something specific is easier to hold than a paragraph of description,
and the reader who has watched a character act has been given a basis for
predicting them, which is most of what attachment is made of.

## Common forms and variants

- **Introduction by action** — the character first appears doing something
  that shows how they operate.
- **Introduction by reputation** — others discuss them before they arrive, so
  the entrance is measured against an expectation the book has built.
- **Introduction by contrast** — arriving in a scene where their difference
  from the people present is the point.
- **Deliberate underplay** — a significant character introduced casually, so
  their importance is a later surprise. Effective, and costly if the reader
  genuinely fails to register them.
- **The delayed entrance** — a character named and withheld, where anticipation
  does the characterization until they arrive.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character's first line of dialogue doing the work a paragraph of
  description would otherwise do.
- One physical detail that recurs, so the reader has a hook to hang the name
  on.
- Six named characters in two pages, none acting, all described.
- A significant character introduced in a crowd scene and not distinguished
  from it, then treated fifty pages later as though the reader knows them.

## Common failure modes

- **The name flood.** Many characters introduced together, so none is
  distinct and the reader stops tracking.
- **Introduction by inventory.** Hair, height, eye colour, clothing —
  cataloguing appearance in place of showing behavior.
- **Mismatched signalling.** A major character given a minor entrance, or the
  reverse, so the reader budgets attention wrongly and feels misled later.
- **Interchangeable arrivals.** Several characters introduced the same way, in
  the same register, which makes them hard to separate regardless of how
  different they later become.
- **The unearned reintroduction.** A character reappearing after a long
  absence with no reminder, in a book with a cast too large for the reader to
  have retained them.

## Questions for the author

- What does this character's first appearance let a reader predict about
  them?
- Does the size of this entrance match the size of this character's role?
- If a reader met this character only once, what single thing would they
  remember?
- How many new names does this chapter ask the reader to hold, and does it
  need to?
- When this character returns after an absence, does the text help the reader
  place them?

## Revision or design experiments

1. List each named character with the chapter of their first appearance and
   what they do in it. Entries where the answer is "is described" are
   candidates for rework.
2. Take the most crowded introduction scene and stagger it: move two arrivals
   later and see whether anything is lost.
3. Give a hard-to-remember character one specific, repeatable detail — a
   habit, an object, a way of speaking — and track whether they become easier
   to follow.
4. For a character who returns after fifty pages, add one clause of reminder
   and see whether the scene reads more easily.

## When this advice does not apply

- Large-canvas novels whose readers expect a substantial cast and arrive
  willing to work, often with a dramatis personae to lean on.
- Deliberate disorientation, where a crowd of undistinguished names is the
  intended experience.
- Ensemble openings where the group, not any individual, is the unit being
  introduced.
- Later books in a series, where the cast is established and full
  introductions would be redundant — though the reintroduction problem
  becomes sharper, not weaker.

## Evidence and detection limits

A coach can locate first appearances of named entities, count new names per
chapter, measure the gap between a character's appearances, and note whether
a first appearance contains dialogue or action attributed to them. Name
density per chapter is straightforwardly countable and often the single most
useful figure here.

What cannot be determined is whether an introduction *worked*. Memorability
is a reader-side effect, and the same entrance may be vivid to one reader and
invisible to another. A high name count is not a fault — some books support
it easily. The coach should surface the density and the gaps, and ask whether
the attention budget matches the author's intent.

## Original micro-examples

*Introduction by inventory:* "Sera was twenty-four, with dark hair she wore
in a braid, green eyes, and a scar above her left eyebrow. She was a talented
archer." — Four facts, no behavior, nothing to predict from.

*Introduction by action:* "Sera shot the lock off the gate, then stood
looking at the ruined mechanism with an expression of mild regret, as though
it had been someone else's idea." — One act and one reaction, and the reader
already knows something about how she will handle the next problem.

## Sources and confidence notes

Orson Scott Card ([[src.book.card-characters-and-viewpoint]]) supplies the
argument that a character's introduction signals their importance and that
readers allocate attention accordingly; Janet Burroway
([[src.book.burroway-writing-fiction]]) supplies the treatment of
characterization through action and speech rather than catalogued
description; John Gardner ([[src.book.gardner-art-of-fiction]]) supplies the
principle about concrete detail that this record applies to entrances
specifically.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the handoff's second sense: the
characterization principles are independently supported by three established
craft sources, while the framing of entrances as an *attention budget*, the
name-density diagnostic, and the reintroduction problem are this drafting
pass's synthesis. No claim is made about how many names readers can in fact
track; that number is often asserted in craft advice and, as far as this pass
found, not established by any study worth citing.
