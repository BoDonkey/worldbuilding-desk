## What it is

The climax is the scene, or sequence of scenes, where the story's central
dramatic question is finally decided — usually the point of maximum pressure,
where the protagonist's choices and the accumulated cost of the story's
events converge. The resolution (or denouement) follows it, showing what the
climax's outcome costs and what the new normal looks like. The two are
distinct beats: a climax that answers the question and a resolution that
lets the reader feel the answer settle.

## Why readers may care

A climax works best when it's decided by what the protagonist chooses to do,
not by an outside force resolving the conflict for them — this is where a
story's accumulated agency and causal escalation pay off at once. A
resolution that's skipped or rushed can leave a strong climax feeling
incomplete, because the reader never gets to see the outcome's cost land, and
a resolution that's dragged out can dilute a climax's impact by refusing to
let the story end.

## Common forms and variants

- **Decisive climax**, where the protagonist's own choice, under pressure,
  directly resolves the central question.
- **Costly-win climax**, where the protagonist succeeds but at a specific,
  dramatized cost that the resolution then addresses.
- **Failure climax**, where the protagonist's central goal is not achieved,
  and the resolution shows how the story's world and characters adjust to
  that failure — a legitimate, sometimes more resonant, structure.
- **Compressed climax-resolution**, common in shorter or faster-paced work,
  where the two blend into a single brief final beat rather than distinct
  scenes.
- **Extended resolution / epilogue**, showing a significant time jump after
  the climax to demonstrate lasting consequence — powerful when earned,
  risky when it re-litigates or undercuts the climax's outcome.

## What it can look like on the page

- A final confrontation whose outcome traces directly to a choice the
  protagonist makes in the moment, rather than an external rescue or
  coincidence.
- A scene following the climax that shows cost, aftermath, or a changed
  status quo, rather than jumping straight to "the end."
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a climax resolved by an outside force, or a
  resolution that arrives so quickly the climax's cost never registers.

## Common failure modes

- **The outsourced climax**, where the actual resolving action is taken by
  someone other than the protagonist, undercutting the payoff of everything
  the story built toward the protagonist's agency.
- **The rushed resolution**, ending within a paragraph or two of the climax
  with no room for the reader to feel the outcome's weight.
- **The over-extended resolution**, dragging on long enough to dilute the
  climax's momentum or introduce new complications that read as delaying the
  actual ending.
- **A climax that doesn't answer the central question** the story raised,
  resolving a different, lower-stakes conflict instead.

## Questions for the author

- Does the climax turn on a choice the protagonist makes, or on something
  that happens to them?
- What central dramatic question did this story raise in its opening act,
  and does the climax actually answer it?
- Is there a resolution scene that lets the reader feel the climax's cost, or
  does the story end the moment the conflict resolves?
- If there's an extended resolution or epilogue, does it earn its length, or
  does it delay an ending that's already arrived?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Identify the specific choice the protagonist makes at the climax; if the
   resolution instead depends on an outside actor or event, consider
   revising so the protagonist's choice is what decides it.
2. Name the central dramatic question from the opening act in one sentence
   and check whether the climax answers that exact question.
3. If the resolution feels rushed, add a scene (even a short one) that shows
   a specific cost or changed relationship resulting from the climax.
4. If an epilogue exists, test cutting it and see whether the climax alone
   provides a satisfying stopping point; add back only what the story needs.

## When this advice does not apply

- Ongoing serials without a single planned ending may have climaxes that
  resolve an arc's question while deliberately leaving a larger one open —
  the pattern still applies at the arc level even without series-level
  closure.
- Ambiguous or deliberately unresolved endings are a legitimate structural
  choice in some literary traditions, where withholding a clear resolution
  is itself the intended effect.
- Some ensemble structures distribute climactic weight across several
  characters rather than a single protagonist's choice.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a climax is decided by the protagonist's own choice, and whether it
answers the story's central dramatic question, requires reading and
interpreting the whole manuscript's arc — a judgment no structured data
substitutes for. A coach could identify the manuscript's final chapters as
candidate evidence but cannot verify from text alone whether the climax
satisfies the question the opening raised.

## Original micro-examples

*Outsourced climax:* The protagonist confronts the antagonist, and just as
the confrontation turns dire, an allied army arrives and settles the
conflict without further action from the protagonist.

*Decisive climax:* The same confrontation instead turns on the protagonist
choosing, at personal cost, to reveal a secret that turns the antagonist's
own allies against them — the outcome traces directly to a choice only the
protagonist could make.

## Sources and confidence notes

The climax-as-decision and resolution-as-cost framing is foundational in
both Robert McKee's writing on story structure
([[src.book.mckee-story]]) and John Truby's structural writing on climax and
revelation ([[src.book.truby-anatomy-of-story]]); both long-established,
widely taught texts, supporting a `high` confidence rating for the core
principle, while its application to any specific manuscript remains
model-assisted.
