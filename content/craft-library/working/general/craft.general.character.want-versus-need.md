## What it is

A want is what a character consciously pursues — a goal they could state out
loud, driving the external plot. A need is what would actually resolve their
underlying wound or misbelief, which the character often can't articulate
and may actively avoid. The two frequently point in different directions for
much of a story, and a common (not universal) shape of a satisfying arc is
the character eventually recognizing, choosing, or being forced toward the
need, sometimes at the cost of the original want.

## Why readers may care

A story where want and need are identical from page one has less room to
surprise a reader with growth, because getting what the character wants and
what they need are the same event. A story where they diverge creates a
second, internal question running alongside the external plot: will this
character get what they're chasing, and will it actually satisfy them if
they do? That second question is often what makes an ending feel earned
rather than merely resolved.

## Common forms and variants

- **Convergent arcs**, where pursuing the want gradually reveals or produces
  the need, and the two align by the story's end.
- **Divergent arcs**, where achieving the want explicitly fails to satisfy
  the need, forcing a reckoning (often at the climax) about what actually
  matters.
- **Sacrifice arcs**, where the character must give up the want to attain the
  need, or vice versa, making the choice itself the story's climax.
- **Static-want stories**, where the want and need are aligned from the
  start and the story is about obstacles to a clear goal rather than
  internal misalignment — a legitimate structure for plot-forward or
  external-conflict-driven fiction.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character explicitly stating or pursuing one goal while the narration or
  subtext suggests a different, deeper dissatisfaction underneath it.
- A moment where achieving the stated want doesn't produce the expected
  relief or resolution, prompting reflection.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a character who gets exactly what they
  wanted and the story treats this as a complete, satisfying ending with no
  acknowledgment of anything left unresolved, when the setup implied
  something deeper was at stake.

## Common failure modes

- **No stated want at all**, leaving the plot with nothing concrete to
  organize around (related to the broader motivation-monotony pattern when
  the only stated want is generic).
- **Want and need collapsed with no distinction**, which can work for
  plot-forward stories but can also read as shallow if the story's tone
  implies deeper character stakes it never delivers.
- **A need resolved off-page**, where the character's internal shift happens
  between scenes rather than being dramatized, leaving the arc's payoff
  feeling asserted rather than earned.

## Questions for the author

- What does this character consciously want, and could they state it out
  loud?
- Is there something underneath that want — a wound, a fear, a misbelief —
  that the character hasn't named, even to themselves?
- If the character got exactly what they wanted, would it actually satisfy
  them? If yes, is that itself the story you're telling, or has an implied
  deeper need gone undelivered?
- Where in the manuscript does the character's internal shift actually
  happen on the page, rather than being summarized after the fact?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write one sentence for the character's want and one for their need,
   using different vocabulary for each; if they're identical, decide whether
   that's intentional.
2. Find the scene where achieving the want and satisfying the need come into
   direct conflict, and check whether that conflict is dramatized rather
   than only implied.
3. If the need resolves off-page, try finding or adding a scene that
   dramatizes the internal shift directly.
4. For a static-want story, confirm the tone and stakes match a plot-forward
   structure rather than implying unresolved internal stakes the story never
   pays off.

## When this advice does not apply

- Plot-forward genres (some adventure, heist, or procedural structures) can
  legitimately keep want and need aligned throughout, with the satisfaction
  coming from execution rather than internal change.
- Ensemble stories may distribute want/need tension across several
  characters rather than concentrating it in one protagonist.
- A character explicitly written as static or resistant to growth (used
  deliberately, as in some tragedies or satires) may never resolve the
  want/need gap, with that failure to change being the point.

## Evidence and detection limits

Identifying a character's underlying need, distinct from their stated want,
requires interpreting subtext, internal narration, and the arc of the whole
manuscript — a judgment no structured data can substitute for. A coach could
shortlist scenes containing explicit want-statements as candidate evidence,
but inferring an unstated need is model-assisted and should be offered as a
hypothesis for the author to confirm or reject.

## Original micro-examples

*Convergent:* A character wants to win a prestigious competition to prove
their worth to a dismissive parent; over the story, they realize the need
underneath is self-respect independent of the parent's approval, and winning
the competition becomes meaningful only once that shift has happened.

*Divergent, unresolved need surfaced:* The same character wins the
competition exactly as planned, and the final scene has them call the parent
with the news, only to realize mid-call that the approval they're hoping for
still won't come — the want was achieved, the need wasn't, and the story
ends on that gap rather than resolving it.

## Sources and confidence notes

The want/need distinction and its use in arc design draws directly on Lisa
Cron's contemporary craft writing ([[src.book.cron-story-genius]]), which
frames it through a misbelief lens, and on John Truby's structural writing
