## What it is

This pattern names a specific author practice: keeping an explicit,
maintained list of the human problems a protagonist's growing power will
never solve — grief, a broken relationship, a moral failure, a structural
injustice, loneliness — and deliberately protecting that list from being
quietly closed by the next capability the character earns. Every capability
gain that shrinks a real human problem nudges a story toward what's
sometimes called competence porn: a protagonist so thoroughly capable that
nothing meaningful actually costs them anything anymore.

## Why readers may care

Power growth is satisfying because it changes what a character can attempt.
It stops being satisfying, and starts feeling hollow, when it changes
*everything* — when a character who couldn't fix their marriage two hundred
chapters ago can now, apparently, fix it too, simply because they're
stronger. The reader loses the sense that this story has stakes a bigger
sword can't touch, and the protagonist becomes someone the reader admires
without being able to worry about.

## Common forms and variants

- **A maintained negative-space list**, kept by the author outside the
  manuscript, of problems the power system explicitly cannot solve, checked
  against new capabilities as they're introduced.
- **In-text acknowledgment**, where a character or the narration explicitly
  notes that a new ability doesn't touch the problem that actually matters
  to them — making the negative space visible to the reader, not just
  protected by the author offstage.
- **Structural negative space**, where the story's power system has a hard,
  stated boundary (cannot resurrect the dead, cannot restore a severed bond,
  cannot undo a choice) that the narrative respects consistently.
- **Emergent negative space**, where the unsolvable problem isn't decided in
  advance but discovered as the story is written, and then protected once
  identified.

## What it can look like on the page

- A scene where a new, significant capability is gained, and the narration
  or a character explicitly notes what it does *not* change about their
  personal situation.
- A long-running relationship or regret that remains unresolved across many
  power-tier increases, without the story ever implying that more strength
  would have fixed it.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a personal or relational problem that
  quietly disappears from the narrative around the same time the character
  crosses a major power threshold, with no scene actually resolving it.

## Common failure modes

- **Letting power silently absorb a problem** by simply not returning to it —
  the problem isn't resolved on the page, it's just never mentioned again
  after the character becomes capable enough that a reader might expect it
  to be trivial now.
- **Solving a structural or emotional problem with a power-system shortcut**
  (a spell that erases grief, a stat that removes trauma) which can work as
  a deliberate, examined choice, but reads as a cheat if it arrives
  unexamined.
- **Never establishing any negative space at all**, so every problem in the
  story is implicitly power-shaped, and the character's eventual
  overwhelming strength quietly promises to make every future conflict
  trivial too.

## Questions for the author

- What does this character want that no amount of strength can get them?
- Is there a problem from early in the manuscript that a reader might now
  expect to be trivially solvable — and if so, has the story addressed that
  expectation directly?
- If you listed every capability this character has gained, would any of
  them plausibly "solve" a relationship, a regret, or a loss the story hasn't
  actually resolved?
- Does the power system have any stated hard limits, and do they hold under
  narrative pressure?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Draft an explicit list of three to five problems this character's power
   system cannot touch, and check every major capability gain against it
   going forward.
2. Find a moment where a new ability could plausibly be read as solving an
   old unresolved human problem, and add a line acknowledging that it
   doesn't.
3. Revisit an early emotional or relational thread and check whether the
   manuscript quietly dropped it around a major power increase; if so,
   either resolve it on the page or make its persistence visible.
4. For dungeon-core or similarly structural premises, use the protagonist's
   built-in limitations (immobility, forced proximity, inability to choose)
   as a starting negative-space list rather than inventing one from scratch.

## When this advice does not apply

- A story whose explicit premise is that power *can* solve everything (a
  wish-fulfillment or satire piece examining that idea directly) is using
  the absence of negative space on purpose.
- Not every problem needs to be permanently unsolvable — a negative-space
  list can shrink over a series as the story earns a resolution, provided the
  resolution is dramatized rather than assumed.
- Early volumes of a planned long series may deliberately leave the list
  large and mostly untouched, with payoff planned for a later book.

## Revision note on the product angle

The research behind this pattern flags that an explicit, author-maintained
negative-space list is a natural fit for a structured worldbuilding record —
tracked status, linked scenes — separate from prose itself. Whether such a
structure exists in a given project is something a coach could observe
deterministically; whether the prose meaningfully engages a listed problem,
or quietly lets power solve it anyway, remains an interpretive judgment.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where an author maintains an explicit, structured negative-space list linked
to scenes, a coach could deterministically report its existence and status.
Whether the prose actually honors that list — engaging a listed problem
without quietly resolving it through power — requires reading the
manuscript and is model-assisted, not deterministic. Absent an explicit
list, this pattern is teaching-only: it can be taught as a practice, but a
coach cannot claim to have checked an unstructured manuscript against it.

## Original micro-examples

*Silent absorption (a caution, not a model):* Wren's estrangement from a
sibling is a live thread through the manuscript's first third. Wren crosses
a major power threshold at the midpoint. The sibling thread is never
mentioned again, with no scene resolving or acknowledging it.

*Protected negative space:* Wren crosses the same threshold, and the very
next scene has Wren specifically think that this new strength changes
nothing about the sibling's silence — the story keeps that problem visibly
alive rather than letting it quietly vanish.
