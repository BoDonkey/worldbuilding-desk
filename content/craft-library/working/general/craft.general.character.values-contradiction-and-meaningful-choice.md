## What it is

A character's values are what they hold important enough to act on;
contradiction occurs when two of those values (or a value and a
circumstance) come into genuine conflict, forcing a choice that can't
satisfy both. A meaningful choice is precisely this kind of choice: one
where the available options each cost the character something they
actually value, so the option they pick reveals which value wins under
pressure — distinct from a choice between a clearly good option and a
clearly bad one, which reveals little beyond whether the character is
"good." This pattern is closely related to character agency (does the
choice determine the outcome) but focuses specifically on what the choice
reveals rather than whether it matters mechanically to the plot.

## Why readers may care

A character who never faces real contradiction between things they value
can feel simple or predictable, because every choice has an obvious right
answer given their established values. A character forced to choose between
two things they genuinely care about — loyalty and honesty, safety and
principle, love and duty — reveals depth precisely because the reader
can't predict which way they'll go, and the choice they make becomes
evidence about who they really are under pressure, not just who they claim
to be.

## Common forms and variants

- **Value-versus-value contradiction**, where two things the character
  holds important come into direct conflict (loyalty to a friend versus
  loyalty to a cause).
- **Value-versus-circumstance contradiction**, where an external situation
  makes a stated value costly or dangerous to act on, testing whether the
  character actually holds it or only claims to.
- **Value-versus-self-interest contradiction**, the classic moral-choice
  structure, where acting on a value costs the character something they
  want personally.
- **Contradictory values revealed over time**, where a character's
  stated values in one context are shown to conflict with their behavior
  in another, without the character necessarily recognizing the
  inconsistency themselves — a rich source of character complexity when
  handled deliberately.
- **False contradiction**, where a choice appears to pit two values against
  each other but one option is actually costless once examined — this
  weakens the choice's revelatory power and is worth distinguishing from
  genuine contradiction.

## What it can look like on the page

- A decision point where every available option costs the character
  something they've been shown to value, with no clearly "correct" choice
  from the character's own perspective.
- A character's chosen action in a moment of contradiction becoming
  evidence the reader uses to understand their real priorities, distinct
  from what the character has claimed about themselves.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a choice framed as difficult that, on
  examination, has an option with no real cost, making the difficulty
  assigned to it unearned.

## Common failure modes

- **The false dilemma**, presenting a choice as difficult when one option
  is actually costless or clearly superior once examined.
- **Values with no established weight**, where a character is said to
  value something but the story has never shown them acting on it, so a
  later contradiction has nothing real to draw tension from.
- **Choices resolved by author fiat rather than character logic**, where
  the "right" answer is telegraphed by narrative framing rather than
  emerging from the character's own established priorities.

## Questions for the author

- Is there a choice in this manuscript where every option costs the
  character something they've been shown to value?
- For a choice presented as difficult, does close examination reveal one
  option to actually be costless, undermining the difficulty?
- Has a character's stated value actually been shown in action before a
  later scene asks the reader to feel the weight of contradicting it?
- Does a character's choice under pressure ever surprise the reader in a
  way that's still consistent with who they've been shown to be?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Identify a choice currently framed as difficult and examine both options
   for genuine cost; if one is actually costless, either raise its stakes
   or reconsider whether the choice needs reframing.
2. Find a value the character claims or implies and add an earlier scene
   showing them act on it at some cost, so a later contradiction has
   established weight to draw from.
3. Design one scene specifically to put two of a character's established
   values in direct conflict, and let the resolution reveal something the
   reader didn't already know about their priorities.
4. Audit the manuscript's major choices for author-fiat resolution (the
   "correct" answer telegraphed by narrative framing) and consider whether
   a genuinely uncertain outcome would serve the story better.

## When this advice does not apply

- Plot-forward genres where a character's values are meant to be stable and
  legible throughout (a classic adventure hero) may not need frequent
  value contradiction to succeed on their own terms.
- Very minor or functional characters may not need developed values or
  contradiction at all, especially in large ensemble casts.
- A story deliberately depicting a character with genuinely simple, single-
  minded values (as a character trait being examined, not an oversight) is
  using consistency intentionally.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a choice presents genuine contradiction, or only the appearance of
one, requires interpreting a character's established values and the
actual costs of each option — a judgment no structured data substitutes
for.

## Original micro-examples

*False dilemma:* A character is presented with a choice between betraying a
friend's trust or losing an opportunity, but the story has never shown the
opportunity to matter to the character, so the "difficult" choice has no
real second option.

*Genuine contradiction:* The same character instead has been shown, across
several earlier scenes, building toward a long-desired goal; the choice to
abandon it in order to protect the friend costs them something the reader
has watched them want — and their choice, either way, becomes real evidence
about what they actually value most.

## Sources and confidence notes

The values-in-conflict and meaningful-choice framework is foundational in
both John Truby's structural writing on moral argument and character
revelation ([[src.book.truby-anatomy-of-story]]) and Robert McKee's writing
on value-driven scene structure ([[src.book.mckee-story]]); both
long-established, widely taught texts, supporting a `high` confidence
rating for the core principle, while identifying it in a specific
manuscript remains model-assisted.
