## What it is

A scene, in this structural sense, is a unit built around three moving
parts: a character enters wanting something specific and attainable within
the scene (a goal), meets resistance to getting it (conflict), and the scene
ends with the situation changed from how it started (an outcome) — often, but
not always, worse than the character hoped. This is one of the oldest
formalized models in modern craft teaching, usually traced to the
goal-conflict-disaster / reaction-dilemma-decision pairing, and it remains
widely taught because it gives a concrete diagnostic for "why doesn't this
scene work."

## Why readers may care

A scene with a clear goal gives a reader something to root for or worry
about from its first page. A scene with real conflict makes the outcome
uncertain enough to matter. A scene with a changed outcome — rather than a
character simply getting what they wanted cleanly — creates the causal link
that pulls a reader into the next scene, because something is now different
than it was.

## Common forms and variants

- **Classic goal-conflict-disaster.** A concrete, attainable goal; active
  opposition; an outcome that denies, complicates, or twists the goal rather
  than granting it cleanly.
- **Goal achieved, cost incurred.** The character gets what they wanted, but
  the getting costs something else, keeping the outcome from being a clean
  win.
- **Discovery scenes.** The "goal" is understanding rather than acquisition —
  a character seeking information meets resistance (a reluctant source, a
  locked archive, a lie) and the outcome changes what they know, which
  changes what they can do next.
- **Sequel/reflection scenes.** A companion unit, not this pattern's subject
  directly: a character reacts emotionally to the prior scene's disaster,
  weighs a dilemma, and makes a decision that becomes the next scene's goal.

## What it can look like on the page

- A scene opening with a character's clearly implied want, even if never
  stated in so many words.
- Escalating obstacles between the want and its pursuit, rather than a
  single easily-cleared hurdle.
- A final beat that changes the character's situation, knowledge, or
  relationships from where the scene began.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a scene where the character wants nothing
  in particular, meets no real resistance, and ends exactly where it began.

## Common failure modes

- **Goalless scenes** that exist for atmosphere or information delivery with
  no want driving them, which readers often describe as "nothing happened."
- **Conflict-free goals**, where the character wants something and simply
  gets it, removing any uncertainty that would make the outcome matter.
- **Outcomes that reset the situation**, ending a scene in the same state it
  began, which erodes momentum even when the individual scene reads fine in
  isolation.
- **Treating the model as a rigid formula** applied identically to every
  scene, which can flatten scenes that are doing different, legitimate work
  (mood, characterization, transition).

## Questions for the author

- What does the point-of-view character want at the start of this scene, and
  is it specific enough to be pursued in the pages that follow?
- What is actively working against that want, and is it strong enough to
  make the outcome uncertain?
- How is the situation different at the end of this scene than at the start?
- If this scene were cut entirely, would the story lose a causal link, or
  only some texture?

## Revision or design experiments

1. For a scene that feels flat, write one sentence naming its character's
   goal, one naming the opposition, and one naming the outcome; if any is
   hard to write, that's the gap to address.
2. Try converting a clean "goal achieved" outcome into a "goal achieved, cost
   incurred" outcome and see whether the scene gains tension.
3. For a scene with no clear resistance, introduce one obstacle — even a
   small one — and see whether the scene's stakes sharpen.
4. Trace the outcome of one scene forward: does it become the seed of the
   next scene's goal? If not, consider whether the causal chain has a gap.

## When this advice does not apply

- Interlude, mood, and pure-transition passages can legitimately lack this
  structure, provided they're used sparingly and the surrounding scenes carry
  the causal weight.
- Literary fiction working in a more associative, non-causal mode may
  deliberately decline conventional goal-conflict-outcome structure as a
  stylistic choice.
- Very short scenes (a single beat, a fragment) may compress or omit parts of
  this structure without losing effectiveness.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a scene has a legible goal, real conflict, and a changed outcome
requires reading and interpreting the prose; there is no structured data
substitute. A future coach could shortlist a scene's opening and closing
passages as evidence for the author's own comparison, but concluding that a
scene lacks structure is an interpretive judgment, not a deterministic one.

## Original micro-examples

*Missing structure:* A scene opens with a character walking through a
market, observing goods and vendors for two pages, then leaving. No want is
stated or implied; nothing resists; the situation at the end matches the
start.

*Present structure:* The same market scene opens with the character needing
to sell a family heirloom before nightfall to cover a debt, a vendor refuses
the asking price and threatens to report the item as stolen, and the scene
ends with the character having pawned it for less than needed and now owing
someone a favor to make up the difference.

## Sources and confidence notes

The goal-conflict-disaster / reaction-dilemma-decision model traces to Dwight
Swain's foundational text ([[src.book.swain-techniques-selling-writer]]),
still widely taught under various renamings. Robert McKee's scene-as-value-
shift framing ([[src.book.mckee-story]]) supports the outcome-must-change-the-
situation principle from a complementary angle. Both are established,
long-standing craft texts rather than recent or contested ones; confidence
is set to `high` on that basis, while individual application to any specific
manuscript remains model-assisted.
