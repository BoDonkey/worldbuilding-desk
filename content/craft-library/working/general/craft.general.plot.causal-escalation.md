## What it is

Causal escalation is the principle that each major plot event should be
caused by the one before it and should raise the stakes of what follows,
rather than simply occurring next in sequence. A commonly cited shorthand
distinguishes "and then" plotting (events connected only by sequence) from
"therefore" and "but" plotting (events connected by consequence and
complication) — the second produces a felt sense of a story building, the
first produces a felt sense of a list of things that happened.

## Why readers may care

A reader tracks cause and effect even without consciously naming it. A story
where each event follows logically, and consequentially, from the last
creates a growing sense of momentum and inevitability; a story where events
are merely sequential — this happens, then this happens, then this happens —
tends to read as episodic even if individual scenes are well written, because
nothing compounds.

## Common forms and variants

- **Direct causation**, where one event's outcome is the direct cause of the
  next event's setup.
- **Delayed causation**, where an early event's consequence surfaces several
  scenes or chapters later, requiring the reader to hold the connection in
  mind.
- **Compounding complications**, where a solution to one problem creates or
  reveals a new, larger problem (the "but" half of "therefore, but").
- **Escalating stakes without escalating scale**, where the *kind* of risk
  deepens (a physical threat becomes a moral one) rather than simply growing
  bigger.

## What it can look like on the page

- A plot outline where each beat can be connected to the previous one with
  "therefore" or "but," rather than "and then."
- A midpoint or late-act event that recontextualizes an earlier one,
  revealing it as more consequential than it first appeared.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a sequence of events that could be
  reordered without changing what any later scene depends on.

## Common failure modes

- **Episodic plotting**, where each chapter or arc resolves cleanly with
  little carrying forward into the next, common in early drafts and some
  serialized structures under pressure to deliver a self-contained unit
  regularly.
- **Escalation by scale alone**, raising the size of a threat (a bigger
  monster, a higher number) without raising the complexity or kind of
  problem it poses, which can read as repetitive despite technically
  "escalating."
- **Convenient rather than caused turns**, where a plot event happens because
  the story needs it to, not because prior events made it likely.

## Questions for the author

- For each major event in this act, can you name what earlier event caused
  it?
- If two chapters were reordered, would anything downstream break? If not,
  is the connective tissue between them as strong as it should be?
- Does the story's central problem grow more complex over time, or just
  bigger?
- Is there a solution in this manuscript that creates a new problem, rather
  than simply resolving cleanly?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Outline the manuscript's major beats and label each connection as
   "and then," "therefore," or "but"; look for clusters of "and then."
2. For an episodic stretch, identify one consequence from an early event
   that could be planted to pay off later, tying the stretch into the
   larger causal chain.
3. Try converting one clean solution into a solution-with-a-cost, so it
   creates a new complication rather than simply closing a loop.
4. Test reordering two adjacent chapters; if nothing breaks, look for a
   missing causal link between them.

## When this advice does not apply

- Anthology-like or deliberately episodic structures (some serialized web
  fiction, picaresque narratives) can use loosely connected events as a
  structural choice, provided the overall work signals that mode.
- A story working toward a thematic rather than causal throughline (a mosaic
  novel, for instance) may connect its parts by resonance rather than direct
  causation.
- Very early chapters establishing a world or ensemble may reasonably feel
  more sequential before the causal engine fully engages.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether one plot event causes another, and whether stakes are genuinely
escalating rather than merely repeating at a larger scale, requires reading
and interpreting the manuscript's actual causal chain; no structured data
substitutes for this. A coach can prompt the author to trace causal links
explicitly but cannot verify them from text alone with certainty.

## Original micro-examples

*And-then plotting:* A character survives a storm at sea, then encounters a
merchant in the next port, then is challenged to a duel, then wins a prize —
each event self-contained, none causing the next.

*Therefore-but plotting:* The same character survives the storm specifically
by jettisoning cargo that belonged to a merchant, who now demands repayment
the character cannot afford; to raise the funds, the character accepts a
duel whose prize would cover the debt, but winning it exposes a skill the
character was hiding, which the merchant recognizes and uses as leverage
instead.

## Sources and confidence notes

The causal-versus-sequential distinction is foundational in both John
Truby's structural writing ([[src.book.truby-anatomy-of-story]]) and Robert
McKee's story-causality framing ([[src.book.mckee-story]]); both are
long-established, widely taught texts, supporting a `high` confidence
rating for the core principle. Its application to any specific manuscript
remains model-assisted.
