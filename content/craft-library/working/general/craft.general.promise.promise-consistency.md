## What it is

A story's opening chapters — sometimes its opening pages — establish an
implicit promise: what kind of experience this is, what tone it will hold,
what stakes it takes seriously, what it will and won't do to its characters.
Promise consistency is the degree to which the rest of the chapter, the rest
of the manuscript, and the rest of a series keep that contract. It operates
at every scale a coach has available: does this scene keep the chapter's
promise, does this chapter keep the opening's promise, does this book keep
book one's promise.

## Why readers may care

Two independent lines of evidence point at the same idea from different
angles. Reader-side analysis of long-running series names inconsistent
promise-keeping — a cozy opening that turns grim, or a desperate-stakes
opening that goes soft — as a throughline in series that lose readers over
time. Separately, early-chapter retention research treats poor chapter-one
to chapter-two retention as a promise mismatch rather than a prose-quality
problem: readers aren't leaving because chapter two is badly written, they're
leaving because chapter two isn't the book chapter one promised. Promise
consistency is the concept that connects a scene-level craft note to a
series-level retention outcome.

## Common forms and variants

- **Tonal promise** — a story's emotional register (cozy, grim, comic,
  desperate) established early and either sustained or drifted from.
- **Stakes promise** — the level of consequence and risk a story establishes
  as its baseline, which readers calibrate their expectations around.
- **Structural promise** — the kind of plot a story signals it will deliver
  (a mystery that will be solved, a romance that will resolve, a quest that
  will complete), which readers track even when they can't articulate the
  signal that told them.
- **Series-level promise** — the contract a first book makes that later
  books either honor, deliberately and visibly evolve, or quietly abandon.
- **Deliberate promise subversion** — a story that signals one promise and
  breaks it on purpose, which can be a powerful effect when the break itself
  is the point, and a betrayal when it reads as drift rather than intent.

## What it can look like on the page

- An opening chapter's tone, stakes, and implied genre, compared against a
  chapter from the book's midpoint or a later book in a series.
- A cozy or low-stakes opening followed by sustained brutal violence with no
  signaled tonal shift.
- A story that promises a specific mystery or quest resolution and then
  quietly drops or indefinitely defers it across volumes.
- Reader reviews or comments (where available) describing a specific book or
  chapter as feeling like "a different story" from what came before it.

## Common failure modes

- **Unintentional tonal drift**, where later installments of a long series
  gradually shift tone as the author's own interests or skills evolve,
  without the shift being signaled to readers as deliberate.
- **Confusing escalation with promise-keeping**, where rising stakes are
  mistaken for a stable promise even though the actual felt experience (cozy
  versus desperate, hopeful versus grim) has changed underneath the rising
  numbers.
- **Treating promise-keeping as "never changing anything,"** which
  overcorrects into stagnation; a story can evolve its promise deliberately
  and transparently without breaking faith with readers, and confusing that
  with rigid sameness is its own failure mode.

## Questions for the author

- If a reader described this book's opening chapter in three words, would
  those same three words describe its climax?
- Has anything about this story's tone, stakes, or implied kind of ending
  changed since the opening — and if so, was that change signaled to the
  reader anywhere?
- In a series, does book three keep the promise book one made, or has it
  quietly become a different kind of book?
- If early chapters are losing readers, is that a prose problem, or is
  chapter two simply not the book chapter one promised?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write a one-sentence promise statement for the opening chapter (tone,
   stakes, implied kind of story) and check it against a sentence written the
   same way for the midpoint and the ending.
2. For a series, compare a scene from book one against a comparable scene
   from the most recent book, looking specifically at tone and stakes rather
   than plot content.
3. If a deliberate promise shift is planned, look for a scene that can
   explicitly signal the shift to the reader before it happens, rather than
   letting it arrive unannounced.
4. For a struggling early-chapter retention problem, reread chapter one
   asking only "what does this promise," then reread chapter two asking only
   "does this deliver on that," separately from any prose-quality read of
   either.

## When this advice does not apply

- A story built around a structural twist in its own premise — an opening
  that deliberately misleads the reader about what kind of book this is, as
  the twist itself — is using inconsistency as a technique, not failing to
  keep a promise.
- Long series legitimately evolve as their authors and audiences do; a
  transparent, gradual evolution acknowledged within the text is different
  from an unannounced drift.
- A single outlier chapter (a flashback, an interlude in a different voice)
  can depart from the main promise briefly without breaking it, provided the
  surrounding text signals the departure as intentional.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a specific scene, chapter, or book keeps or breaks an established
promise requires reading and comparing prose for tone, stakes, and implied
genre — an interpretive judgment with no deterministic substitute. A future
coach could shortlist an opening passage and a comparison passage as
evidence for the author's own review, but concluding that a promise was kept
or broken is model-assisted, not something structured data can settle.
Series-level promise-consistency claims additionally require access to
multiple books' worth of manuscript, which most coaching sessions will not
have simultaneously.

## Original micro-examples

*Promise kept:* A story opens with a character narrowly avoiding eviction and
worrying about a light bill. Its climax three hundred pages later still turns
on a resource shortage the character cannot simply out-power — the stakes
grew in scale but stayed the same kind.

*Promise broken without signal:* The same opening, but by the climax the
character commands an army and the story's central tension has become a
political succession crisis with no scene marking the shift from personal,
survival-scale stakes to political, systemic ones.

## Sources and confidence notes

The series-longevity framing and the "consistently delivers on its initial
promise" finding come from a single vendor's coded analysis of Reddit
discussion ([[src.chapterchronicles.series-falloff]]), marked practitioner/
reader consensus rather than controlled research; that same source frames
promise consistency as a candidate connecting concept rather than a settled
conclusion, and `docs/research-litrpg-craft-failures.md` explicitly raises,
as an open question, whether promise consistency deserves to be the
library's organizing idea rather than one entry among many
([[src.internal.litrpg-craft-failures-research]]). The general-craft framing
of reader promise and stakes escalation draws on established craft writing
about market-aware structure ([[src.book.maass-writing-breakout-novel]]).
This record treats promise consistency as a strong, cross-source hypothesis
worth prioritizing in coaching, not as a proven causal mechanism.
