## What it is

Humor in fiction is often treated as a temperament — some writers are funny
and some are not — but comedy writing has a craft literature that treats it
as constructible: built out of exaggeration, reversal, incongruity, timing,
and characters whose flaws produce trouble. This record treats humor as a
craft element with jobs to do, rather than as a personality trait an author
either has or lacks.

It sits alongside voice as a craft element rather than inside it. Voice is
the narration's overall sensibility; humor is a specific effect that can
operate through voice, through character, through situation, or through
structure, and each of those behaves differently under revision.

## Why readers may care

Humor buys relief, and relief is a pacing instrument. A story running at
sustained pressure exhausts readers; a moment of comedy releases it without
stopping the plot, in much the way a quieter scene does but faster.

It also characterizes efficiently. What a person finds funny, whether they
joke under pressure, and whether anyone laughs at them tells a reader a great
deal in very little space. And in darker material, comedy is frequently what
makes the dark parts bearable — a book with no lightness anywhere asks a lot
of a reader across a long serial.

## Common forms and variants

- **Character humor** — funny because of who this person is, which is the
  most durable kind since it also does characterization.
- **Situational comedy** — funny because of what has gone wrong, usually
  built by escalation.
- **Voice humor** — funny in the narration itself, through phrasing, timing,
  and understatement.
- **Banter** — comedy in the exchange between characters, which carries
  relationship information alongside the joke.
- **Undercutting** — a comic beat placed immediately after a serious one,
  which either releases tension usefully or discards it, depending on
  placement.
- **The comic register that never breaks** — sustained comedy as the book's
  whole mode, which changes what its serious moments cost.

## What it can look like on the page

- A joke that only this character could have made.
- A scene whose comedy comes from escalating a small problem rather than
  from anyone saying anything funny.
- Understatement in the narration doing the work no character has to do
  aloud.
- Conversely: a designated funny character whose lines could be redistributed
  to anyone, and a running gag repeated past the point where it stopped being
  a surprise.

## Common failure modes

- **The joke that costs the scene.** Comedy placed where tension was
  accumulating, releasing pressure the story needed.
- **The designated comic.** One character carrying all humor, which flattens
  them into a function — closely related to the exposition-companion pattern.
- **Tonal whiplash.** Comedy and gravity alternating without transition, so
  neither register is trusted.
- **The gag with no half-life.** A running joke repeated at the same
  intensity until the reader is waiting for it to stop.
- **Humor as an apology.** Undercutting every sincere moment, so the book
  never asks the reader to take anything seriously — sometimes a voice,
  frequently a defence.

## Questions for the author

- What is the humor here *for* — relief, characterization, tone, or
  contrast?
- Could another character have made this joke? If yes, it may not be
  character humor.
- Is this comic beat releasing tension the story wanted released?
- Does the book have any lightness in it at all, and if not, is that a
  choice?
- Does the running gag change over its repetitions, or only recur?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Mark every comic beat in a chapter and note what each is doing. Beats with
   no answer are candidates for cutting or repurposing.
2. Move one joke a paragraph later, past the tension peak rather than into
   it, and see what changes.
3. Redistribute one of the designated comic's lines to a serious character
   and see whether it gets funnier.
4. Take a running gag and escalate or invert its third appearance rather than
   repeating it.

## When this advice does not apply

- Work whose promise is unrelieved intensity — some horror, some tragedy —
  where lightness would break the contract deliberately made with the reader.
- Comic novels, where humor is the mode rather than an element, and the
  questions above invert: the interesting problem becomes when to stop being
  funny.
- Cultural and personal variation in what is funny is wide enough that
  "this isn't landing" is often about audience rather than construction, and
  a coach should be slow to call a joke a failure.

## Evidence and detection limits

This is among the least detectable patterns in the library. Humor has no
reliable textual signature: a coach cannot identify a joke, and certainly
cannot tell whether one works. Some weak proxies exist — dialogue density,
the recurrence of a distinctive phrase that might be a running gag — and none
is trustworthy.

A coach should treat humor as something the author raises, not something the
system detects. If asked, it can discuss placement relative to tension, the
distribution of comic lines across characters, and the structural questions
above, all as reasoning about a passage the author has identified rather than
as detection.

## Original micro-examples

*Redistributable joke:* "'Well, that went well,' said Pell, who was the
funny one." — The line is fine and belongs to nobody.

*Character humor:* Roan, who had never in his life admitted to being
frightened, said the cave was structurally interesting and that he would
guard the entrance from out here, where the light was better for it.

## Sources and confidence notes

John Vorhaus ([[src.book.vorhaus-comic-toolbox]]) supplies this record's
central premise — that comedy is constructible from identifiable components
rather than being an innate gift — along with the roles of exaggeration,
reversal, and character flaw. Ursula K. Le Guin
([[src.book.leguin-steering-the-craft]]) supplies the treatment of timing and
rhythm this record applies to comic placement. Everything else is marked
synthesis ([[src.synthesis.craft-library-editorial]]).

`source_confidence` is `limited`, deliberately. Vorhaus writes for screen and
comedy writing, not prose fiction, so his framework is being transferred
rather than applied; Le Guin does not discuss humor specifically. No craft
source examined by this pass treats humor's *structural* role in long-form
prose — the relief, pacing, and bearability arguments here are this pass's
reasoning, not sourced claims. This is a revision-pass target: there is a
substantial theoretical literature on comedy, from Bergson onward, that a
later pass with access to it should bring in. Nothing here should be read as
a claim about what readers in general find funny, which varies far too widely
to generalize.
