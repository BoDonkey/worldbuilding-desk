## What it is

Psychic distance describes how close narration sits to a character's
immediate perception and thought, ranging from a very distant, summarizing
register ("she was angry for a long time") to an extremely close one that
renders thought and sensation almost as they occur, with minimal narratorial
mediation ("the words hit like a slap, and she was already moving before
she'd decided to"). Interiority is the related but distinct question of how
much inner life — thought, sensation, memory, association — the prose
actually renders at all, independent of how close or distant that
rendering is. Both are dials an author controls scene by scene and even
sentence by sentence; they aren't fixed properties that come bundled with a
choice of first or third person.

## Why readers may care

Distance and depth of interiority directly shape a reader's felt closeness
to a character's experience. Close psychic distance during a high-intensity
moment can make a reader feel almost inside the character's skin; pulling
back to a more distant register can create a sense of remove, dread, or
reflection. A story that never varies its distance — staying uniformly
close or uniformly distant throughout — loses one of the most precise tools
available for calibrating a scene's emotional temperature to its actual
content.

## Common forms and variants

- **Very distant psychic distance**, summarizing a character's state from
  outside, closer to an omniscient or reportorial register.
- **Moderate psychic distance**, conventional close-third narration that
  reports thought and feeling with some narratorial framing ("she felt
  a flash of anger").
- **Very close psychic distance**, minimizing narratorial mediation,
  rendering thought and sensation nearly as the character experiences them,
  often without dialogue tags like "she thought."
- **Deliberate distance shifts**, moving between registers within a single
  scene — pulling close for an emotional peak, pulling back for reflection
  or dread — as a controlled technique rather than an accident.
- **Rich interiority at any distance**, where a lot of inner life is
  rendered even at a moderate distance, versus **sparse interiority**,
  where very little inner life is shown even in close narration — these
  are independent of the distance dial itself.

## What it can look like on the page

- A scene's psychic distance visibly tightening at its most emotionally
  significant moment, then loosening again as the intensity passes.
- Rich, specific interiority rendered at a close distance during a
  character's decisive moment, giving the reader direct access to the
  reasoning or sensation driving the choice.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: uniform psychic distance throughout an
  entire manuscript, with no variation regardless of a scene's emotional
  weight.

## Common failure modes

- **Uniform distance throughout**, missing the opportunity to intensify or
  soften a scene's emotional register through a deliberate shift.
- **Distance mismatched to content**, staying distant during a moment that
  calls for closeness (an emotional climax narrated at arm's length) or
  staying uncomfortably close during a moment that would benefit from some
  remove.
- **Interiority overload**, rendering so much inner life at close distance
  that pacing suffers, especially during action sequences where extended
  interiority can stall momentum.
- **Interiority starvation**, especially in close POV, where a character's
  actions are reported with almost no access to the reasoning or feeling
  behind them, leaving the reader oddly disconnected despite the close
  technical distance.

## Questions for the author

- Does this manuscript's psychic distance vary deliberately across scenes,
  or does it stay at roughly the same setting throughout?
- At this scene's most emotionally significant moment, is the narration
  closer or more distant than the surrounding prose — and is that a
  deliberate choice?
- Is there a scene where extended interiority is slowing momentum that the
  content doesn't actually need?
- Is there a scene where a character's actions are reported with too
  little access to the reasoning or feeling behind them, leaving the reader
  disconnected?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Mark a manuscript's psychic distance at several points (very
   distant/moderate/very close) and check whether the pattern correlates
   with each scene's emotional weight.
2. For a flat emotional peak, try tightening psychic distance — cutting
   narratorial framing like "she felt" or "he thought" and rendering the
   sensation more directly.
3. For an action sequence that drags, try loosening psychic distance and
   trimming interiority to keep momentum.
4. For a scene where a character's motivation feels opaque, add a small
   amount of interiority at the moment of decision, even briefly.

## When this advice does not apply

- Some genres and traditions (certain thrillers, some literary styles)
  deliberately maintain consistent distance throughout as a stylistic
  signature, where variation would work against the intended effect.
- Very short forms may not have room for the shift-and-return pattern this
  technique typically relies on.
- Omniscient narrators working in a consistently distant register
  throughout are using a different, equally legitimate mode, not failing to
  vary distance.

## Evidence and detection limits

Identifying a passage's psychic distance and whether it's calibrated to its
content requires reading and interpreting the prose directly — a judgment
no structured data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Distant, at the wrong moment:* "She was devastated by the news" is the
entirety of the narration at a story's most significant emotional turn,
with no closer access to the character's actual experience.

*Calibrated closeness:* The same moment instead renders the immediate,
disordered sensation directly — the specific detail she fixates on
irrationally, the half-formed thought that won't complete — before the
narration pulls back out to a calmer, more distant register once the
initial shock passes.

## Sources and confidence notes

Psychic distance as a controllable, scene-by-scene dial is foundational,
widely taught craft knowledge, drawn here from Ursula K. Le Guin's
dedicated writing on the concept ([[src.book.leguin-steering-the-craft]])
and John Gardner's writing on narrative distance and the "vivid, continuous
dream" ([[src.book.gardner-art-of-fiction]]); both long-established texts,
supporting a `high` confidence rating for the core principle, while
identifying specific instances in any given manuscript remains
model-assisted.
