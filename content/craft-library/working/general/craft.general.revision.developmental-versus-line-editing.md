## What it is

Developmental revision changes what the book is: which scenes exist, what
happens in them, who the characters are, what the story is about. Line
revision changes how it reads: sentence by sentence, word by word, at the
level of clarity and rhythm.

The distinction matters because the two interact asymmetrically. A
developmental change can delete a scene that took a day to line-edit; a line
change almost never affects structure. That asymmetry implies an order, and
this record is largely about the cost of ignoring it.

## Why readers may care

Readers do not encounter revision, only its results — but they encounter the
consequences of misdirected revision constantly. A manuscript that has been
polished sentence by sentence without a structural pass reads as
well-written and slow: nothing is wrong with any paragraph, and the book
does not move. Editors' craft literature returns repeatedly to this pattern,
because it is what a great many submitted manuscripts look like.

The reverse case is rarer and easier to fix. A structurally sound book with
rough prose can be improved by a line pass; a beautifully written book with
no shape usually cannot be improved without losing much of the writing.

## Common forms and variants

- **The structural pass** — scene inventory, ordering, cutting, and
  rewriting at the level of what happens.
- **The character pass** — reading for one character's arc, motivation, and
  consistency across the whole draft.
- **The continuity pass** — facts, timelines, names, and established rules,
  treated separately in this library.
- **The line pass** — clarity, rhythm, repetition, and habitual
  constructions.
- **The read-through** — an uninterrupted reading with no editing at all,
  used to find where attention drifts rather than to fix anything.
- **Simultaneous revision** — doing all of it at once, which is what most
  drafts get and which reliably produces polished sentences inside unresolved
  structure.

## What it can look like on the page

- A chapter with three well-turned paragraphs of description and no scene
  goal.
- Dialogue rewritten many times inside a conversation the book does not
  need.
- Conversely: a draft where a whole subplot has been cut cleanly, leaving
  rough seams that a later pass can smooth.
- A manuscript whose first fifty pages read noticeably better than the rest —
  usually a sign of repeated line passes that never reached the middle.

## Common failure modes

- **Line-editing a doomed scene.** The most expensive error available in
  revision, and the most common.
- **The endless first chapter.** Polishing the opening repeatedly instead of
  finishing or restructuring the book.
- **Structural avoidance.** Line work as a way of feeling productive while
  postponing a change the author already knows is needed.
- **Premature line-editing during drafting.** Which slows drafting and
  produces material that is harder to cut, because it now represents effort.
- **Treating a reader's line complaint as a line problem.** "The prose got
  bogged down here" is often a scene problem reported at the level the reader
  noticed it.

## Questions for the author

- Is this scene definitely staying? If not, why is it being polished?
- What kind of change would the note I just received actually require —
  structural or sentence-level?
- Which pass am I on right now, and am I doing only that pass's work?
- Where has this manuscript received the most attention, and does that match
  where it needs the most?
- If the structure is not yet settled, what would it cost to leave the prose
  rough for one more pass?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Make a one-line-per-scene inventory of the draft and mark each scene keep,
   cut, or unsure. Do no line work in any scene marked cut or unsure.
2. Do a read-through with a pen you are not allowed to use except to mark
   where attention drifted, then diagnose those places before fixing
   anything.
3. Take one beta-reader note about prose and ask what structural cause could
   produce that symptom. Sometimes there is none; often there is.
4. Set an explicit pass order for the next revision and hold to it for one
   pass, even where a sentence is visibly annoying.

## When this advice does not apply

- Writers whose process genuinely requires clean sentences to think, and who
  cannot draft forward over rough prose. This is a real working style; its
  cost is that more polished material eventually gets cut.
- Serialized publishing, where chapters are released as written and
  structural revision of published chapters is not available. The pass order
  is then a planning question rather than a revision one, which changes the
  advice substantially.
- Short work, where the scales collapse and a single pass is often adequate.
- Final proofreading, which is neither of these and should follow both.

## Evidence and detection limits

This record is marked `practice`, and the mark is doing real work: it
describes an author's working process, not a property of the manuscript. A
coach cannot inspect a draft and determine what revision it has received.
Uneven polish between early and late chapters is sometimes visible, and even
that is weak evidence — a first chapter may simply have been written later.

Nothing here should be reported as an observation about a manuscript. The
coach's role with a practice record is to offer the distinction and let the
author recognize their own process in it, never to infer from the text that
the author has been revising in the wrong order.

## Original micro-examples

*Misdirected effort:* the tavern scene has been through eleven revisions. Its
dialogue is excellent. In the current outline the characters no longer go to
the tavern.

*Symptom reported at the wrong level:* three readers said chapter nine
"dragged." Chapter nine's prose is the cleanest in the book. Chapter nine is
where the protagonist has nothing to decide.

## Sources and confidence notes

Renni Browne and Dave King ([[src.book.browne-king-self-editing]]) supply the
editorial account of what manuscripts typically need and the case for
distinguishing levels of revision; Janet Burroway
([[src.book.burroway-writing-fiction]]) supplies the general revision
framework and the read-through practice; Donald Maass
([[src.book.maass-writing-breakout-novel]]) supplies the argument that
structural weakness cannot be resolved at the sentence level.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the handoff's second sense. The
distinction between developmental and line revision is standard and
independently supported; the specific claim that misdirected effort
concentrates in line-editing doomed scenes is a widely repeated editorial
observation rather than a measured finding, and is presented here as
practitioner consensus. The serialized-publishing exception is drawn from
this library's own genre research, since none of the three sources addresses
publishing chapters as they are written — a context where much of this
record's advice simply does not apply.
