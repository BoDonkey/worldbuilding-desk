## What it is

Protecting the intended reader experience means evaluating and applying
revision advice relative to what a specific manuscript is trying to
accomplish for its specific intended reader, rather than against a generic
standard of "good writing" that doesn't account for genre, register, or
authorial intent. The same piece of feedback — trim the description, slow
down here, raise the stakes, cut this subplot — can be exactly the right
call for one manuscript and actively wrong for another with a different
promise, and the difference isn't always obvious from the feedback alone.
This is less a single technique than a stance a coach or reviewer should
hold throughout revision: understand what the book is trying to be before
recommending changes to how well it's being it.

## Why readers may care

A manuscript revised against a generic standard rather than its own
intended promise risks losing what made it distinctive in the first place —
sanding a deliberately dense, atmospheric opening down to match
"industry-standard pacing" can strip a literary novel of its actual appeal;
adding unwanted interiority to a fast, plot-forward adventure can slow it
in ways its intended readers never wanted. This pattern exists to name the
risk directly: revision feedback that's technically sound in the abstract
can still be wrong for a specific book.

## Common forms and variants

- **Genre-calibrated feedback**, evaluating pacing, description density,
  and structural choices against the conventions of the manuscript's
  actual genre and subgenre, not a generic standard.
- **Promise-calibrated feedback**, checking proposed changes against what
  the manuscript's own opening has promised (closely related to the
  promise-consistency pattern), rather than an external ideal.
- **Author-intent clarification**, explicitly asking what a specific choice
  is meant to accomplish before recommending it be changed, distinguishing
  deliberate craft choices from unintentional gaps.
- **Audience-calibrated feedback**, accounting for who the manuscript is
  actually written for — a middle-grade adventure and a literary novel for
  adults have different, equally legitimate standards for pacing,
  complexity, and content.

## What it can look like in a revision process

- A piece of feedback that names the manuscript's own genre or promise
  explicitly before recommending a change, rather than applying a
  one-size-fits-all standard.
- A revision note distinguishing "this doesn't work for what your book is
  trying to do" from "this doesn't work, full stop" — the two require very
  different responses from an author.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: revision feedback that would apply
  identically regardless of the manuscript's genre, audience, or stated
  intent, suggesting it wasn't actually calibrated to this specific book.

## Common failure modes

- **Generic feedback applied without calibration**, recommending changes
  that would improve an abstract "well-written novel" without checking
  whether they'd improve this particular book's actual promise.
- **Mistaking a deliberate choice for an oversight**, recommending a
  change to something the author did intentionally, without first asking
  why it's there.
- **Over-indexing on personal taste**, where a reviewer's own genre
  preferences shape feedback more than the manuscript's own stated or
  implied intentions do.

## Questions for the author

- Does this piece of feedback account for what this specific manuscript is
  trying to be, or would it apply the same way to any manuscript?
- Is a proposed change addressing something that isn't working, or
  something that's working differently than a generic standard would
  expect?
- Before changing a specific choice, has its purpose been considered — is
  it possible this was deliberate?
- Would this manuscript's intended reader actually want the proposed
  change, given what the book has promised them so far?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Before applying a piece of revision feedback, state explicitly what
   genre, promise, and intended reader the manuscript is written for, and
   check the feedback against that statement.
2. For a proposed change to something that might be intentional, ask why
   the choice was made before recommending it be altered.
3. Distinguish, in writing, feedback that says "this doesn't achieve what
   you're going for" from feedback that says "what you're going for isn't
   working for me personally" — treat the two differently.
4. When multiple pieces of feedback conflict, check each against the
   manuscript's own stated promise rather than trying to satisfy all of
   them simultaneously.

## When this advice does not apply

- Genuine craft errors (inconsistent POV, broken causality, unintentional
  continuity errors) aren't relative to intended reader experience in the
  same way — this pattern applies most directly to calibration-sensitive
  choices like pacing, density, and tone, not to outright mistakes.
- A manuscript still finding its own intended promise during early drafting
  may not yet have a stable standard to calibrate feedback against; this
  pattern is most useful once a manuscript's direction is reasonably
  settled.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a piece of feedback is properly calibrated to a manuscript's
intended promise, genre, and audience requires understanding that promise
in the first place — a judgment that depends on interpreting the
manuscript holistically and remains model-assisted; a coach cannot verify
"author intent" from text alone and should treat this as a question to ask
rather than an assumption to make.

## Original micro-examples

*Uncalibrated feedback:* A reviewer recommends cutting a fast-paced
adventure novel's brief, functional descriptions to add more sensory depth,
applying a standard suited to literary fiction without checking whether the
adventure novel's intended readers value pace over density.

*Calibrated feedback:* The same reviewer instead first identifies that the
manuscript is a tightly plotted adventure aimed at readers who prioritize
momentum, and recommends instead that one specific description — at the
story's most significant reveal — be expanded, since even a pace-forward
book can afford to slow down at its most important moment.
