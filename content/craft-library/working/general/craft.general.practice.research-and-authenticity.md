## What it is

Almost every novel requires the author to write about something they have not
done — a trade, an injury, a country, a period, a life — and about people
whose experience differs from theirs. This record is about how that gets
done: what research is for, what it cannot supply, and how to tell when a
draft is running on assumption.

It is a `practice` record. It describes an author's working process, not a
detectable property of a manuscript.

## Why readers may care

Concrete, correct detail is one of the cheapest sources of authority in
prose. A reader who recognizes that the author knows how a forge behaves, or
how a hospital shift runs, extends credit to everything else in the book. The
reverse is also true and less forgiving: a reader with direct knowledge of
the subject will notice an error immediately, and their trust does not fully
recover.

For writing about people whose lives differ from the author's, the stake is
different. Errors there are not only credibility failures — they can
misrepresent people who have no say in the portrayal, and readers who share
that experience are the ones who bear it.

## Common forms and variants

- **Procedural research** — how a thing works: a job, a technology, a
  process. Usually the easiest to do and the most immediately visible.
- **Sensory research** — what a thing is like: sound, smell, physical toll.
  Often what makes a scene land, and rarely found in reference material.
- **Lived-experience research** — talking to people who know, which supplies
  what documents do not and carries its own obligations.
- **Sensitivity reading** — a reader with relevant experience reading for
  representation rather than craft.
- **Deliberate invention** — knowing the real answer and departing from it on
  purpose, which is a legitimate choice and different from not knowing.

## What it can look like in an author's process

- A scene that gets specific in a way that could not have been guessed.
- Research notes far exceeding what appears on the page — usually the right
  ratio.
- Conversely: a profession rendered entirely in the terms a film would use.
- A character whose difference from the author is signalled but never has any
  consequence in how they experience a scene.

## Common failure modes

- **The research dump.** Effort spent visibly, because it was expensive to
  acquire. The reader does not need to know what it cost.
- **Research as substitute for scene.** Accurate detail arranged where a
  dramatized moment should be.
- **Getting the facts and missing the texture.** Procedurally correct,
  sensorially blank.
- **Assumed knowledge.** The subject the author is confident about and has
  not checked, which is where errors concentrate — the same pattern this
  library found in its own sourcing.
- **Difference as decoration.** A character's background stated and never
  affecting anything they perceive or decide.

## Questions for the author

- What in this scene do I actually know, and what am I assuming?
- What would someone who does this for a living notice first?
- Which details here could only come from the real thing?
- Who could read this and tell me what I have got wrong — and have I asked?
- Where am I departing from the real answer on purpose, and is that visible
  as a choice?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Mark every factual assertion in a scene as *known*, *checked*, or
   *assumed*. The assumed list is the research task.
2. Find one sensory detail that reference material would not give you, and
   get it from a person or a primary account.
3. Cut the most expensively researched paragraph and see whether the scene
   survives. It usually does, and reads better.
4. For a character whose experience differs from yours, write a paragraph of
   an ordinary moment — not a plot moment — and ask whether it is specific or
   generic.

## When this advice does not apply

- Secondary-world fantasy where the subject is invented; internal consistency
  replaces accuracy, though sensory texture still has to come from somewhere
  real.
- Deliberately stylized or absurdist modes where accuracy is not the contract.
- Fast-turnaround serial drafting, where an author may knowingly accept
  approximation to keep a schedule — a real trade, best made consciously and
  fixed in revision where it matters.
- Cases where the author *is* writing from experience, and research advice
  would displace something they already know better than any source.

## Evidence and detection limits

Nothing here is detectable, hence the `practice` mark. A coach cannot verify
facts about the world from inside a manuscript, cannot tell researched detail
from invented detail, and must not attempt to judge whether a portrayal of
someone's experience is accurate — it has no standing to make that call and
would be guessing from surface features.

What a coach can legitimately do is notice where a passage becomes
noticeably less specific than its neighbours and ask about it, and it can
offer the questions above when an author raises the subject. It should not
volunteer opinions about representation from textual signals.

## Original micro-examples

*Assumed:* "The forge was hot and loud, and the smith hammered the glowing
metal into shape."

*Known:* "He worked in silence for the first hour, because the fire needed
watching more than the iron did, and the noise people imagined — the ringing
— was only the last few minutes of anything."

## Sources and confidence notes

Janet Burroway ([[src.book.burroway-writing-fiction]]) supplies the general
principle about concrete, significant detail; John Gardner
([[src.book.gardner-art-of-fiction]]) supplies the treatment of authority in
fiction and how it is lost. Neither addresses research practice or writing
across difference directly, so the substance of this record — the taxonomy,
the known/checked/assumed diagnostic, the failure modes, the boundaries on
what a coach may say about representation — is this drafting pass's synthesis
([[src.synthesis.craft-library-editorial]]).

`source_confidence` is `limited` accordingly. This is a topic with a real
literature: writing craft has an extensive practical discussion of research
method, and representation across difference has a serious body of writing by
authors and editors who have thought about it far longer than this pass has.
A later pass should bring both in, and the representation material in
particular should be sourced to people writing from the relevant experience
rather than synthesized. Until then, this record is deliberately cautious
about that half of its subject.
