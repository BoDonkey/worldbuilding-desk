## What it is

A scene's entry and exit points are the moments an author chooses to begin
narrating and to stop, which don't need to match where the scene's
underlying events actually start and finish. A scene can be entered well
before its central conflict begins (showing full arrival, small talk, and
setup) or well into it (opening mid-conversation, mid-action); it can be
exited once every loose end is settled, or the moment its central turn
lands, leaving the aftermath to the reader's imagination or the next
scene's opening. The common advice to "start late and leave early" names a
default bias — enter as close to the point of interest as orientation
allows, exit as soon as the scene's essential work is done — without
making it an absolute rule for every scene.

## Why readers may care

Scenes that include their full run-up and full aftermath can feel padded,
asking the reader to sit through transitional material that doesn't carry
the scene's actual interest. Scenes that enter close to their point of
tension and exit at or near their turn tend to feel tighter and more
propulsive, because the reader's attention is directed at the material that
matters most. This is a different lever from narrative summary versus
scene — it's not about whether to dramatize an event at all, but about how
much of a dramatized event's edges to actually show once the decision to
dramatize it has been made.

## Common forms and variants

- **Late entry**, opening a scene close to or inside its central action or
  conflict, with necessary orientation supplied economically rather than
  through a full run-up.
- **Early exit**, ending a scene at or shortly after its central turn,
  before every loose end (goodbyes, logistics, full emotional processing)
  is resolved on the page.
- **Full-coverage scenes**, including complete arrival, setup, and
  resolution — appropriate when the transitional material itself carries
  meaning, or when a slower pace serves the moment.
- **Cross-cut entry/exit**, where a scene is entered or exited via a jump
  cut from or to a different scene, compressing transitional time
  implicitly rather than narrating it.

## What it can look like on the page

- A scene opening mid-action or mid-conversation, with context supplied
  through a few economical lines rather than a full establishing sequence.
- A scene ending at its emotional or plot turn, with the immediate
  aftermath left implicit or deferred to the next scene.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a scene that includes several paragraphs
  of arrival, greeting, and settling in before its actual point of interest
  begins, or lingers well past its central turn resolving minor,
  non-essential loose ends.

## Common failure modes

- **Full run-up on every scene**, including transitional material
  (traveling to a location, settling in, small talk) as a default habit
  rather than a deliberate choice, slowing pacing across the manuscript.
- **Exiting too early**, cutting a scene before its essential turn has
  actually landed clearly, leaving the reader confused about what happened
  rather than intrigued by what's left implicit.
- **Uniform application**, applying "start late, leave early" so
  consistently that scenes needing a slower approach (an intentional
  breather, a scene where the transitional material itself matters) get cut
  the same way as ones that don't.

## Questions for the author

- Could this scene begin later — closer to its point of actual interest —
  without losing necessary orientation?
- Could this scene end earlier — at or near its central turn — without
  leaving the reader confused about what happened?
- Is a scene's run-up or aftermath included because it does real narrative
  work, or out of habit?
- Are there consecutive scenes in the manuscript that all follow the same
  entry/exit pattern, and would varying it sharpen the ones that matter
  most?

## Revision or design experiments

1. For a scene that feels slow to start, identify the earliest point that
   still gives the reader necessary orientation and cut everything before
   it.
2. For a scene that lingers, identify the moment its central turn lands and
   test cutting everything after it, moving any essential remaining
   information to the next scene's opening.
3. Audit several consecutive scenes for entry/exit pattern uniformity; vary
   the treatment for the scenes carrying the most narrative weight.
4. For a scene where the transitional material seems worth keeping, name
   specifically what work it's doing (mood, characterization, pacing
   breather) to confirm the choice is deliberate.

## When this advice does not apply

- Scenes where the transitional material itself is the point (a quiet
  arrival that reveals character, an ending's aftermath that needs space to
  land emotionally) should be given the room they need, not trimmed by
  default.
- Very short or fragmentary scene structures may not have a meaningful
  "full coverage" version to compare against.
- Some literary styles deliberately linger on transitional or liminal
  moments as part of their voice and pacing philosophy.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a scene's entry or exit point serves its content, or merely follows
habit, requires reading and interpreting the prose and its surrounding
context — a judgment no structured data substitutes for. Scene length is
directly observable from raw text.

## Original micro-examples

*Full run-up:* A scene opens with a character waking, dressing, eating
breakfast, and walking to a meeting before the meeting's actual
conflict — the scene's point of interest — finally begins on the third
page.

*Late entry:* The same scene instead opens on the meeting's first
contentious line of dialogue, with the character's morning routine omitted
entirely; any necessary context arrives economically, woven into the
conversation itself.
