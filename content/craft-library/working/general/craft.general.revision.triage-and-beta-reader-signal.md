## What it is

Revision triage is the practice of sorting what a manuscript needs by scale
and order rather than addressing every issue as it's noticed: developmental
concerns (structure, character arc, pacing at the manuscript level) usually
need to be settled before line-level concerns (word choice, sentence
rhythm, individual dialogue) are worth polishing, since developmental
changes can make line-level work on an affected passage moot. Beta-reader
signal is the related practice of separating what a reader reports
experiencing (a symptom — "I got bored around chapter ten," "I didn't
believe the romance") from what they diagnose as the cause, since readers
are reliable narrators of their own experience but not always reliable
diagnosticians of why they had it.

## Why readers may care

This pattern serves the author's process rather than directly shaping
reader experience, but it has an indirect and significant effect: a
manuscript revised out of order (polishing prose before structure is
settled, or restructuring based on a misdiagnosed symptom) tends to need
more revision passes, cost more author time and energy, and risk the kind
of burnout symptoms covered in the related pattern — all of which eventually
surface in what readers experience, even though the practice itself happens
off the page.

## Common forms and variants

- **Developmental-first triage**, addressing structure, arc, and pacing
  concerns before line-level polish, the most commonly recommended default
  order.
- **Symptom-and-cause separation**, treating a beta reader's reported
  experience ("I got bored," "I didn't buy the reconciliation") as reliable
  data while treating their proposed fix ("you should cut chapter ten," "add
  a scene where they talk it out") as one hypothesis among several possible
  causes.
- **Pattern-across-readers triage**, weighting feedback that recurs across
  multiple independent readers more heavily than a single reader's specific
  reaction, which may reflect that reader's individual taste rather than a
  structural issue.
- **Targeted beta requests**, asking readers to focus feedback on a specific
  concern (pacing, a particular character, whether a twist lands) rather
  than open-ended reaction, trading breadth for depth on a known concern.

## What it can look like in an author's process

- A structural revision pass completed before a line-editing pass begins,
  rather than the two happening simultaneously on the same draft.
- Beta feedback organized by what readers experienced, with proposed causes
  tracked separately and checked against the author's own read of the
  manuscript before being acted on.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: line-editing a chapter that later gets
  cut or substantially restructured, or accepting a reader's proposed fix
  without checking whether it addresses the actual symptom they reported.

## Common failure modes

- **Line-editing before structure is settled**, wasting effort on passages
  that later change or disappear.
- **Treating a single reader's proposed cause as settled fact**, especially
  when it isn't corroborated by other readers or the author's own
  independent assessment.
- **Ignoring a recurring symptom because the proposed fixes seem wrong**,
  discarding valid signal (readers getting bored at the same point) because
  the accompanying diagnosis doesn't ring true, rather than looking for a
  different cause.
- **Revising reactively to every piece of feedback**, without triaging by
  scale or recurrence, which can fragment a manuscript's voice and
  coherence.

## Questions for the author

- Is structural revision (plot, arc, pacing) settled before investing in
  line-level polish on the same material?
- Does a piece of beta feedback recur across multiple independent readers,
  or is it a single reader's individual reaction?
- Is a reader's reported experience being treated as reliable while their
  proposed cause is checked against the author's own read of the
  manuscript, or is the proposed fix being accepted uncritically?
- Has a valid, recurring symptom been dismissed because the accompanying
  diagnosis didn't feel right, without looking for an alternative cause?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Before line-editing, confirm the manuscript's structure is stable enough
   that the chapter being edited won't be cut or substantially reordered.
2. For a piece of beta feedback, separate the reported experience from the
   proposed cause in writing, and generate at least one alternative
   hypothesis for the cause before revising.
3. Collect feedback from several readers on the same draft and look for
   symptoms that recur, weighting those over single-reader reactions.
4. If a recurring symptom's proposed fixes all seem wrong, revisit the
   affected section fresh and look for a cause none of the readers named.

## When this advice does not apply

- Very short revision cycles (a quick polish pass with no structural
  concerns) may not need the full developmental-first sequencing this
  pattern describes.
- Some authors' processes genuinely work better with simultaneous
  structural and line-level revision; this is a common default recommendation,
  not a universal requirement.
- A single trusted reader's feedback (an experienced editor, a
  longtime collaborator) may reasonably be weighted more heavily than the
  "multiple readers" corroboration standard would otherwise suggest.

## Evidence and detection limits

This is primarily a practice pattern concerning an author's revision
process, not something a manuscript's current state can directly reveal;
whether a specific structural issue is genuinely settled, or whether a
piece of feedback has been triaged appropriately, is not verifiable from
text alone. A coach may teach this practice and, where a project explicitly
tracks revision-pass history, could report that history, but cannot verify
an author's process discipline from the manuscript itself.

## Original micro-examples

*Misdiagnosed feedback:* Several beta readers report losing interest around
chapter ten; readers who diagnose a cause disagree (too much description,
not enough dialogue, an unlikeable side character), and the author,
confused by the conflicting diagnoses, makes no change — missing that the
recurring symptom itself (attention drops at that specific point) is valid
signal regardless of the conflicting proposed causes.

*Triaged appropriately:* The same author instead treats the recurring
symptom as reliable and investigates chapter ten independently, discovering
the actual cause is a stalled subplot with no forward motion at that
point — a diagnosis none of the readers happened to name, but one the
symptom pattern pointed toward.
