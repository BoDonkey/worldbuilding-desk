## What it is

A setup is a detail, ability, relationship, or piece of information planted
earlier in a story specifically so that a later payoff feels earned rather
than arbitrary. The payoff doesn't need to be predictable in advance — the
best setups are often invisible on a first read and only recognizable in
hindsight — but it does need to survive the reader mentally rewinding and
asking "was this actually possible, given what I knew?"

## Why readers may care

An unearned payoff — a solution, twist, or rescue that depends on
information or ability the story never established — reads as a violation of
the reader's trust that the story is playing fair, even when it isn't
technically impossible within the world. A well-planted payoff produces the
opposite feeling: satisfaction at recognizing, in hindsight, that the story
was building toward this the whole time.

## Common forms and variants

- **Plot-mechanical setups**, where an object, ability, or piece of
  information is introduced early and becomes load-bearing later (the
  classic "Chekhov's gun" formulation).
- **Character setups**, where an established trait, skill, or flaw becomes
  the specific lever a later scene depends on.
- **Thematic setups**, where an early statement or image resonates with a
  later one, reinforcing meaning rather than mechanics.
- **Red herrings**, a deliberate variant where a setup appears to point
  toward one payoff and is redirected toward another — effective when
  the misdirection still holds up on a reread, weak when it depends on
  withheld information the reader couldn't have had.

## What it can look like on the page

- An object, line, or ability introduced without immediate consequence,
  later becoming central to resolving a conflict.
- A late-story solution that a reader could trace back to an earlier,
  specific moment if asked to find its origin.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a resolution that depends on information,
  ability, or a coincidence introduced for the first time in the same scene
  it's needed.

## Common failure modes

- **The unplanted payoff** ("deus ex machina" in its plot-mechanical form),
  where a solution arrives from outside anything the story established.
- **The buried setup**, planted so subtly or so early that it fails to
  register even in hindsight, making the payoff feel unearned despite
  technically being planted.
- **The telegraphed setup**, planted so heavily that the payoff becomes
  predictable well before it should be, draining tension from the moment it
  finally lands.

## Questions for the author

- If a reader paused at this payoff and asked "how did the story make this
  possible," could you point to an earlier, specific moment that answers it?
- Is a given setup subtle enough to reward a careful reader without being so
  hidden that no reader will ever consciously connect it?
- Are there any "guns on the mantelpiece" — established elements that raise
  an expectation of use — that never fire, and if so, is that absence
  deliberate?
- For a twist or reveal, does it hold up on a hypothetical reread, or does it
  depend on information the reader genuinely couldn't have had?

## Revision or design experiments

1. For each major late-story payoff, trace backward to find (or add) its
   setup; if none exists, either plant one in revision or reconsider the
   payoff.
2. List established elements (objects, abilities, relationships) introduced
   with apparent significance and check whether each pays off or is
   deliberately left as a red herring or texture.
3. Test a major reveal against a "fair play" read: could an attentive reader
   have pieced it together with the information given, even if they
   probably wouldn't have?
4. For a setup that feels too obvious, try moving it earlier and burying it
   in a scene with other narrative business competing for attention.

## When this advice does not apply

- Stories built around genuine surprise as their mode (certain literary or
  absurdist traditions) may deliberately violate fair-play setup
  conventions as part of their effect.
- A payoff building on established genre convention rather than an in-text
  setup (a reader's genre knowledge supplying the expectation) doesn't
  necessarily need its own explicit textual setup.
- Early volumes of a planned series may plant setups whose payoff is
  intentionally deferred well beyond the current book.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a specific payoff is adequately set up requires tracing a causal
and informational chain through the manuscript, an interpretive task with
no deterministic substitute; a coach could shortlist earlier mentions of a
payoff's key elements as candidate evidence where an explicit search is
possible, but confirming that a setup "counts" as adequate requires human or
model judgment, not verification.

## Original micro-examples

*Unplanted payoff:* A character escapes a locked room in the story's climax
by picking the lock with a skill never mentioned before that scene.

*Planted payoff:* An earlier chapter shows the same character idly picking
at a stuck drawer latch with a bent hairpin, framed as an incidental habit;
the climax's lock-picking recalls that habit specifically, so the reader
recognizes the setup in hindsight.

## Sources and confidence notes

The setup/payoff and "fair play" framing draws on Robert McKee's writing
about planting and payoff as a component of causal story structure

texts. `source_confidence` is `mixed` because the red-herring and
