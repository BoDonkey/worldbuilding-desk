## What it is

A sequel (in the classic structural sense, unrelated to "sequel" meaning a
follow-up book) is the unit that follows a scene's disaster: a character's
emotional reaction to what just happened, a dilemma weighed among imperfect
options, and a decision that becomes the next scene's goal. Where a scene is
built on external action and conflict, a sequel is built on internal
processing — it's the connective tissue that keeps a string of disasters
from reading as a character simply being battered by plot with no interiority
in between.

## Why readers may care

A story that moves from disaster to disaster with no reflection can feel
relentless in a way that numbs rather than thrills — readers stop being able
to track why a character makes their next move, because they never see the
character actually choose it. A sequel gives the reader access to the
character's reasoning and lets the next goal feel like a decision rather than
a random new problem the author introduced.

## Common forms and variants

- **Full reaction-dilemma-decision**, played out explicitly across a page or
  more: an emotional beat, a weighed choice among bad options, a committed
  decision.
- **Compressed sequel**, handled in a paragraph or even a sentence, common in
  fast-paced or serialized fiction where a full sequel would slow momentum
  too much.
- **Interleaved sequel**, woven into the opening of the next scene rather
  than standing alone, so the reaction and decision happen on the move.
- **Deferred sequel**, where the emotional reaction is intentionally
  withheld and paid off later — used for characters who compartmentalize, but
  risky if the deferral reads as the author forgetting rather than the
  character choosing.

## What it can look like on the page

- A passage of interiority following a disaster, naming or implying the
  character's emotional response before the next action begins.
- A visible weighing of options, even briefly, before a character commits to
  a course of action.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: consecutive disasters with the character
  moving directly into the next goal with no visible reaction or reasoning.

## Common failure modes

- **Omitting the sequel entirely** in fast-paced fiction, leaving reader
  disorientation about why the character does what they do next.
- **Over-length sequels** that stall momentum by lingering on reaction and
  dilemma far longer than the preceding disaster's weight justifies.
- **Decisions that don't follow from the dilemma** — the character reacts,
  weighs options, and then chooses something the reflection didn't actually
  point toward.

## Questions for the author

- After this disaster, does the reader get any access to how the character
  feels about it before the next goal launches?
- Is the length of this sequel proportional to the weight of what just
  happened?
- Does the character's next decision follow logically from the dilemma just
  weighed, or does it feel like a fresh, disconnected choice?
- In a fast-moving stretch, would a single compressed line of reaction be
  enough to keep the reader oriented, even without a full sequel?

## Revision or design experiments

1. After a major disaster, add one line of explicit emotional reaction if
   none currently exists, and see whether the next scene's goal reads more
   motivated.
2. For a sequel that feels bloated, try compressing the dilemma to its
   essential tension and cutting restated options.
3. Check that a character's next decision can be traced back to the specific
   dilemma just weighed; if not, either adjust the dilemma or the decision.
4. In a high-tempo action stretch, try an interleaved sequel (folded into the
   next scene's opening beat) instead of a standalone pause, to preserve
   momentum while still grounding the choice.

## When this advice does not apply

- Fast, adrenaline-driven sequences (a chase, a battle) can legitimately
  compress or omit full sequels to preserve pace, provided the reader isn't
  left unable to track motivation.
- A character written as deliberately unreflective, or a story working in a
  close, externally-focused POV, may minimize interiority as a stylistic
  choice rather than an oversight.
- Very short scenes may not need a distinct sequel at all if the next scene's
  goal is self-evident from context.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a sequel is present, proportionate, and logically connected to the
following decision requires reading and interpreting prose; there is no
deterministic substitute. A coach can point to the passage following a
disaster as candidate evidence but cannot conclude, from structured data
alone, whether the emotional beat lands.

## Original micro-examples

*Missing sequel:* A character's mentor is killed on page forty. Page
forty-one opens on the character already three streets away, executing a
plan, with no line acknowledging the loss.

*Present sequel:* The same death is followed by a paragraph in which the
character sits with the body for one line too long, weighs whether to pursue
the killer alone or return for allies, and chooses to go alone specifically
because waiting feels like a second betrayal — a decision the reflection
visibly produces.
