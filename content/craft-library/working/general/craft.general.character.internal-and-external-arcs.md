## What it is

The external arc tracks what changes in a character's circumstances,
relationships, and world through the plot's events — status, resources,
allies, physical stakes resolved or not. The internal arc tracks what
changes within the character themselves — belief, self-understanding,
values, capacity to trust or love or forgive. The two arcs are analytically
separable, and a manuscript can have a strong external arc with a flat
internal one (a character wins the war but is exactly the same person at the
end) or the reverse (deep internal change with no external consequence to
show for it). The strongest, most widely taught version of this pattern has
the two arcs intersect: the internal change is what makes the external
resolution possible, or the external resolution is what forces the internal
change.

## Why readers may care

A story with a well-developed external arc and no internal one can feel
eventful but hollow — things happened, but the protagonist doesn't feel
different for having lived through them. A story with a well-developed
internal arc and no external correlate can feel static or overly
introspective, since the reader has no plot-level evidence the internal
change actually mattered to anything. When the two arcs intersect — the
character's growth is what lets them finally solve the external problem, or
solving the external problem is what finally forces the growth — both arcs
reinforce each other and the ending lands with more weight than either could
produce alone.

## Common forms and variants

- **Intersecting arcs**, where the internal change directly enables the
  external resolution (the character can only win once they've learned to
  trust an ally) — the most commonly taught and often most satisfying
  form.
- **Parallel arcs**, where internal and external change occur across the
  same story without directly causing each other — workable, but risks
  feeling like two stories running side by side rather than one.
- **Inverted-cost arcs**, where achieving the external goal costs the
  internal growth (a character wins by becoming someone worse), a
  legitimate tragic or morally complex structure.
- **Static-external, dynamic-internal**, where the external situation barely
  changes but the character's relationship to it transforms completely —
  common in literary and slice-of-life registers.
- **Static-internal, dynamic-external**, where the character remains
  essentially the same while circumstances around them change dramatically
  — a legitimate structure for plot-forward genres, discussed further in the
  related want/need record's note on static-want stories.

## What it can look like on the page

- A climax or major turning point where a character's internal shift
  (a belief changed, a fear overcome) is the specific thing that makes the
  external resolution possible.
- An external victory or resolution followed by a scene showing the
  character is measurably different than they were at the story's start.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a character who achieves everything the
  external plot demanded while their internal narration, values, and
  relationships remain identical to the opening chapters.

## Common failure modes

- **The unchanged victor**, where a character achieves total external
  success with no accompanying internal change, leaving the ending feeling
  hollow despite plot resolution.
- **The unearned internal shift**, where a character's stated growth isn't
  connected to any external event or choice that would plausibly cause it.
- **Two disconnected stories**, where internal and external arcs develop
  independently with no scene where they intersect or affect each other.

## Questions for the author

- What does this character believe or value at the story's start, and is it
  different by the end? If so, what specific event or choice caused the
  shift?
- Is there a moment where the character's internal change is what makes the
  external resolution possible, or are the two arcs running independently?
- If the external arc were removed, would the internal arc still make sense
  as a story? If the internal arc were removed, would the external arc still
  land with the same weight?
- For a character who stays essentially the same throughout, is that
  stasis the point (a plot-forward structure, a tragedy of unchangeability),
  or an underdeveloped internal arc?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write one sentence describing the character's internal state at the
   start and one for the end; if they're identical, decide whether that's
   intentional.
2. Find the specific scene where internal change and external plot
   intersect; if none exists, look for the climax or a major turning point
   where one could be added.
3. For a character whose victory feels hollow, add a small scene
   demonstrating internal change — not necessarily happiness, just
   difference from who they were.
4. For an inverted-cost arc, make sure the cost of the external win is
   dramatized specifically, not just implied.

## When this advice does not apply

- Plot-forward genres (heist, procedural, some adventure structures) can
  legitimately keep the internal arc minimal, with satisfaction coming from
  execution rather than transformation.
- Ensemble stories may distribute internal and external arcs across several
  characters rather than requiring every character to carry both.
- A story explicitly about resistance to change (a tragedy of stasis) may
  intentionally show a character whose external circumstances shift while
  they remain unable or unwilling to change internally, with that failure
  as the point.

## Evidence and detection limits

Identifying a character's internal arc, and whether it intersects with the
external plot, requires reading and interpreting characterization,
interiority, and causal structure across the whole manuscript — a judgment
no structured data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Disconnected arcs:* A protagonist wins a kingdom (external arc complete)
while an internal narration thread about learning to trust others resolves
in a separate scene, triggered by an unrelated minor event with no
connection to the external victory.

*Intersecting arcs:* The same protagonist instead wins the kingdom
specifically because they finally trust an advisor's warning they would have
dismissed earlier in the story — the internal shift (learning to trust) is
the direct cause of the external resolution (avoiding a fatal ambush).

## Sources and confidence notes

The internal/external arc distinction and their intersection as a strong
structural form draws on Lisa Cron's contemporary craft writing
([[src.book.cron-story-genius]]) and John Truby's structural writing on
character revelation and moral argument
([[src.book.truby-anatomy-of-story]]); both established, widely taught
texts, supporting a `high` confidence rating for the core principle, while
its application to any specific manuscript remains model-assisted.
