## What it is

A resolution is earned when it follows causally from elements the story has
already established, dramatized, and given the reader reason to track —
character growth that was shown developing, a capability that was set up
earlier, a relationship whose trust was built on the page. A resolution
feels unearned when it depends on something introduced at or near the
story's end specifically to make the ending work: a new power, a
convenient coincidence, an ally who appears exactly when needed with no
prior establishment, or a character change asserted rather than shown
happening. This pattern sits close to setup/payoff and causal escalation
but focuses specifically on the ending's relationship to everything that
preceded it, as a distinct diagnostic question worth asking on its own.

## Why readers may care

An earned resolution gives a reader the satisfaction of having watched the
machinery that produces the ending being built throughout the story — the
climax feels like the culmination of what came before, not a separate event
tacked onto it. An unearned resolution, even one that's exciting in the
moment, tends to produce a specific kind of reader dissatisfaction once the
excitement fades: the sense that the story didn't actually solve its own
problem, it introduced a new element that solved the problem for it.

## Common forms and variants

- **Causally earned resolution**, where the ending traces directly to
  established character growth, relationships, and capabilities.
- **Cost-earned resolution**, where the ending's positive outcome is
  balanced by a dramatized cost consistent with what the story has been
  building toward, rather than arriving as an unqualified, costless win.
- **Deliberately unearned or ironic resolution**, where a story
  intentionally resolves through an outside force or coincidence as
  commentary (on fate, on genre convention, on the limits of individual
  agency) — a legitimate choice when the artificiality itself is the point,
  distinct from an unintentional shortfall.
- **Partially earned resolution**, common and often realistic, where most
  of the ending traces to established elements but a smaller, plausible
  piece of good or bad fortune plays a supporting role — the line between
  this and an unearned resolution is largely about proportion and
  plausibility.

## What it can look like on the page

- A climax whose resolution depends specifically on a capability, insight,
  or relationship the reader watched develop over the course of the story.
- A costly or bittersweet element in an otherwise triumphant ending, that
  traces to sacrifices or risks established earlier.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a resolution that depends on a new
  character, capability, or coincidence introduced within the story's final
  stretch specifically to make the ending work.

## Common failure modes

- **The deus ex machina**, resolving the central conflict through an
  outside force with no prior establishment — the most direct version of
  this failure.
- **Unearned internal change**, where a character's resolution-enabling
  growth (finally trusting someone, finally being brave) is asserted in the
  climax without having been shown developing across the story.
- **A costless triumph after a story that implied real stakes**, where the
  tone and stakes established earlier suggest the ending should cost
  something, but it doesn't, creating a mismatch between promise and
  delivery.

## Questions for the author

- Does this story's resolution trace to something specific the reader
  watched develop — a capability, a relationship, a piece of established
  growth?
- Is there an element in the climax (a character, a capability, a
  coincidence) introduced too close to the ending to have been
  anticipated?
- If the resolution is a clean, costless win, does that match the stakes
  the rest of the story established, or does it undercut them?
- If the resolution is intentionally coincidental or ironic, is that
  artificiality legible as a deliberate choice rather than an oversight?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Trace the resolution's key elements backward and confirm each one was
   established earlier in the manuscript, not introduced at the end.
2. For an element that appears only in the climax, either plant it earlier
   or find a way to resolve the conflict using something already
   established.
3. If the ending feels costless against a story that implied real stakes,
   consider adding a dramatized cost consistent with what's been built.
4. For a partially earned resolution, check the proportion of established
   versus fortunate elements and adjust if fortune is doing more work than
   the story's stakes can support.

## When this advice does not apply

- A story deliberately using an ironic, artificial, or coincidental
  resolution as commentary (on genre convention, on the limits of agency,
  on fate) is using the unearned quality intentionally, provided that
  intent is legible.
- Very short or fable-like forms may use a more schematic, less causally
  developed resolution as part of their intended mode.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a resolution's key elements were adequately established earlier in
the manuscript requires tracing the story's full causal chain — a judgment
no structured data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Unearned resolution:* A protagonist defeats the antagonist in the climax
using a magical ability that first appears in that same scene, with no
earlier mention or development.

*Earned resolution:* The same climax instead uses an ability the
protagonist struggled to control in an earlier chapter, finally mastered
through a specific dramatized breakthrough — the reader watched the tool
being built before it was used to solve the final problem.

## Sources and confidence notes

The earned-resolution principle is foundational in both Robert McKee's
writing on causal story structure ([[src.book.mckee-story]]) and John
Truby's structural writing on climax and revelation
([[src.book.truby-anatomy-of-story]]); both long-established, widely
taught texts, supporting a `high` confidence rating for the core
principle, while its application to any specific manuscript remains
model-assisted.
