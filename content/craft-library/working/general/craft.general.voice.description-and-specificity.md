## What it is

Specificity in description means choosing the particular, telling detail
over the generic category label — "a chipped enamel mug with a faded rooster
on the side" rather than "a mug," or a character's exact gesture rather than
"she looked upset." The principle isn't that more description is better; a
single well-chosen specific detail often does more work, and does it more
efficiently, than several generic ones stacked together. Specificity also
extends beyond physical description to characterization, dialogue tags, and
world detail — anywhere a generic placeholder could be replaced with
something particular to this story's specific world and characters.

## Why readers may care

Generic description asks a reader to fill in their own mental image from a
category ("a forest," "a sad expression"), which is efficient but forgettable
— readers report that unspecific prose tends to blur together across a
manuscript. A specific detail gives the reader something particular to
picture and, often, to feel — a chipped enamel mug carries implications
(age, use, a story) that "a mug" doesn't, without needing to state those
implications directly.

## Common forms and variants

- **Sensory specificity**, grounding description in a particular sight,
  sound, smell, texture, or taste rather than a general category.
- **Behavioral specificity**, replacing generic emotional labels ("she was
  nervous") with a specific physical or behavioral detail that implies the
  emotion without naming it.
- **World-specific detail**, grounding a setting in details particular to
  this story's world rather than generic genre furniture (a specific,
  invented local custom rather than "a market town").
- **Selective specificity**, choosing one or two telling details rather than
  exhaustively describing everything in a scene — specificity is not the
  same as thoroughness, and can be undermined by over-description as easily
  as by vagueness.

## What it can look like on the page

- A single, particular detail doing the work several generic ones would
  otherwise need to do.
- Emotional states conveyed through specific physical behavior rather than
  named directly.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: description built entirely from category
  labels (a house, a forest, an angry expression) with no particular,
  observed detail anywhere in a passage.

## Common failure modes

- **Generic description**, relying on category labels instead of particular
  detail, especially common in fast first drafts.
- **Over-description**, piling on many details (some specific, some not)
  past the point where they're doing useful work, slowing pacing without
  adding proportional value.
- **Specificity without relevance**, choosing a vivid but irrelevant detail
  that doesn't connect to character, mood, or plot, functioning as texture
  with no deeper purpose.
- **Telling the emotion and showing the detail redundantly**, stating "she
  was nervous" and then also describing the physical signs of nervousness,
  when the detail alone would have been sufficient.

## Questions for the author

- Is there a passage using a generic category label (a house, a forest, an
  expression) that could be replaced with one particular, observed detail?
- Does a description pile on multiple details past the point where they're
  doing distinct work?
- Is an emotional state named directly and also shown through physical
  detail, when one or the other would be sufficient?
- Does a vivid, specific detail actually connect to character, mood, or
  plot, or is it decorative texture with no deeper purpose?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Find a passage relying on generic category labels and replace one with a
   single particular, observed detail.
2. For an over-described passage, cut details until only the ones doing
   distinct work remain, and compare the result.
3. For an emotional state stated directly, try replacing the direct
   statement with only the physical detail and see whether the emotion
   still reads clearly.
4. Check a vivid detail's connection to the story's character, mood, or
   plot; if it has none, decide whether it earns its place as pure texture
   or should be cut or replaced.

## When this advice does not apply

- Fast-paced action sequences may deliberately minimize description,
  including specific detail, to maintain momentum — sparse prose here is a
  pacing choice, not a failure of specificity.
- Some prose styles (minimalist, spare traditions) use generic or minimal
  description deliberately, where the absence of detail is itself a
  stylistic signature.
- A first draft's generic description is a normal, expected stage of
  process, not a finished-manuscript failure — this pattern is most useful
  during revision.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a description relies on generic labels or particular detail, and
whether a given detail connects meaningfully to character or plot, requires
reading and interpreting the prose — a judgment no structured data
substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Generic:* "The old house looked abandoned, with an overgrown yard and a
sad, empty feeling to it."

*Specific:* "Ivy had pulled two shutters loose from their hinges, and a
child's bicycle, rusted through, lay where it had fallen against the porch
steps years before" — the same abandonment, conveyed through particular,
implication-carrying detail rather than category labels.
