## What it is

Stakes are the concrete answer to "what happens if this character fails, or
succeeds." A reader needs to be able to hold the stakes in mind to feel
tension about an outcome — the more specific and personal the stakes, the
easier they are to track and feel, even when the scale of the threat is
world-ending. Stakes can rise across a story, but they need to be legible at
each point, not simply asserted as high.

## Why readers may care

Tension depends on a reader caring what happens. A reader can't care about an
outcome they can't picture; "everything is at stake" is often less gripping
than "if she's late, her brother ages out of the program that's kept him
safe," because the second is something a reader can hold onto and worry
about specifically. Concrete stakes also let escalation register — a reader
can feel stakes rising only if they knew clearly what the baseline was.

## Common forms and variants

- **Personal stakes**, tied to a specific relationship, need, or cost the
  character (and reader) can track individually.
- **Communal or systemic stakes**, affecting a group, nation, or world —
  often more effective when grounded through a personal stake that
  represents the larger one.
- **Escalating stakes**, where the scale or kind of risk grows across the
  story, provided each stage is made concrete rather than only asserted.
- **Layered stakes**, where a scene carries both an external stake (winning
  a fight) and an internal one (proving something to oneself), doubling the
  tension without doubling the scale.

## What it can look like on the page

- A clearly stated or strongly implied consequence tied to a scene's outcome,
  specific enough to picture.
- A large-scale threat (world-ending, civilization-ending) grounded through
  a specific character's concrete loss.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a scene described as high-stakes in
  narration or dialogue with no concrete consequence a reader can actually
  picture.

## Common failure modes

- **Asserted stakes**, where characters state that something matters greatly
  without the story dramatizing a concrete consequence.
- **Stakes inflation**, where every scene is framed as maximally high-stakes,
  flattening the ability to feel any single moment as more urgent than
  another.
- **Abstract or unlimited stakes**, ("everything," "the whole world," with
  no specific, trackable cost) that are hard for a reader to hold onto
  emotionally despite their nominal scale.

## Questions for the author

- If this scene's protagonist fails, what specifically happens? Can you name
  it in one concrete sentence?
- Is a large-scale threat in this manuscript grounded through any specific
  character's personal, trackable loss?
- Are there scenes narrated as high-stakes where the actual consequence, if
  spelled out, would be vague or minor?
- Across the manuscript, do stakes escalate in a way a reader could
  actually notice, or does everything sit at the same asserted intensity?

## Revision or design experiments

1. For a scene, write the concrete consequence of failure in one sentence;
   if it's hard to write specifically, the stakes may need sharpening.
2. For a world-ending threat, find or add a single character-level stake
   that represents it concretely.
3. Chart stakes (rough: low/medium/high) scene by scene and look for
   clusters where everything sits at the same asserted level.
4. Try lowering the stated stakes of an early scene to leave more room for
   later escalation to actually register.

## When this advice does not apply

- Slice-of-life or low-stakes-by-design fiction can intentionally keep
  consequences small and personal throughout, without needing escalation
  toward anything larger.
- A story building dread through ambiguity (the reader unsure exactly what's
  at risk) may withhold concrete stakes briefly as a deliberate technique,
  provided it resolves the ambiguity before it curdles into vagueness.
- Comedic or absurdist registers may treat stakes ironically, where the
  mismatch between asserted and actual stakes is the joke.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether stated stakes are concrete and trackable, or vague and abstract,
requires reading and interpreting prose; no structured data substitutes for
this judgment. A coach could shortlist passages that assert high stakes as
candidate evidence for the author's own review, but cannot determine
concreteness from text alone with certainty.

## Original micro-examples

*Vague stakes:* "If they fail, everything will be lost," a character says,
with no further specificity about what "everything" concretely means for
anyone in the scene.

*Concrete stakes:* "If they fail, the last working well in the valley goes
to the garrison instead of the village, and Mirren's grandmother won't
survive another dry season" — a large-scale resource conflict grounded
through one trackable, personal cost.
