## What it is

A complication changes the shape of an existing problem rather than simply
making it bigger — a new constraint, a hidden cost to the obvious solution,
a competing obligation that the original goal now conflicts with. This
distinguishes a complication from a raw obstacle (a bigger monster, a
higher wall), which raises difficulty along the same axis a character was
already navigating. A complication instead adds a dimension: the
protagonist doesn't just need to try harder at the same task, they need to
solve a differently shaped problem than the one they started with.

## Why readers may care

A story that raises difficulty only through bigger obstacles along the same
axis can start to feel repetitive even as stakes climb, because the reader
is watching the same kind of struggle intensify rather than a new kind of
struggle emerge. Complications keep a middle section feeling alive by
changing what "solving the problem" actually requires — this is part of
what separates escalation that compounds meaningfully from escalation that
merely scales up.

## Common forms and variants

- **Constraint complications**, where a new limitation (a time pressure, a
  resource restriction, a rule) changes what solutions are viable without
  changing the underlying goal.
- **Competing-obligation complications**, where achieving the goal now
  conflicts with something else the character values, forcing a tradeoff
  (closely related to the values-and-contradiction pattern).
- **Revealed-cost complications**, where the obvious solution to a problem
  turns out to carry a cost that wasn't apparent when the goal was first
  set.
- **Ally-becomes-liability complications**, where a resource or
  relationship the protagonist was counting on turns out to complicate
  rather than help.
- **False-solution complications**, where an apparent fix creates a new,
  unanticipated problem, distinct from simply failing to work at all.

## What it can look like on the page

- A scene where a character's plan is derailed not by a bigger version of
  the expected obstacle, but by a new kind of problem the original plan
  didn't account for.
- A solution that succeeds at its stated goal while creating a new,
  different problem as a direct consequence.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a middle section where each new
  challenge is a larger version of the challenge before it, with no shift
  in the kind of problem the character is solving.

## Common failure modes

- **Escalation-only middles**, raising stakes purely through bigger
  obstacles along the same axis, producing a repetitive feel even as
  danger technically increases.
- **Complications introduced with no connection to the story's
  established world or characters**, arriving as arbitrary new rules rather
  than plausible extensions of what's already been established.
- **A complication that's actually just delay**, adding narrative length
  without genuinely changing the shape of the problem the character faces.

## Questions for the author

- In this manuscript's middle section, do later obstacles differ in kind
  from earlier ones, or only in size?
- Is there a complication that changes what "solving this problem" actually
  requires, rather than just making the existing requirement harder?
- Does a solution anywhere in the manuscript succeed at its stated goal
  while creating a new, different problem?
- Is a complication traceable to something already established in the
  story's world or characters, or does it feel introduced from outside?

## Revision or design experiments

1. List the major obstacles across a story's middle section and label each
   as either "bigger" (same kind, larger scale) or "different" (new
   dimension to the problem); look for long stretches of "bigger" with no
   "different."
2. For a stretch that reads as repetitive, introduce a constraint or
   competing obligation that changes what the protagonist's existing
   solution actually requires.
3. Find a solved problem in the manuscript and add a revealed cost or
   unintended consequence that follows plausibly from the solution.
4. Trace a planned complication back to something already established in
   the story to confirm it doesn't arrive from nowhere.

## When this advice does not apply

- Very short works may not have room for the shift from obstacle to
  complication that a longer middle section needs.
- Some fast-paced action or adventure registers may deliberately favor
  pure escalation (bigger, faster, higher stakes) as their genre promise,
  where complication in this sense would slow momentum rather than serve
  it.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a plot beat functions as a genuine complication (changing the
problem's shape) or merely a bigger obstacle (raising difficulty along the
same axis) requires reading and interpreting the manuscript's causal
structure — a judgment no structured data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Bigger obstacle only:* A character needs to cross a guarded bridge; later,
they need to cross a bridge guarded by more soldiers. Same kind of problem,
larger scale.

*Genuine complication:* The same character instead discovers the bridge is
guarded by soldiers from their own former unit, meaning victory now
requires either violence against people they once served with or finding
an entirely different route — the problem has changed shape, not just
size.

## Sources and confidence notes

The obstacle-versus-complication distinction is foundational in both
Robert McKee's writing on story structure and escalating conflict
([[src.book.mckee-story]]) and John Truby's structural writing on plot
complexity ([[src.book.truby-anatomy-of-story]]); both long-established,
widely taught texts, supporting a `high` confidence rating for the core
principle, while identifying it in a specific manuscript remains
model-assisted.
