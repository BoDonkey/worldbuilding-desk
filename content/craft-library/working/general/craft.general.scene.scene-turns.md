## What it is

A scene turn is the point where a scene's emotional or situational charge
flips — from hope to dread, from safety to danger, from ignorance to
knowledge that can't be unknown — usually driven by a piece of new
information, an action, or a decision. The concept comes from screenwriting
craft, where a "value" (life/death, love/hate, freedom/captivity) is tracked
across a scene, and a scene without a turn is one where that value starts and
ends in the same place: nothing, in this specific structural sense, changed.

## Why readers may care

A reader tracks emotional charge even without naming it. A scene that turns
gives the reader the sensation that the story moved; a scene that doesn't can
feel busy — full of dialogue, action, description — while still landing as
inert. The turn is often smaller and more precise than "something happened";
it's the specific moment the situation's meaning flipped.

## Common forms and variants

- **Single clean turn.** One clear pivot point, usually near the scene's
  back half, where the charge flips once.
- **Multiple turns.** Longer or more complex scenes can turn more than once —
  hope to dread to grim resolve, for instance — each turn raising or
  complicating the last.
- **Micro-turns within dialogue.** A conversation can turn line by line as
  information is revealed, negotiated, or withheld, with the scene's overall
  turn being the sum of several small ones.
- **The negated turn**, where a character expects a turn and it doesn't
  arrive — used deliberately for anticlimax, dread, or dark comedy, but risky
  if it reads as an author's oversight rather than a choice.

## What it can look like on the page

- A specific line, action, or revelation that can be pointed to as the
  moment the scene's emotional charge changed direction.
- Dialogue that ends in a materially different relational position than it
  began (trust broken, an alliance formed, a secret revealed).
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a scene that ends in the same emotional
  register it started in, despite containing dialogue or action.

## Common failure modes

- **Activity without a turn** — fights, chases, and conversations that
  generate motion without changing what anything means.
- **A turn buried too early**, leaving the rest of the scene to coast on
  momentum that's already spent.
- **A turn that isn't earned**, where the pivot depends on information or a
  decision that hasn't been set up, landing as arbitrary rather than
  inevitable-in-hindsight.

## Questions for the author

- Can you point to the specific line or beat where this scene's emotional
  charge changes direction?
- If a reader described the scene's opening and closing emotional state in
  one word each, would those words differ?
- Is the turn earned by what came before it, or does it depend on something
  the reader hasn't been given reason to expect?
- Does a long scene turn only once, and would a second turn partway through
  sharpen it?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Mark the exact line or beat where a scene's charge flips; if none can be
   found, that's the scene to revise first.
2. For a scene that turns too early, try moving the turning information or
   action later, and use the earlier pages to raise what's at stake in the
   turn.
3. Try adding a second, smaller turn partway through a long scene to keep its
   middle from sagging.
4. For a deliberately negated turn (an expected reveal that doesn't land),
   check that the surrounding text signals the anticlimax as intentional.

## When this advice does not apply

- Mood, transition, and atmosphere passages can function without a turn,
  provided they're not doing the narrative heavy lifting of a full scene.
- Some literary styles work in a deliberately flat, plotless register where
  charge shifts are subtle or absent by design.
- Very short beats within a larger scene may not need their own individual
  turn if the containing scene turns as a whole.

## Evidence and detection limits

Identifying a scene's turn — or its absence — requires reading and
interpreting the emotional charge of prose; no structured data substitutes
for this. A coach can highlight candidate turning points for the author's
own confirmation but cannot determine emotional charge from text alone with
certainty.

## Original micro-examples

*No turn:* A scene opens with two characters cautiously trusting each other
and closes with them still cautiously trusting each other, despite three
pages of conversation in between.

*Clear turn:* The same conversation includes one character letting slip a
detail only the betrayer could know; the scene ends with trust broken and an
alliance in question — a specific, locatable pivot point.

## Sources and confidence notes

The value-shift/turn concept is drawn from Robert McKee's screenwriting
craft text ([[src.book.mckee-story]]), adapted here for prose scene
structure rather than quoted directly; `source_confidence` is set to `mixed`
