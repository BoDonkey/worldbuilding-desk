## What it is

Point of view determines whose head the reader is in and how close the
narration sits to that character's immediate thought and sensation (often
called psychic distance — from a distant, summarizing "he was angry" to a
close "the words hit like a slap"). Every POV choice is simultaneously an
information-control choice: it determines what the reader knows, when they
know it, and what remains hidden until a chosen moment, which is the
mechanism behind suspense, dramatic irony, and unreliable narration alike.

## Why readers may care

Suspense depends on the reader knowing less than a character does; dramatic
irony depends on the reader knowing more. Both are functions of POV and
information control, not separate techniques. A reader's trust in a
narrator — how much they believe what they're told — is also shaped by
psychic distance: a very close POV can make even false beliefs feel
persuasive, because the reader is inside the character's immediate
perception rather than receiving it filtered through an implied objective
narrator.

## Common forms and variants

- **First person**, maximally restricting information to what the POV
  character knows, notices, and chooses to report.
- **Close third**, similarly restricted to one character's knowledge and
  perception, with slightly more narrative distance available for the
  author to modulate.
- **Distant or omniscient third**, able to move between characters' knowledge
  and offer information no single character possesses, at the cost of the
  intimacy close POV provides.
- **Multiple POV**, alternating between characters' restricted knowledge to
  create dramatic irony (the reader knowing something no single character
  does) across sections.
- **Unreliable narration**, a deliberate mismatch between what a close POV
  character believes and what's actually true, exploited for effect once the
  reader recognizes the gap.

## What it can look like on the page

- Information revealed to the reader exactly when the POV character learns
  it, versus information the narration includes that the POV character
  couldn't plausibly know or notice.
- Psychic distance shifting deliberately at a key emotional moment (zooming
  close for intensity, pulling back for reflection or dread).
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a "head-hop," where narration briefly
  reports another character's internal thought or knowledge the POV
  character has no access to, without a clear structural signal (a section
  break, a POV-labeled chapter) marking the shift.

## Common failure modes

- **Unintentional head-hopping**, breaking the established POV contract
  without reader-visible signal, which can disorient or reduce trust in the
  narration.
- **Information leakage**, where the narration reveals something the POV
  character doesn't yet know, undercutting suspense the scene was trying to
  build.
- **Uniform psychic distance throughout**, never varying closeness, which can
  flatten emotional peaks that would benefit from a deliberate zoom-in.
- **Withholding information from the reader that the POV character
  demonstrably has**, which can read as an unfair trick rather than earned
  mystery if the withholding isn't justified by the character's own reason
  for not thinking about it.

## Questions for the author

- At this point in the scene, what does the POV character actually know,
  and does the narration ever step outside that knowledge?
- Is a moment of suspense built on the reader knowing less than the
  character, and is that gap consistent with the chosen POV?
- Does psychic distance shift at the story's most emotionally significant
  moments, or does it stay uniform throughout?
- If information is being withheld from the reader that the POV character
  has, is there an in-character reason they wouldn't be dwelling on it right
  now?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Reread a scene and mark any sentence that reports something the POV
   character couldn't know or perceive; either cut it or restructure the
   POV to accommodate it deliberately.
2. For a flat emotional peak, try pulling psychic distance closer (shorter,
   more immediate, more sensory) and see whether the moment sharpens.
3. For a mystery or suspense beat that isn't landing, check whether the
   reader actually has less information than the POV character at that
   point, or whether information has leaked ahead of the reveal.
4. If multiple POV is in use, chart which character knows what by the
   midpoint, and check whether the reader's resulting dramatic-irony
   position is the one the story intends.

## When this advice does not apply

- Deliberately omniscient or intrusive narrators (used in some literary and
  comic traditions) can break restricted-POV conventions as a stylistic
  signature, provided the reader is oriented to expect it from the start.
- A single, brief, clearly signaled POV shift (a labeled interlude, a
  distinct section break) is a structural choice, not an unintentional
  head-hop.
- Some genres and traditions (certain fairy-tale or epic registers)
  conventionally use a more distant, all-knowing narrative voice throughout,
  where close psychic distance would work against the intended effect.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a passage violates an established POV's information limits requires
tracking what a specific character has been shown to know across the
manuscript and comparing it to what a passage reports — a task requiring
close reading and interpretation. A coach could flag candidate
inconsistencies for the author's review where POV is explicit and
consistently labeled, but confirming an actual violation (versus an
intentional shift) is model-assisted, not deterministic.

## Original micro-examples

*Information leakage:* A close-third scene follows a character searching a
room, and the narration mentions "the killer, watching from the doorway,"
though the POV character never notices anyone there — information the POV
character doesn't have has leaked into the reader's awareness.

*Controlled information:* The same scene instead has the POV character
notice, without understanding its significance, a chair angled toward the
door that "definitely wasn't like that ten minutes ago" — the reader gets a
clue exactly at the level the POV character perceives it, preserving both
suspense and fair play.

## Sources and confidence notes

Psychic distance and POV-as-information-control are foundational, widely
taught concepts, drawn here from Ursula K. Le Guin's prose-craft writing
([[src.book.leguin-steering-the-craft]]) and John Gardner's writing on
narrative distance and the "vivid, continuous dream"
([[src.book.gardner-art-of-fiction]]); both long-established texts,
supporting a `high` confidence rating for the core principles, while
identifying specific violations in any given manuscript remains
model-assisted.
