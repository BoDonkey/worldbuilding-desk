## What it is

A binding is a promise the world enforces: an oath that damages the breaker,
a contract that cannot be evaded, a geas that compels, a sect vow whose
violation the system detects. What distinguishes it from an ordinary promise
is that compliance is not a matter of character — the mechanism, not the
person, guarantees it.

That is the design tension the whole mechanic turns on. A promise kept
because a character chose to keep it characterizes them. A promise kept
because breaking it would kill them does not. The interesting versions
recover the choice somewhere else: in the decision to swear, in what the oath
costs to honor, or in a situation where honoring it is worse than dying.

## Why readers may care

Enforced promises make constraints legible. A reader who knows a character
cannot lie about a particular thing reads every conversation about it
differently, and a reader who knows what an oath will cost watches the
approach of the moment it comes due.

Bindings also solve a specific problem in high-power settings: they are one
of the few constraints that scale. A character strong enough that nothing can
stop them can still be bound by something they agreed to, which keeps a
story's conflicts available long after physical opposition has stopped
working.

## Common forms and variants

- **The sworn oath** — voluntarily taken, enforced by the system, usually
  with a stated penalty.
- **The imposed geas** — placed on a character by another party, which makes
  the binding an antagonist's tool.
- **The contract** — negotiated terms with mutual obligations, where the
  interesting material is in the drafting and the loopholes.
- **The lifelong vow** — sect or order membership as a standing constraint,
  covered in part by the sects record.
- **The conditional binding** — active only under stated conditions, so the
  story controls when it bites.
- **The unbreakable versus the costly** — bindings that literally cannot be
  violated versus those that can, at a price. The second is almost always the
  more useful, because it restores the choice.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character choosing terms carefully, because the terms are the whole
  negotiation.
- An oath honored at a cost the reader has been shown in advance.
- A binding used against its holder by someone who read it more carefully
  than they did.
- Conversely: an oath introduced and never tested, which functions as
  worldbuilding decoration rather than as a constraint.

## Common failure modes

- **The untested oath.** A binding established and never brought into
  conflict with anything the character wants.
- **Compliance without character.** The oath removes the decision, so scenes
  that should be about choice become about mechanism.
- **The convenient loophole.** An escape discovered exactly when needed,
  which retroactively drains every earlier oath of weight.
- **Unstated terms.** A binding whose scope is vague, so the reader cannot
  anticipate when it applies and cannot feel it tighten.
- **Oath inflation.** Many bindings across a cast, individually interesting
  and collectively reducing the world to paperwork.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

The failure state is the design. What happens on violation — death, power
loss, physical damage, expulsion, a curse, mere detection — determines what
kind of story the binding supports. Lethal enforcement makes oaths absolute
and removes them from play; survivable enforcement keeps them live, because a
character can weigh breaking one and the reader can wonder whether they will.

Visibility matters in two directions. Can others tell a character is bound?
A visible binding is a social fact — it makes the character trustworthy in a
specific way and exploitable in another, since anyone who knows the terms
knows what they cannot do. A hidden binding produces different scenes
entirely: pressure the other characters cannot see the source of.

Consequentially, a world where promises are mechanically enforceable has a
radically different legal and commercial order. Contracts need no courts,
trust between strangers is cheap, and someone has built an industry around
drafting, witnessing, and dissolving bindings. Settings with oath magic
usually leave this unexplored — a large piece of distinctive worldbuilding
sitting unused.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Any enforced promise invites lawyering: precise wording that satisfies the
letter, oaths sworn on false premises, delegating a forbidden act to someone
unbound, or arranging circumstances where the binding's conditions never
technically trigger. Coercion is the sharpest edge case — an oath extracted
under duress is enforced identically by most systems, which makes forced
swearing a weapon and raises a question about the mechanism's indifference
that a story can profitably notice. Bindings also interact with sacrifice and
cost mechanics, since many are priced in the same currency, and with
betrayal, which becomes structurally different when the system can detect it.

## Questions for the author

- What does this oath cost to keep, in a scene the reader will see?
- Could the character break it? If not, where does the choice live instead?
- Who wrote the terms, and did the other party read them as carefully?
- Can anyone tell this character is bound, and what do they do with that?
- What has a world with enforceable promises built that ours has not?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write the scene where honoring the oath costs the character something
   they badly want. If the scene cannot be written, the binding is currently
   decoration.
2. State the exact terms in one sentence, then look for the loophole. If you
   find one easily, so will a reader.
3. Have a minor character exploit the binding's letter, and see whether the
   world feels more inhabited.
4. Try converting an unbreakable oath into a costly one and see what the
   story gains in choice.

## When this advice does not apply

- Settings where oaths are ordinary social promises with no enforcement, in
  which case this is character material rather than system design.
- Stories where the binding is a background condition — a standing vow that
  is simply part of who someone is — and interrogating it would distract from
  the actual subject.
- Comedic treatments where absurd contract-lawyering is the joke and the
  costs are meant to be weightless.
- Premises where the binding *is* the antagonist, in which case the record's
  questions apply to the antagonist's design rather than the protagonist's
  choices.

## Evidence and detection limits

Very little here is computable. A coach might locate oath-swearing scenes by
their vocabulary and check whether stated terms recur later in the
manuscript, which is a weak consistency check at best.

Whether an oath has been tested, whether keeping it cost anything, and
whether a loophole reads as clever or convenient are all semantic judgments.
An untested oath is not necessarily a fault — it may be waiting for a later
volume — so the coach should ask whether the test is coming rather than
report an absence as a problem.

## Original micro-examples

*Untested oath:* he swore on the Amber Rite never to raise a hand against a
Vashti, and in the following two hundred pages met no Vashti.

*Tested oath:* he had sworn never to raise a hand against a Vashti, and the
girl bleeding in the doorway was a Vashti, and the man who had put her there
would be back before dark. He sat down on the step to think about what
counted as a hand.

the general design principle that a rule-enforced constraint must be tested
