## What it is

Currency in a progression-fiction setting is a medium of exchange whose
value depends on supply relative to demand, exactly as in a real economy —
which means a story where a small number of characters rapidly accumulate
enormous wealth (through adventuring rewards, dungeon loot, or trade)
implies inflationary pressure on that currency unless the setting has
mechanisms (sinks) removing money from circulation at a comparable rate. A
market is the setting's mechanism for exchanging goods and currency, with
its own supply, demand, and price logic; markets that behave inconsistently
across a story (a scarce good's price never responding to its established
scarcity, for instance) undermine the sense that the setting's economy is
real rather than purely decorative.

## Why readers may care

A story doesn't need to model its economy with rigor to be enjoyable, but
readers do notice when large sums of money stop meaning anything —
when a character's wealth escalates so far past what anything in the
story costs that money effectively becomes an abstraction with no felt
weight, the economic dimension of the story's power fantasy quietly stops
functioning even if the story doesn't acknowledge the shift. A setting
that engages inflation, sinks, and price consistency directly — even
briefly — signals that its economy is more than set dressing, which can
open genuine plot material (a currency crisis, a market a character
manipulates, a good made cheap by the character's own success).

## Common forms and variants

- **Unmanaged wealth accumulation**, where characters' currency grows
  rapidly with no acknowledged effect on prices or the wider economy — the
  most common default, workable as long as the story doesn't ask readers to
  track economic realism closely.
- **Explicit inflation acknowledgment**, where a story directly engages the
  effect of a character's (or a group's) wealth accumulation on local
  prices, turning the economic side effect into plot material.
- **Currency sinks**, mechanisms that remove money from circulation
  (taxes, expensive consumables, mandatory fees, decaying currency)
  offsetting the inflationary pressure of accumulating wealth, closely
  related to the resource-lifecycle-design record's general
  source/sink framework applied specifically to currency.
- **Multiple, non-fungible currencies**, where different currencies serve
  different economic niches (a common trade currency, a rare
  faction-specific one, a barter-only local economy), adding texture and
  potentially interesting exchange-rate or access tensions.
- **Localized market variation**, where prices differ meaningfully between
  regions or communities based on their own supply and demand, making
  travel and trade economically consequential rather than uniform.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character's growing wealth shown to affect what things cost around
  them, whether through explicit price changes or through characters
  reacting to the shift.
- A price consistently reflecting an established good's scarcity across
  multiple scenes, rather than fluctuating arbitrarily.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a character whose wealth has grown by
  orders of magnitude across a story continuing to purchase goods at prices
  that never reflect any economic pressure from that accumulation.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and cross-mechanic interaction

Currency and market consistency interact directly with the crafting-and-
economy-loops record's scarcity and market guidance, applied here
specifically to money rather than goods. They interact with the systemic-
social-consequences pattern, since rapid, concentrated wealth accumulation
by adventurers or cultivators has plausible effects on labor, class
structure, and local economies that a story can engage or leave
unexamined. They also interact with quest and reward design broadly: a
reward economy that consistently outpaces any sink mechanism will,
eventually, either need the story to acknowledge the resulting inflation or
simply stop tracking currency as meaningful at all — a legitimate choice,
but one worth making deliberately rather than by default.

Almost nobody in an economy can see the economy, and that gap is usable.
Characters experience inflation as prices that feel wrong, a wage that no
longer covers rent, or a merchant who will not take the coin they were paid
in — not as a supply curve. A setting where some institution *can* see the
aggregate (a merchant guild with records, a treasury, a system interface
quoting market rates) has effectively given someone economic sight, and that
asymmetry is itself a power the story can dramatize. For the reader, the
choice is whether the narration explains the mechanism or lets them infer it
from prices. Explaining converts the economy into worldbuilding the reader
admires; withholding lets them feel the squeeze alongside the characters,
and rewards the attentive reader who works it out first.

## Common failure modes

- **Wealth escalation with no acknowledged economic effect**, where large
  sums accumulate with no corresponding change in what anything costs,
  eventually making currency numbers feel arbitrary.
- **Inconsistent pricing**, where an established scarce good's price
  fluctuates with no traceable cause.
- **No sink mechanisms at all**, in a setting that otherwise tracks
  currency carefully, creating an implied but unaddressed inflationary
  spiral the story never engages.

## Questions for the author

- As this character's wealth has grown, has the story shown or implied any
  effect on local prices or the wider economy?
- Are prices for established goods consistent across the scenes where
  they're mentioned, or do they fluctuate without explanation?
- Does this setting have any currency sinks (taxes, expensive necessities,
  decay) that would offset the inflationary pressure of rapid wealth
  accumulation?
- If currency stops feeling like it means anything by the story's later
  chapters, was that a deliberate choice or an unexamined drift?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Track a character's wealth at several points across the manuscript and
   consider whether any scene should acknowledge its economic effect.
2. Audit an established good's price across multiple mentions for
   consistency.
3. If no currency sink exists in a setting that otherwise tracks wealth
   carefully, consider introducing one, or deliberately deciding that
   currency tracking stops mattering past a certain point in the story.
4. For a large wealth accumulation, consider a scene where another
   character reacts to or is affected by the resulting economic shift.

## When this advice does not apply

- Very lightweight systems where currency is never load-bearing for plot
  or characterization don't need this level of economic consistency.
- A story deliberately depicting a currency's meaninglessness as
  commentary (a setting where money has stopped mattering, examined
  directly) is using the drift intentionally rather than by accident.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where a project explicitly tracks currency amounts and prices linked to
scenes, consistency checks are close to deterministic. Whether the
narrative adequately engages the economic implications of wealth
accumulation requires interpreting the worldbuilding and remains
model-assisted.

## Original micro-examples

*Unmanaged inflation:* A character's wealth grows from a few coins to an
amount that could purchase a small kingdom, and by the story's end they're
still purchasing a loaf of bread for the same price established in chapter
one, with no acknowledgment of the disconnect.

*Engaged economic effect:* The same character's spending instead is shown
driving up prices in the town where they're staying, with a local merchant
explicitly commenting on how much more expensive everything has gotten
since adventurers like the protagonist started passing through — a small
scene that makes the economy feel real.
