## What it is

Many progression systems include chance: critical hits, drop rates, skill
activation percentages, an explicit Luck attribute, or a system that rolls
for outcomes the characters cannot control. This record is about designing
that chance deliberately — where it operates, who knows about it, and what
it is allowed to decide.

Games and fiction want opposite things from randomness. A game uses it to
produce uncertainty the player experiences as tension. A novel's outcome is
fixed before the reader arrives, so randomness in fiction is always a
*representation* of chance rather than chance itself. That difference is the
whole design problem.

## Why readers may care

Uncertainty is a real source of engagement — games scholarship treats it as
close to the substance of play — and fiction can borrow the effect. A world
where outcomes are not guaranteed feels less arranged, and a character who
can fail at something they are good at is more interesting than one whose
competence is deterministic.

The hazard is symmetrical and larger. When a favourable outcome is
attributed to chance, the reader cannot credit the character for it. A
protagonist who wins a fight through a lucky critical has not demonstrated
anything, and a story that resolves difficulties this way is spending the
credibility its progression system exists to build. Readers of this genre
are, by disposition, people who like watching things be earned.

## Common forms and variants

- **Displayed probability** — the system states odds, so characters and
  readers can reason about risk explicitly.
- **Hidden randomness** — chance operates unseen and outcomes simply happen,
  which reads as fortune rather than mathematics.
- **The Luck attribute** — chance itself made a stat, which is a distinctive
  genre move and quietly makes fortune into something a character can invest
  in.
- **Bounded randomness** — variance around an outcome the character's skill
  determines, so chance colours results without deciding them.
- **Catastrophic tails** — rare, extreme outcomes either way: memorable, and
  dangerous used more than sparingly.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character choosing a risk with stated odds, so the decision is legible
  and the outcome — either way — lands as a consequence of the choice.
- A drop or a critical narrated as remarkable *because* the story has
  established how unlikely it is.
- Conversely: a fight turned by a fortunate roll the reader had no way to
  anticipate.
- Repeated improbable good fortune, which readers tend to notice in
  aggregate even when each instance passed unremarked.

## Common failure modes

- **Luck as plot armour.** Chance that consistently favours the protagonist,
  which is not randomness but authorial protection wearing its costume.
- **Rolled resolutions.** Climaxes decided by chance rather than by anything
  the character did, the sharpest version of the earned-resolution problem.
- **Odds without consequence.** Stated probabilities that never produce a
  bad outcome, teaching the reader that the numbers are decoration.
- **Unbounded variance.** A system where any outcome is possible at any time,
  which prevents the reader from anticipating anything.
- **Chance as an excuse for the improbable.** A convenient event justified
  after the fact by a system that permits it, which readers read as
  coincidence with paperwork.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

Visibility is the central decision here, more than in most mechanics. If
probabilities are displayed, risk becomes a subject characters can discuss
and the reader shares the calculation — which converts chance into decision
material and is usually the safer choice for the earned-resolution problem.
If chance is hidden, outcomes read as fortune, which is atmospheric but gives
the reader no way to distinguish a designed system from an author's thumb on
the scale.

Progression interacts with chance in a specific way worth deciding
explicitly: does growth reduce variance or increase the ceiling? A system
where advancement makes outcomes more reliable produces a protagonist who
becomes steadier; one where advancement raises the top end produces a
protagonist who becomes more explosive and no more dependable. Both are
legitimate, and they feel entirely different.

Socially, a world with real randomness in it develops institutions for
managing risk — insurance, guarantors, hedging, sworn appraisals, gambling —
and people whose profession is absorbing variance for others. A setting with
drop rates and no economy of risk around them has left an obvious feature of
its own rules unused.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Any stated probability invites manipulation: reroll mechanics, luck-boosting
consumables, repeating a low-cost attempt until it succeeds, or arranging
many small trials rather than one large one. Where the system permits
grinding an improbable outcome, someone in the world is doing exactly that
for a living. Chance also interacts sharply with loot and economy — drop
rates are a supply curve, and a rare item's value depends on a probability
someone can compute — and with fake progression, since variance can disguise
a flat power curve by making outcomes look eventful.

## Questions for the author

- What is chance allowed to decide in this story, and what is it never
  allowed to decide?
- Can the reader tell the difference between good luck and good judgment
  here?
- Does the protagonist's luck run both ways, and where is the scene that
  proves it?
- Does advancement make outcomes more reliable or more extreme?
- If the odds were stated aloud, would this scene become a decision instead
  of an event?

## Revision or design experiments

1. List the last ten significant outcomes and mark each as skill, choice, or
   chance. A chance-heavy list is worth looking at even if each instance
   seemed fine.
2. Take one lucky outcome and rewrite it as a decision made under stated
   odds. The scene usually gains a beat and loses nothing.
3. Write the scene where luck runs against the protagonist at a cost that
   sticks, and see whether the system suddenly reads as real.
4. Decide, in one sentence, whether growth reduces variance or raises the
   ceiling — then check the draft against the answer.

## When this advice does not apply

- Comedic and picaresque work where absurd fortune is the mode and
  scrutinizing it would break the register.
- Stories deliberately about fortune, fate, or the unfairness of chance,
  where a protagonist's luck is the subject rather than a mechanism.
- Systems with no randomness at all, where this record simply does not apply
  and its questions should not be imported.
- Gambling and risk-centred premises, where the failure modes above are the
  point and the reader is watching the variance on purpose.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where a manuscript reports probabilities or roll outcomes in structured text,
a coach can extract them and compare observed frequencies against stated
odds — one of the few genuinely deterministic checks available anywhere in
this library, and a striking one when a system's own numbers do not match its
events.

Everything else is interpretation. Whether an outcome reads as earned or
lucky is a semantic judgment; a run of favourable results may be authorial
protection or may be a deliberately fortunate character. The coach can report
the ratio of stated-chance to skill-determined outcomes, and should present
that as a description rather than a verdict.

## Original micro-examples

*Rolled resolution:* the killing blow triggered a 2% critical, and the duel
he had been losing for six pages ended. He had done nothing differently.

*Chance as decision:* the appraisal put the ritual at one chance in five, and
told him what the other four did to the people who tried. He went ahead
anyway, and the book had already shown the reader what he was accepting.
