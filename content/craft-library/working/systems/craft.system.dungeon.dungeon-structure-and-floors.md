## What it is

A dungeon, tower, gate, or rift is a bounded space with internal structure —
usually tiered, usually escalating, usually with a defined entrance and some
notion of depth. The convention gives a story three things at once: a supply
of appropriate difficulty, a spatial metaphor for progress, and a natural
episode boundary.

This record is about designing that space as a system: what governs its
difficulty, what it produces, who else uses it, and what it is for in the
world beyond being where the protagonist goes to fight.

## Why readers may care

Depth is the most legible progress metaphor the genre has. "He reached the
fortieth floor" communicates advancement without a single statistic, and a
reader who has watched floor eleven cost the protagonist dearly understands
what floor forty means without being told.

A bounded space also solves the serial's episode problem. Each descent has a
natural beginning, an escalating middle, and an exit — a shape that can be
repeated without feeling repetitive, provided what happens inside it changes.

## Common forms and variants

- **The linear tower** — strictly ordered floors, each harder, progress
  measured by number. Clearest and most prone to monotony.
- **The branching dungeon** — routes and choices within a level, so
  navigation is a decision rather than a march.
- **The persistent world-dungeon** — a space others also use, with its own
  economy, factions, and territorial politics.
- **The regenerating dungeon** — a space that resets or respawns, which
  permits grinding and requires a stated reason it is not simply farmed.
- **The living dungeon** — a space with agency of its own, whether by design
  or as an antagonist. The dungeon-core profile covers the case where that
  agency is the protagonist.
- **The one-way descent** — no retreat, which converts every floor into a
  commitment.

## What it can look like on the page

- A floor whose difficulty comes from its shape — a corridor that removes the
  party's numbers advantage — rather than from its inhabitants' statistics.
- Established rules about resting, retreating, and resupplying, honored under
  pressure.
- Other delvers encountered, competing, failing, or having got there first.
- Conversely: floors distinguished only by monster level, and a dungeon that
  seems to exist solely for the protagonist's benefit.

## Common failure modes

- **The corridor of statistics.** Floors differing only in enemy numbers, so
  descent produces no new experience.
- **Unexplained exclusivity.** A rich, accessible dungeon that nobody else
  seems to exploit, in a world with obvious incentives to exploit it.
- **Rules that bend.** Retreat, resupply, or respawn rules relaxed when the
  plot needs relief, which teaches the reader the space is not real.
- **The infinite tower with no shape.** Endless floors with no landmarks,
  thresholds, or structural change, which removes the sense of progress the
  form exists to provide.
- **Depth without cost.** Descent that risks nothing durable, making the
  measuring stick meaningless.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

The most important decision is what failure inside the space costs and
whether it is recoverable. A dungeon with a working retreat mechanism
produces stories about judgment — when to turn back — while a one-way
structure produces stories about commitment. Both work; leaving it undecided
produces a space whose danger the reader cannot calibrate.

Visibility shapes anticipation. If delvers can learn what a floor holds —
through scouting, records, survivors, or an appraisal system — descent
becomes preparation, and the reader shares it. If each floor is unknown,
descent is discovery, which is tense and harder to sustain across many
iterations because the reader has nothing to anticipate with.

Consequentially, a bounded space full of resources is an economy. It implies
guilds, licences, tolls, insurance, information brokers selling floor maps,
a town at the entrance living off delvers, and someone claiming ownership of
the whole thing. Progression settings often build the dungeon carefully and
leave everything around it thin, when the surrounding economy is usually the
more distinctive material.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Bounded spaces with rules invite optimization: farming a safe floor for
resources, camping a respawn point, luring enemies to the entrance, using a
retreat rule to reset an encounter, or splitting a party to cover more ground
at known risk. If a dungeon regenerates, the exploit is grinding, and the
world needs a reason — cost, diminishing returns, danger, law — why everyone
is not doing it constantly. Dungeon structure also interacts directly with
enemy scaling, since floors are where difficulty curves become visible, and
with loot economies, since the space is usually the primary faucet through
which items enter the world.

## Questions for the author

- What makes floor forty different from floor four, apart from numbers?
- Who else is in this space, and what have they made of it?
- What are the rules about leaving, and has the story ever honored them
  inconveniently?
- Where does what comes out of here end up, and who profits?
- If the protagonist stopped descending, what would happen to them and to the
  place?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Describe three floors in one line each, in terms of the *problem* each
   poses. Lines that reduce to "stronger monsters" mark where the space needs
   design rather than scaling.
2. Write the town at the entrance: who lives off this dungeon and how. The
   economy usually generates plot faster than the dungeon does.
3. State the retreat and resupply rules explicitly, then find the scene where
   the plot wanted to bend one.
4. Put a rival party on the same floor and see what the space becomes when it
   is contested.

## When this advice does not apply

- Stories where a dungeon appears once as a location rather than as a
  recurring structure, and this record's economy questions would be overkill.
- Dungeon-core premises, where the space is the protagonist and the profile
  record governs; this record covers the space as a system others enter.
- Horror-inflected treatments where the space is deliberately unknowable and
  its rules are meant to be untrustworthy — though even there, the *reader*
  usually needs a rule to feel it broken.
- Settings where the bounded space is metaphorical or singular, such as a
  one-time trial, where repetition questions do not arise.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where floors or tiers are numbered, a coach can chart progress against
chapter count, note how long the protagonist spends per floor, and compare
that curve to any reported growth — a genuinely useful and largely
deterministic observation for tower structures.

What cannot be computed is whether floors are meaningfully different. That
requires reading what each demands, which is interpretive. A fast descent may
indicate a treadmill or a deliberately accelerating arc, and floor-count
progress says nothing about whether the space feels real. The coach should
report the curve and ask about the design.

## Original micro-examples

*Corridor of statistics:* floor twelve had the same wolves as floor eleven,
with more health. Floor thirteen had more of them.

*Designed floor:* floor twelve was flooded to the knee, and the wolves could
swim. The party's spearman, who had carried every fight so far, spent the
whole floor being useless and knowing it.
