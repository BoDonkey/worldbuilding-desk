## What it is

Two related questions, often left implicit: how does a character come to have
a skill at all, and how does a skill they have get better? A system can grant
skills as rewards, sell them, teach them, or grow them through use — and can
improve them by repetition, instruction, insight, or expenditure.

This is distinct from the classes-versus-skills comparison, which is about
how progression is *structured*, and from perks and talents, which are
discrete selected upgrades. This record is about the continuous middle: the
slow part, where competence is built.

## Why readers may care

Skill acquisition is where a progression system can show effort. Levels and
rewards arrive; skills are practiced. If the system grows what a character
does repeatedly, then what they practice becomes characterization — a reader
learns who someone is from what they got good at.

Game design's mastery literature is useful here with a caveat: it describes
the pleasure of a player becoming competent, which a reader does not
experience. What transfers is the shape — early rapid gains, a long middle,
a plateau — and the emotional register it implies. What does not transfer is
the satisfaction; the reader has to be given that through the character's
experience rather than their own.

## Common forms and variants

- **Use-based growth** — skills improve by doing, so the character's history
  is legible in their sheet. Rewards specialization and invites grinding.
- **Instruction-based** — skills taught by someone, which makes access a
  social question and puts teachers in the plot.
- **Insight or breakthrough** — advancement by understanding rather than
  repetition, common in cultivation and well suited to dramatization.
- **Purchased or granted** — skills bought with points or awarded by the
  system, the fastest and the least characterizing.
- **Ranked tiers** — named mastery levels (novice, adept, master) that make
  progress legible and create thresholds worth crossing.
- **Skill fusion or evolution** — skills combining into new ones, which
  rewards experimentation and needs rules or it becomes arbitrary.

## What it can look like on the page

- A skill that improved because of something the reader watched the character
  do.
- A plateau the character has to solve rather than out-repeat.
- A master demonstrating the same skill at a level that reframes what the
  protagonist thought they had.
- Conversely: a skill list growing between chapters with no scene attached to
  any entry.

## Common failure modes

- **Acquisition without practice.** Skills appearing as rewards, so the sheet
  grows and no effort is ever shown.
- **The unearned prodigy.** A protagonist who masters in weeks what the
  setting says takes decades, with no stated reason — which quietly tells the
  reader that the world's rules are decorative.
- **Grinding narrated.** Repetition depicted at length because the system
  rewards it, which is accurate and inert.
- **Mastery without ceiling.** No plateau, no diminishing returns, so
  practice never poses a decision about what to spend time on.
- **The unused skill.** Entries acquired and never used again, which trains
  the reader to skim the list — related to the stat-block density problem.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

Failure in a skill system is usually a plateau, and how the story handles it
determines whether skills are interesting. If effort always yields progress,
practice is a formality. If progress stalls and the character must find
another route — a teacher, an insight, a different approach — then the
plateau becomes a scene.

Visibility decides whether skill is social. If levels are legible to others,
skill is a credential: people can be hired, ranked, and doubted on it, and
concealing a skill becomes an act. If skill is invisible until demonstrated,
reputation must be built the slow way and underestimation is available. The
reader's view can differ from both, which is where dramatic irony lives.

Systemically, a world with teachable, rankable skills has schools,
certifications, guild examinations, apprenticeships, and a labor market
sorted by them — and a black market in forged credentials. If skills grow by
use, someone has industrialized the use: training halls, practice partners
for hire, safe monsters to grind against. That infrastructure is usually more
distinctive than the skill list itself.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Use-based systems invite the obvious abuse: repeating a trivial action safely
to farm progress, which every such world should have noticed. Other standard
edges are training against helpless targets, exploiting a skill that levels
faster than its cost, and stacking skills whose bonuses were never meant to
combine. Skill systems interact with experience sources — if both grant
growth, the story has two advancement economies and should know how they
relate — and with classes, since a class that gates skills makes acquisition
a question of permission rather than practice.

## Questions for the author

- How did this character get each of their significant skills, and did the
  reader see it?
- What does practice cost here — time, risk, money, opportunity?
- Where does progress stall, and what breaks the stall?
- Who else has this skill, and how did they learn it?
- If a skill has not been used in twenty chapters, is it still doing anything?

## Revision or design experiments

1. List the protagonist's skills with the scene where each was acquired.
   Blank entries are where growth is being asserted rather than shown.
2. Write a plateau: a skill that stops improving, and a character who has to
   do something other than repeat.
3. Put the same skill in a master's hands for one scene and see what it does
   to the reader's sense of the protagonist's level.
4. Name the most efficient exploit in this system and decide who in the world
   is using it.

## When this advice does not apply

- Systems with no skills, where growth is attribute- or tier-based and this
  record does not apply.
- Stories whose promise is rapid acquisition — power fantasy where watching
  the list grow is the pleasure readers came for.
- Cultivation settings where breakthrough rather than practice is the
  advancement model; the realms record governs that case.
- Short work, where an acquisition arc has no room to develop.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where skills and ranks are reported in structured text, a coach can extract
them, chart acquisitions against chapters, and identify entries that appear
once and never recur. Both are close to deterministic and genuinely useful —
the unused-skill list in particular is hard to see by reading and easy to
compute.

What cannot be determined is whether an acquisition was earned. That requires
reading the surrounding scene, and a skill granted without practice may be a
deliberate gift with its own consequences. The coach should report the shape
of the acquisition curve and ask.

## Original micro-examples

*Acquisition without practice:* "Skill acquired: Bladework (Novice). Skill
acquired: Tracking (Novice). Skill acquired: Herbalism (Novice)." — three
competences and no afternoons.

*Practice shown:* he had been throwing the knife at the same post for eleven
days, and the system had said nothing at all, and then on the twelfth day it
said Novice and he sat down in the dirt and laughed.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the handoff's second sense, with the
disanalogy stated in the record itself: all three sources describe a *player*
acquiring competence, while a reader watches a character do it and gets none
of the mastery pleasure directly. The argument that practice characterizes,
and the social and economic consequences described here, are this drafting
pass's synthesis rather than sourced claims.
