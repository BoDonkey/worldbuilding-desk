## What it is

This record applies the general systemic-social-consequences framework to
three specific, closely related domains: institutions (the organizations —
guilds, sects, licensing bodies, military orders — that form around a
power system), labor (what work looks like in a society where some
fraction of the population has access to power that could replace,
augment, or devalue conventional work), and governance (who actually rules,
and whether that authority tracks personal power, institutional position,
or something independent of both). A setting that hasn't decided how these
three interact tends to produce inconsistencies a careful reader will
notice — a "kingdom" whose government never reckons with the fact that a
handful of its citizens could individually topple its army, for instance.

## Why readers may care

Institutions, labor, and governance are where a power system's abstract
implications become concrete, trackable social structure — a reader can
hold "the mages' guild controls licensing and therefore controls who gets
to practice" in mind much more easily than an abstract claim that "magic
has changed society." These three domains are also a rich, underused
source of plot material distinct from personal power conflicts: political
maneuvering over guild leadership, labor displaced or transformed by
magical automation, a government whose legitimacy is genuinely threatened
by individuals more powerful than its institutions.

## Common forms and variants

- **Power-tracks-governance systems**, where political authority directly
  correlates with personal cultivation or magical power — the simplest and
  most common default, though one that raises its own questions about why
  weaker administrators would be tolerated at all.
- **Power-independent-of-governance systems**, where political authority
  is deliberately separated from raw personal power (a bureaucracy, a
  hereditary monarchy, an elected council) that maintains control despite
  not commanding the setting's strongest individuals — often more
  narratively interesting because it requires the story to explain how
  that separation is sustained.
- **Institution-mediated power**, where guilds, sects, or licensing bodies
  sit between individual power and formal governance, controlling access
  and legitimacy without being the government themselves.
- **Displaced-labor settings**, where magical or system-based automation
  has visibly changed what conventional work looks like, with characters
  or communities affected by that displacement.
- **Unexamined-default settings**, a failure mode rather than a variant,
  where institutions and governance simply mirror a real-world historical
  period with no adaptation for the setting's actual power system.

## What it can look like on the page

- A political or institutional structure whose stability is explicitly
  addressed given the setting's power distribution — why the government
  hasn't been overthrown by more powerful individuals, or how it maintains
  legitimacy despite them.
- Labor or economic activity shown adapting to the presence of a power
  system — jobs that exist because of it, jobs displaced by it, workers
  whose livelihoods depend on navigating around it.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a kingdom, guild, or government whose
  structure and stability are never addressed despite the setting's power
  system implying they should be under obvious strain.

## Common failure modes

- **Unexamined governance stability**, where a weak formal government
  persists despite the setting having individuals capable of overturning
  it, with no explanation for the persistence.
- **Labor untouched by the power system**, where an economy functions
  exactly as a non-magical historical setting would despite the story's
  own power system implying it shouldn't.
- **Institutions with no political stakes**, where a guild or sect exists
  as flavor with no actual competition, hierarchy dispute, or governance
  function affecting plot.

## Questions for the author

- Given this setting's power distribution, why hasn't formal governance
  been overturned by more powerful individuals — or has it, and does the
  story engage that?
- Has labor or the economy visibly adapted to the presence of this
  setting's power system, or does it function as though the system doesn't
  exist?
- Do institutions (guilds, sects, licensing bodies) have genuine political
  stakes affecting the plot, or do they function as decorative setting
  detail?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write one sentence explaining why formal governance in this setting
   hasn't been overturned by its most powerful individuals, and check
   whether that explanation is legible anywhere in the text.
2. Find or add a scene showing labor or economic activity shaped by the
   presence of the power system.
3. Give an institution a genuine political stake — a leadership dispute, a
   resource competition — that affects a plot decision.

## When this advice does not apply

- Small-scale, personal-stakes stories may deliberately keep their frame
  narrow enough that governance and institutional questions are outside
  scope, provided the setting doesn't imply a larger scale it never
  engages.
- A newly emerged power system (early system apocalypse, a recent
  awakening) hasn't had time to reshape institutions yet, and the absence
  of adaptation is accurate rather than an oversight.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a setting's institutions, labor, and governance plausibly reflect
its power system requires reading and interpreting the manuscript's
worldbuilding as a whole — a judgment no structured data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Unexamined governance:* A kingdom is ruled by a hereditary monarchy with
no magical ability at all, in a setting where several independent
cultivators could individually raze the capital, with no in-text
explanation for why this arrangement persists.

*Examined governance:* The same monarchy instead maintains power through an
explicit, dramatized arrangement — a mutual-destruction pact among the
realm's most powerful cultivators, brokered generations ago and actively
maintained by a dedicated institution whose collapse would immediately
destabilize the whole political order — giving the setting's power/
governance gap a legible, plot-relevant explanation.
