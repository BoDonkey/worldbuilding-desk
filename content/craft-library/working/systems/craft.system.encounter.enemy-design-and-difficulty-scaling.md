## What it is

Enemy design is the deliberate construction of opposition: what a threat can
do, how it differs from the last one, and what a character must change in
order to beat it. Difficulty scaling is how that opposition grows as the
protagonist does — whether by larger numbers, new capabilities, worse
circumstances, or opponents who are simply better at fighting.

The distinction that matters most is between opposition that is *bigger* and
opposition that is *different*. Both are legitimate. Only the second reliably
requires the protagonist to demonstrate anything the reader has not already
seen.

## Why readers may care

Progression fiction's central promise is that growth is real. Opposition is
where that claim is tested: a protagonist who grows tenfold and faces enemies
who also grow tenfold has, from the reader's seat, not moved. Game design
frames this as the relationship between challenge and skill — a challenge
that stays exactly proportional to ability produces neither mastery nor
threat.

Enemies also carry a great deal of worldbuilding cheaply. What a world sends
against people says what that world is: whether threats are natural,
manufactured, administered by a system, or other people with their own
reasons.

## Common forms and variants

- **Numerical scaling** — the same opposition with larger values. Simple to
  run and the fastest route to a treadmill.
- **Capability scaling** — enemies with new abilities that invalidate a
  previously winning approach, forcing adaptation.
- **Circumstantial difficulty** — the same enemy made harder by terrain,
  timing, obligations, or something the protagonist must protect.
- **Attrition** — difficulty produced by sequence rather than by any single
  fight, where the challenge is what remains after the third one.
- **Peer opposition** — antagonists on the protagonist's own progression
  curve, whose growth is visible and competitive.
- **Tiered or boss structure** — set-piece opponents that mark thresholds,
  common in tower and dungeon structures.

## What it can look like on the page

- A fight the protagonist wins with a tactic that would not have worked
  fifty chapters ago.
- An enemy whose defeat requires giving something up rather than exceeding a
  number.
- Conversely: a series of fights differing only in the size of the numbers
  reported, which readers often describe as the book becoming repetitive
  without being able to say why.
- Opposition that arrives exactly matched to current capability every time,
  which reads as a world arranged around the protagonist.

## Common failure modes

- **The treadmill.** Opposition scaled in lockstep with growth, so nothing
  the protagonist has gained is ever visible.
- **Difficulty as arithmetic.** Enemies distinguished only by statistics, so
  no fight teaches the reader anything new about the world or the character.
- **The unearned spike.** A sudden, unforeshadowed leap in opposition,
  survived by something the protagonist had not previously demonstrated.
- **Fodder inflation.** Ever-larger numbers of trivial enemies substituting
  for meaningful threat.
- **The disposable boss.** A set-piece opponent built up over chapters and
  resolved in a paragraph — a promise made at length and paid briefly.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

The most consequential question is what losing looks like. A system where
defeat means death forces the story either to avoid defeat or to have a
resurrection mechanic; a system where defeat costs progress, resources,
territory, or people allows the protagonist to lose and the story to
continue, which is usually worth more than it costs.

Visibility shapes anticipation directly. If characters can appraise an
enemy's tier before engaging, fights become decisions — approach, avoid,
prepare — and the reader shares that calculation. If they cannot, fights
become discoveries, and tension comes from the unknown rather than from the
odds. Costikyan's treatment of uncertainty is useful here: a fight whose
outcome the reader can compute is a different experience from one they
cannot, and a system that reports exact numbers has chosen the first.

Consequentially, a world's threat level is a fact about that world.
Consistently dangerous regions imply depopulation, defensive institutions,
insurance, hazard pay, and people who make a living where others cannot. If
monsters are a standing feature and nobody has organized around them, the
setting is quieter than its own premise.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Any legible scaling rule can be gamed: hunting just below a threshold that
triggers stronger spawns, farming a predictable respawn, luring enemies into
terrain that defeats them, or exploiting a resistance the system displays
plainly. Where enemies grant advancement, the exploit surface merges with
the experience-source record's, and the two should be reasoned about
together. Difficulty also interacts with attributes and caps: a soft cap
that flattens growth changes what scaling means, and a system with hard caps
eventually requires opposition that is *different* rather than larger,
whether the author planned for that or not.

## Questions for the author

- What must the protagonist do differently to beat this enemy that they
  could not have done before?
- What does losing this fight cost, and is that cost survivable for the
  story?
- Can characters see what they are facing before they commit?
- If the protagonist's growth were halved, which of the next five encounters
  would become impossible? If none, the encounters may be scaling with them.
- Who else in this world fights these things, and what has that done to how
  they live?

## Revision or design experiments

1. List the last ten encounters with one line each on what made them hard.
   Repeated entries suggest scaling by size rather than by kind.
2. Take one upcoming fight and remove the protagonist's strongest ability,
   then see whether a solution still exists. If not, the encounter may be
   testing one number rather than a character.
3. Design one defeat that the story can survive, and place it where the
   stakes are legible but not terminal.
4. Write the enemy's own objective in one sentence. Opponents that want
   something tend to produce better scenes than opponents that have
   statistics.

## When this advice does not apply

- Power-fantasy subgenres where effortless dominance is the promise, and
  meaningful opposition would break the contract readers arrived for.
- Horror-inflected work where opposition is deliberately unscaled and
  unbeatable, and the question is survival rather than victory.
- Slice-of-life and crafting-centred stories where combat is peripheral and
  need not carry progression at all.
- Early chapters establishing a baseline, where straightforwardly
  outclassing weak opposition is doing legitimate work.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where a manuscript reports enemy levels, tiers, or statistics, a coach can
extract them and chart opposition against the protagonist's own reported
growth. That comparison is close to deterministic and is one of the more
genuinely computable observations available in this family.

What it cannot show is whether a fight was interesting, whether a tactic was
novel, or whether a threat felt real. A flat difficulty chart may describe a
treadmill or a deliberate plateau; a spike may be an unearned escalation or a
well-prepared reversal. The numbers locate the question; they do not answer
it.

## Original micro-examples

*Scaling by size:* the third gate wolf had 4,000 health instead of 400. He
killed it the same way, for longer.

*Scaling by kind:* the fourth gate wolf did not close. It waited at the edge
of his lantern light for two nights, and by the time it came he had spent
everything that made him dangerous on staying awake.
