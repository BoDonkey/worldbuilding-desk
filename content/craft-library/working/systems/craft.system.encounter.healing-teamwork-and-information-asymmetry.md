## What it is

Healing describes any mechanism that undoes or mitigates harm already
dealt — restoring health, removing a status effect, repairing damaged
equipment — and its rules (how much, how often, at what cost) directly
affect how permanent any given injury feels within a fight. Teamwork
describes how a group's members' distinct capabilities complement each
other tactically, turning a group fight into something more than several
simultaneous individual fights. Information asymmetry describes what each
side of an encounter knows about the other — a team with better information
about an enemy's weaknesses, numbers, or intentions has an advantage
independent of raw power, and managing what each side knows is a tactical
tool in its own right.

## Why readers may care

Healing rules set the felt permanence of injury within a fight: a system
with abundant, cheap healing lowers moment-to-moment stakes (readers know
damage will likely be undone) while a system with scarce or costly healing
raises them. Teamwork that's genuinely complementary — not just multiple
characters attacking the same target — gives an ensemble cast a reason to
fight together rather than functioning as one strong character with
disposable backup. Information asymmetry adds a layer of tension distinct
from raw combat power: a well-informed but weaker team can credibly
outmaneuver a stronger but less-informed one, which broadens what "winning"
a fight can look like beyond simple stat comparison.

## Common forms and variants

- **Abundant, cheap healing**, common in lighter-stakes or comedic LitRPG,
  where injury is a temporary tactical setback rather than a lasting cost.
- **Scarce or costly healing**, raising the stakes of any injury taken,
  common in darker or more grounded systems (see the related death-and-
  respawn record for the most extreme version of this design axis).
- **Role-complementary teamwork**, where each team member's distinct
  capability (tank, healer, damage, support) creates genuine interdependence
  rather than redundant firepower.
- **Coordination-based teamwork**, less about fixed roles and more about
  timing and combination — abilities that specifically synergize when used
  together, rewarding tactical planning over raw stats.
- **Symmetric information**, where both sides of an encounter know roughly
  the same things about each other, common in straightforward confrontations.
- **Asymmetric information**, where scouting, deception, or hidden
  capabilities give one side a meaningful edge independent of raw power —
  closely related to the heist and mystery trope forms' fair-play
  principles when the story lets the reader in on the informational
  advantage.

## What it can look like on the page

- An injury's severity and recovery cost shown consistently with the
  system's established healing rules, rather than resolving conveniently
  when the plot needs a character back at full strength.
- Team members' distinct capabilities shown working together in a specific
  combination, rather than each character simply attacking independently.
- A team's informational advantage (or disadvantage) shown to meaningfully
  affect tactical decisions, not just narrated as a fact with no
  consequence.

## Progression, failure behavior, and cross-mechanic interaction

Healing rules interact directly with stakes and the death-and-respawn
pattern, since cheap healing functions similarly to a soft respawn in
lowering moment-to-moment mortal tension. Teamwork interacts with action
economy and with the found-family and supporting-cast-purpose patterns,
since a team member's specific tactical function often mirrors or reinforces
their narrative function within the cast. Information asymmetry interacts
with POV and information control, since the reader's own knowledge of each
side's information state shapes whether an encounter reads as suspenseful,
dramatic-ironic, or a fair tactical puzzle.

## Common failure modes

- **Healing that's conveniently available whenever the plot needs it**,
  undermining any stakes an injury was meant to carry.
- **Teamwork that's really just parallel individual combat**, where team
  members fight in proximity without any tactical interdependence or
  combination.
- **An informational advantage that's narrated but doesn't affect
  outcomes**, stating that a team knows something useful without that
  knowledge changing any tactical decision or result.

## Questions for the author

- Does healing in this manuscript follow consistent rules, or does it
  resolve conveniently whenever the plot needs a character restored?
- Do team members' distinct capabilities combine tactically, or does the
  team function as several characters fighting the same fight in parallel?
- If one side has an informational advantage, does that advantage
  meaningfully change what happens, or is it narrated without consequence?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Audit healing events against the system's established rules for cost and
   availability; flag any that resolve conveniently outside those rules.
2. Find a fight scene where team members act independently and try adding
   one moment of genuine tactical combination between two characters'
   distinct capabilities.
3. For a stated informational advantage, trace its effect on the actual
   tactical outcome; add a consequence if it currently has none.

## When this advice does not apply

- Solo-protagonist stories with no team combat don't need the teamwork half
  of this pattern developed.
- A story deliberately depicting a team that fights poorly together, as an
  examined weakness or a stage in their development, is using the
  dysfunction as a narrative point rather than an unexamined flaw.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where a project explicitly tracks healing costs, team composition, or
informational state linked to scenes, consistency checks are close to
deterministic. Whether teamwork reads as genuinely complementary, and
whether an informational advantage meaningfully affects outcomes, requires
interpreting the prose and remains model-assisted.

## Original micro-examples

*Parallel combat, no synergy:* Three team members fight the same group of
enemies in the same scene, each independently, with no moment where their
distinct capabilities combine into something none of them could do alone.

*Genuine teamwork:* The same three members instead execute a specific
combination — one draws an enemy's attention and positions them over
terrain a second character has prepared, letting the third land a decisive
strike that wouldn't have been possible without the setup — the team's
distinct roles visibly interlock.
