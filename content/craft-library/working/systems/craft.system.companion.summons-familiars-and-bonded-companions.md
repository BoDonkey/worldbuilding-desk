## What it is

A bonded companion is a creature or entity the system attaches to a
character: a summoned beast, a familiar, a contracted spirit, a tamed
monster, a system-granted partner. What distinguishes it from an ordinary
ally is provenance — the relationship is mechanical, often exclusive, and
frequently something the protagonist acquired rather than earned socially.

Every such record faces the same fork, and the story is largely determined by
which way it goes: the companion is a capability the protagonist deploys, or
a person the protagonist is in a relationship with. Both work. Drifting
between them is what produces the genre's characteristic awkwardness, where a
creature with dialogue and preferences is nonetheless described as being
levelled up.

## Why readers may care

Companions are among the most beloved elements in this genre, and the reason
is not tactical. A bonded creature supplies uncomplicated attachment,
witnessed growth alongside the protagonist's, and a relationship whose terms
are clear — which is restful in a story otherwise full of negotiation.

They also solve a practical problem: a solitary protagonist has nobody to
talk to. A companion permits dialogue, reaction, and interiority made
external, without requiring the protagonist to trust another person.

## Common forms and variants

- **The summon** — called and dismissed, existing at the character's
  discretion, which makes it closest to a capability.
- **The familiar** — persistent, usually with some autonomy and often a
  shared sense or link.
- **The contracted entity** — a bargain with terms, where the companion has
  its own agenda and the relationship is negotiated. Overlaps the oaths
  record.
- **The tamed or raised creature** — acquired young or defeated and kept,
  where the acquisition itself carries moral weight the story may or may not
  address.
- **The bonded equal** — a companion of comparable standing, closer to a
  party member than a possession.
- **The unwilling bond** — a companion who did not choose this, which is the
  most dramatically loaded version and the least often taken seriously.

## What it can look like on the page

- A companion who wants something the protagonist does not, and says so.
- Growth on the companion's own track, with its own costs and choices.
- A summon deployed tactically and described in the same register as a spell,
  consistently.
- Conversely: a creature with a name, jokes, and opinions whose death or
  dismissal the narrative treats as a resource loss.

## Common failure modes

- **The talking tool.** A companion characterized enough to be a person and
  used exclusively as an ability.
- **Unexamined acquisition.** A bond formed by capture, coercion, or system
  fiat, in a story that has given its companion a mind and never asks whether
  it consented.
- **Power laundering.** The companion carries the protagonist's fights, so
  growth is displaced onto an asset rather than demonstrated.
- **The absent companion.** Present in exposition, forgotten in scenes,
  reappearing when useful.
- **No cost of bonding.** A companion that arrives free, costs nothing to
  maintain, and risks nothing — which makes it pure upside and structurally
  inert.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

Whether the companion grows on its own track is the decision that most
changes the story. A companion that levels with the protagonist is a
capability; one that grows through its own experiences, choices, and
setbacks is a second character arc, and it will compete for the reader's
attention — often successfully.

The failure state deserves the same care. If a summon can be dismissed and
recalled intact, its defeat costs mana; if a bonded companion can die, every
fight it enters carries different weight. Systems frequently permit the first
while writing the companion as though the second were true, and readers feel
the mismatch even when they cannot name it.

Visibility is worth deciding too: can others perceive the bond? A visible
bond makes the protagonist legible — people know what they are and what they
can do — while a concealed one supports reveals and misdirection. And
socially, a world where creatures can be bound to people has an economy in
them: breeders, brokers, contracts, welfare arguments, and people who think
the whole practice is monstrous. That last group is almost always the most
interesting and almost never present.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Companions are an action-economy exploit by definition — they add actions per
turn — and any system that permits several has to price that. Other standard
exploits: using a summon as a disposable scout because it can be recalled,
bonding a creature purely for a passive bonus, or acquiring companions faster
than the story can characterize them. Where companions grant the protagonist
capabilities, the bond is functionally a perk and interacts with that record;
where companions are contracted, it interacts with oaths and bindings, and
the terms of the contract are worth writing out.

## Questions for the author

- Is this companion a capability or a person? Does the prose treat it
  consistently as whichever it is?
- What does the companion want that the protagonist does not?
- Did it choose this bond, and does the story ever ask?
- What does the bond cost — to form, to maintain, to break?
- If the companion died, would the loss be grief or expense? Does the book
  agree with itself about that?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write a scene where the companion refuses. If refusal is not possible,
   the story has answered the capability-or-person question already.
2. Give the companion one goal of its own and follow it for a chapter.
3. Remove the companion from a fight it usually decides, and see what the
   protagonist can actually do.
4. Write the character who objects to the practice of bonding, and see
   whether the setting gets more interesting.

## When this advice does not apply

- Systems where summons are explicitly non-sentient constructs, and treating
  them as capabilities is both consistent and intended.
- Stories where the companion is the co-protagonist and the party record
  governs instead.
- Comedic treatments where a companion's personality is a running joke rather
  than a claim about its interiority.
- Cultivation settings with spirit beasts, where the tradition carries its own
  conventions about bonding that this record should not overwrite.

## Evidence and detection limits

A coach can observe whether a companion has attributed dialogue, how often it
appears in scenes relative to being mentioned, and whether it is described
using capability vocabulary or character vocabulary — that last is a genuinely
useful signal for the consistency question this record raises, though it is
an inference and not a measurement.

What cannot be determined is which treatment the author intends. A companion
described as a resource may be a deliberate portrait of a character who thinks
that way, which is a different book from one that has not noticed. The coach
should surface the inconsistency as a question about intent.

## Original micro-examples

*Talking tool:* Ash whined and pressed his head against her knee. Summoning
cost had dropped to 40 mana at Bond Rank 3, which made him considerably more
efficient to field.

*Person:* Ash would not go into the water, and no amount of coaxing moved
him, and in the end she went in alone and came out knowing something about
him she had not known before.
