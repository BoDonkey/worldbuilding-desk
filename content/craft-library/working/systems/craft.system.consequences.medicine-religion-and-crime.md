## What it is

This record applies the general systemic-social-consequences framework to
three more domains: medicine (how healing, disease, and mortality are
understood and treated in a setting where healing magic or restorative
power exists), religion (what a faith tradition claims, and how those
claims are complicated or confirmed by a setting where divine or spiritual
power is directly, verifiably real rather than a matter of belief), and
crime (what becomes possible, and what becomes policeable, when magic or
system-granted power can be used to steal, deceive, coerce, or kill in ways
mundane law was never built to address). These three are grouped together
because they share a common failure pattern: a setting's power system
exists, but medicine, religion, and crime all continue to function as
though it doesn't.

## Why readers may care

These three domains touch daily life more directly than governance or
grand institutions do, which means an unexamined default is often more
immediately noticeable to a reader — a world with reliable healing magic
where people still die routinely of treatable illness, or a religion whose
central claims are directly, verifiably true or false and yet functions
exactly like a real-world faith built on unprovable belief, can read as an
oversight rather than a deliberate choice. Engaging even one of these
domains, even briefly, signals that the setting's power system has been
thought through past its immediate use to the protagonist.

## Common forms and variants

- **Medicine transformed by healing magic**, where access to restorative
  power is itself an economic and social stratifier — who can afford
  healing, who has access, and what conventional medicine still handles
  because magic can't or won't.
- **Medicine untouched by magic**, a legitimate choice when a setting's
  power system doesn't include healing capability, or when healing magic
  is deliberately rare or limited — worth an explicit reason rather than
  simply being unaddressed.
- **Verified religion**, where divine power is directly, provably real,
  raising questions about what faith even means in a setting where belief
  isn't required to access divine favor — a rich and underused source of
  thematic material.
- **Contested or fragmented religion**, where divine power's reality is
  acknowledged but its meaning and moral implications remain genuinely
  disputed among different factions or traditions, preserving some of the
  interpretive uncertainty religion has in settings without verifiable
  divine power.
- **Magic-adapted crime and law enforcement**, where legal and policing
  structures have specifically evolved to address magically-enabled
  offenses (mind-reading as evidence, magical forgery detection,
  supernatural containment for dangerous offenders).
- **Unadapted crime and law**, a failure mode rather than a variant, where
  criminal law and enforcement mirror a non-magical historical system with
  no acknowledgment of what the setting's power system should have changed.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character's access to healing magic (or lack of it) shown to carry
  social or economic weight, rather than functioning as a uniformly
  available convenience.
- A religious tradition whose claims are treated as directly verifiable
  within the story's own logic, with characters engaging what that
  verifiability actually means for faith and doubt.
- A crime or law-enforcement scene that specifically accounts for what the
  setting's power system makes possible, rather than functioning
  identically to a mundane historical equivalent.

## Common failure modes

- **Healing magic with no social stratification**, where restorative power
  exists but is treated as universally, freely available with no economic
  or access implications.
- **Verifiable divine power treated like unprovable faith**, where a
  religion's directly confirmable claims are narrated with the same
  uncertainty and doubt structure as a real-world faith based on belief
  alone, with no acknowledgment of the difference.
- **Law enforcement untouched by magic**, where crime and policing mirror
  a mundane historical system despite the setting's power system implying
  they should function differently.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Each of these three domains has an obvious abuse that a story gains texture
by acknowledging. Medicine: if healing is purchasable, then health tracks
wealth, and someone is rationing, insuring, or counterfeiting it — and a
healer who can restore anything is also someone who can withhold it.
Religion: if divine power is verifiable, then faith becomes falsifiable, and
the interesting characters are the ones whose authority depends on a claim
the system could test — as well as those who have found how to produce the
signs without the substance. Crime: every capability the system grants is
also a criminal method, and every detection ability is also a surveillance
tool, so a setting with mind-reading has either an unusually invasive state
or a very good reason it does not.

The common edge case across all three is verification. Where power is real
and testable, institutions will build tests, and those tests will be gamed,
bribed, and occasionally wrong about someone the reader knows the truth
about — which is frequently the most dramatically useful failure available
here.

## Questions for the author

- If healing magic exists in this setting, does access to it carry social
  or economic weight, or is it treated as universally available?
- If divine or spiritual power is directly verifiable in this setting, does
  the story engage what that means for the nature of faith, or does
  religion function as though it weren't?
- Does crime and law enforcement in this setting account for what the
  power system makes possible, or does it mirror an unmodified historical
  equivalent?

## Revision or design experiments

1. If healing magic exists, add or find a scene showing unequal access to
   it, and let that inequality matter to a character.
2. If divine power is verifiable, find a scene where a character grapples
   with what that verifiability means — for doubt, for worship, for
   moral obligation — rather than treating faith as functioning identically
   to a real-world belief system.
3. For a crime or legal scene, check whether the setting's power system
   would plausibly have changed how the offense is committed, detected, or
   punished, and adjust if it currently doesn't.

## When this advice does not apply

- A setting deliberately keeping magic rare, hidden, or newly emerged
  hasn't had time for medicine, religion, or crime to adapt, and the
  absence of adaptation is accurate rather than an oversight.
- Very narrow, personal-stakes stories may deliberately keep their frame
  too small to engage these domains meaningfully, provided the setting
  doesn't imply a scale it never addresses.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a setting's medicine, religion, and crime plausibly reflect its
power system requires reading and interpreting the manuscript's
worldbuilding as a whole — a judgment no structured data substitutes for.

## Original micro-examples

*Unexamined medicine and religion:* A setting has reliable, common healing
magic, yet a secondary character dies of an easily curable illness with no
explanation for why magical treatment wasn't sought; the setting's temple
also preaches faith and doubt in a deity whose power is otherwise treated
as directly, unambiguously real and observable by everyone.

*Examined medicine and religion:* The same setting instead makes healing
magic's cost or scarcity explicit — the character who died couldn't afford
a healer, and that economic fact is shown to matter — and the temple's
teaching reframes "faith" as devotion and interpretation of a
verifiably real deity's ambiguous will, rather than belief in the deity's
existence itself.
