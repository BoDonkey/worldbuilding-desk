## What it is

A quest system is the machinery by which a story's system issues objectives
and pays for their completion: named quests with stated rewards, standing
bounties, class trials, dungeon clears, or an interface that simply announces
what is required next. Two design questions sit inside it. What does the
system ask for — and what does it give in return, on what schedule?

This record is about that machinery as a narrative system. The quest as a
*story shape* — departure, trials, return — is treated separately in this
library's trope record; the two interact but are not the same thing, and a
book can have an elaborate quest interface and no quest structure at all.

## Why readers may care

A reward schedule is a pacing instrument. Game-design literature treats
reward timing as one of the main levers on engagement, and the same
principle carries into fiction that displays its rewards: a reader who has
been shown a quest with a stated payout is now waiting for a specific,
legible event, and the story has borrowed against a promise it must honor.

Quests also externalize motivation, which is a real risk as well as a
convenience. A character who acts because a system told them to is easier to
move and harder to care about. The strongest use tends to be friction
between the two: the quest asks for something the character would not have
chosen, and the choice to accept or refuse is the scene.

## Common forms and variants

- **Issued quests** — an interface, guild, or patron states an objective and
  a reward. Clear, and the most likely to flatten motivation.
- **Discovered objectives** — the system recognizes something the character
  was already doing, which preserves agency while still paying out.
- **Standing bounties and contracts** — an economy of objectives available
  to anyone, which implies a labor market and competitors.
- **Hidden or conditional quests** — objectives revealed only on completion,
  which converts reward into surprise and removes the pacing promise.
- **Refusable and failing quests** — objectives that can be declined or lost,
  which is what makes accepting one a decision rather than a notification.

## What it can look like on the page

- A quest whose stated reward is exactly what the protagonist needs next,
  repeatedly, so the system reads as an author's delivery mechanism.
- A character weighing a quest's cost against what they actually want.
- Rewards arriving on a rhythm the reader begins to anticipate — and the
  effect when one is withheld.
- Other people in the world taking quests too, failing them, or refusing
  them because the pay is bad.

## Common failure modes

- **Rewards as apology.** Escalating payouts substituting for escalating
  difficulty, which is the fake-progression pattern arriving through the
  quest log.
- **The system as motivation.** Every action initiated by an objective the
  character did not choose, until the reader can no longer say what the
  protagonist wants.
- **Unrefusable quests.** Objectives that cannot be declined, which converts
  the protagonist into a courier and removes the decision from every scene.
- **Reward inflation.** Payouts that outpace the world's economy, quietly
  making everything earlier in the book worthless.
- **The unpaid promise.** A quest announced with a stated reward that the
  story forgets, which readers usually do not.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

Failure is the part most often left undesigned. If quests cannot be failed,
they are errands with a delay; if they can, the terms matter — a lost
deposit, a damaged reputation, a closed opportunity, a patron who now
considers the character unreliable. Visibility compounds this: a quest
system visible to everyone makes completion a public record, and public
records produce rankings, reputations, and people who game them. A private
system produces none of that, and a character's accomplishments must be
established socially some other way.

At the scale of a society, a standing quest economy implies employers,
brokers, insurers, and people whose living depends on objectives being
issued. If the system pays better than ordinary work, ordinary work is being
abandoned somewhere, and that is usually more interesting than the quest
itself.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Any stated reward invites optimization: taking only high-payout objectives,
farming repeatable ones, splitting or padding tasks to trigger completions,
or manufacturing the conditions a quest rewards. A world where nobody has
noticed the most obvious exploit reads as a game with one player. Quest
design also interacts directly with experience sources — if both grant
advancement, the story has two advancement economies and should know how
they relate — and with loot and inventory, since quest rewards are the
usual route by which items enter the world at a rate the economy did not
plan for.

## Questions for the author

- Who issues quests in this world, what do they get from issuing them, and
  can they be wrong?
- What happens if the protagonist declines? If nothing, is that intended?
- Does the reward schedule pay for difficulty, or for time?
- Would the protagonist do any of this without the system asking?
- What does an ordinary person in this world do with the quest system, and
  why is that not the story?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Remove the quest prompt from one scene and see whether the character
   still has a reason to act. If not, the objective is carrying motivation
   the character should be carrying.
2. Write the terms of a failure — what is actually lost — for the next three
   quests. Quests that turn out to have no failure state are errands.
3. List quest rewards in order and check whether the curve tracks
   difficulty or merely chapter number.
4. Draft a scene in which someone refuses a quest, and see whether the world
   has any response to that.

## When this advice does not apply

- Stories with no quest interface at all, where objectives arise from
  circumstance and this record's machinery is simply absent.
- Comedic and satirical treatments where an absurd or exploitative quest
  system is the joke.
- Subgenres whose promise is precisely the pleasure of an orderly objective
  loop, where interrogating motivation would break the contract the reader
  came for — worth doing knowingly rather than by default.
- Early-tutorial sections, where an unrefusable objective is doing
  legitimate onboarding work.

## Evidence and detection limits

Given a manuscript, a coach can count quests, extract stated rewards where
they are displayed, and chart their sequence — genuinely useful when a
system displays its terms in structured text. Reward-versus-chapter curves
can be computed from that.

Whether a reward is *earned* is a semantic judgment, not a computation. So
is whether motivation has been externalized: a character who accepts many
quests may be under-motivated or may be desperate, and the difference is in
the prose. Absence of a refusal scene is not evidence that refusal is
impossible.

## Original micro-examples

*Externalized motivation:* "Quest received: Retrieve the Ashen Key. Reward:
400 XP." He set out at once. — The character has not been given a reason,
only an instruction.

*Friction:* "Quest received: Retrieve the Ashen Key from the shrine at
Vehl." Vehl was where his sister was buried. He read the notice twice and
went to find out who had written it.

## Sources and confidence notes

Ernest Adams's textbook treatment of mission and level structure
([[src.book.adams-fundamentals-of-game-design]]) supplies the vocabulary for
objectives and reward economies; Jesse Schell
([[src.book.schell-art-of-game-design]]) supplies the treatment of reward
pacing and player motivation; Raph Koster ([[src.book.koster-theory-of-fun]])
supplies the argument that reward without increasing challenge stops holding
attention.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the handoff's second sense. The
game-design principles are well supported by three independent, established
sources. Their transfer to prose fiction — that a displayed reward creates a
narrative promise, that externalized motivation costs reader attachment — is
this drafting pass's synthesis, and none of the three sources discusses
fiction. The social and economic consequences described here follow this
library's own systemic-consequences reasoning rather than any cited source.
