## What it is

Perks, feats, and talents (terminology varies by setting) are discrete,
individually chosen character upgrades, distinct from continuous stat
growth — a character doesn't gradually accumulate them through use the way
a skill might improve with practice, but selects them at specific moments,
usually as a reward for a milestone (a level-up, a quest completion, a
tier advancement). Because each one is a discrete choice rather than a
continuous curve, they're a natural place for a story to dramatize a
character actively deciding what kind of capability to become, rather than
simply reporting that growth occurred.

## Why readers may care

A discrete choice moment is inherently more dramatizable than continuous
growth: a reader can watch a character weigh options and commit to one, and
that choice can reveal character (what does this pick say about what the
character values or fears) in a way passive stat growth can't. The risk is
that a system with many available perks can produce a wall of options that
functions more like an inventory list than a meaningful decision, unless
individual picks are shown to actually matter later.

## Common forms and variants

- **Milestone-gated single picks**, where a character chooses one perk at
  each major advancement, keeping the decision moments rare and weighty.
- **Frequent, smaller picks**, chosen more often but each individually
  minor, trading dramatic weight per choice for a broader sense of
  accumulating customization.
- **Prerequisite-gated talents**, where later picks require earlier ones,
  creating build paths that commit a character to a developing specialty
  over time.
- **Situational or triggered perks**, unlocked by specific in-story
  achievements or choices rather than milestone advancement alone, tying
  the mechanical reward directly to a narrative event.
- **Respecable picks**, allowing a character to reallocate past choices —
  interacts with the same identity-stakes questions covered in the
  classes-versus-skill-based-growth record's respec discussion.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character weighing two or more specific options at a choice point,
  with the deliberation revealing something about their values or
  priorities.
- A previously chosen perk becoming the specific solution to a later
  problem, demonstrating that the choice mattered beyond the moment it was
  made.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a list of acquired perks reported in a
  status update with no scene ever showing any of them chosen deliberately
  or mattering to a later outcome.

## Common failure modes

- **Perks as inventory**, reported rather than chosen, with no dramatized
  decision moment and no later payoff.
- **A choice with no real tradeoff**, where one option is obviously
  superior, removing the meaningful-choice value the discrete-pick
  structure is meant to provide.
- **Chosen perks that never resurface**, where a dramatized choice moment
  is never paid off by the perk actually mattering to a later scene.

## Visibility, consequences, and cross-mechanic interaction

Discrete upgrades are unusually easy to make visible, because they have
names. A named perk can be announced, listed, boasted about, recorded by a
guild, and recognized by an opponent who has fought someone with the same
one — which turns a mechanical choice into a social signature. If the story
prefers characters to be read through behavior rather than labels, the same
upgrades can stay private and surface only in what a character turns out to
be able to do. Both work; drifting between them is what confuses readers
about how legible this world is meant to be.

At the scale of a society, a system of named, selectable upgrades implies
training that teaches toward them, institutions that certify them, and a
market for the rare ones — and implies people whose chosen perks made them
unemployable, which is a texture progression settings often skip. Perks also
interact with specialization and respec: a system where selections are
permanent makes each one a commitment worth dramatizing, while freely
reassignable perks are closer to equipment, and are better treated as such
in the prose than given the weight of an identity choice.

## Questions for the author

- When a character gains a perk, feat, or talent, is the choice
  dramatized, or only reported?
- Do the available options at a choice point carry genuine tradeoffs, or is
  one obviously correct?
- Does a previously chosen perk resurface as the specific solution to a
  later problem?
- If the system allows respeccing, does that ever carry narrative weight,
  or is it treated as costless bookkeeping?

## Revision or design experiments

1. For a reported-only perk acquisition, consider converting it into a
   dramatized choice scene, especially if it's narratively significant.
2. Audit a choice point's available options for genuine tradeoffs; add a
   cost to an option that's currently strictly superior.
3. Trace a chosen perk forward and find (or add) a later scene where it's
   the specific solution to a problem.
4. If respeccing is available, find a scene where using it carries a
   narrative cost, rather than functioning as free reallocation.

## When this advice does not apply

- Very lightweight systems where perks function as minor flavor with no
  narrative weight expected may not need dramatized choice scenes for every
  pick.
- A story deliberately depicting a character's growth as externally
  imposed rather than chosen (a system that assigns perks without consent)
  is using the absence of choice as its own narrative point.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where a project explicitly tracks perk or talent acquisition linked to
scenes, whether a choice was dramatized or only reported is directly
observable from the text. Whether a chosen perk's later relevance is
adequately paid off requires tracing its use across the manuscript and
remains model-assisted.

## Original micro-examples

*Perks as inventory:* A status update lists three new perks gained after a
level-up, with no scene showing any of them chosen or later used in a way
that matters to the plot.

*Dramatized, paid-off choice:* A character instead deliberates between a
perk that improves offense and one that improves a companion's safety,
choosing the latter at a personal cost — and two chapters later, that exact
perk is what saves the companion's life in a scene the earlier choice
directly set up.
