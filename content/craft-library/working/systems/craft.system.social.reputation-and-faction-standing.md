## What it is

A reputation system tracks how a person is regarded — by a faction, a
settlement, a guild, or the world at large — and makes that regard
mechanically consequential: prices, access, quests offered, doors opened or
closed. Some systems display it as a value; others keep it as an in-world
social fact the interface never mentions.

This record is about that machinery. It is distinct from the institutional
record, which is about the organizations themselves, and from titles and
honorifics, which are markers a system awards. Reputation is the standing
those markers sit on, and it changes.

## Why readers may care

Reputation is the mechanism that makes a world respond. A protagonist whose
past actions are remembered inhabits a place with continuity; one who arrives
everywhere unknown inhabits a series of disconnected locations. That
continuity is much of what makes a long serial feel like one world rather
than a sequence of arcs.

It also creates a rare kind of stake. Most progression systems only go up.
Reputation can be lost, and lost in ways no amount of capability recovers,
which gives a story consequences that power cannot solve — a scarce and
valuable thing in a genre where power usually solves everything.

## Common forms and variants

- **Numeric standing** — a value per faction, legible and easy to game.
- **Tiered regard** — named states (hostile, neutral, honored) that behave
  like thresholds rather than pools.
- **Reputation as rumor** — no system value at all; standing is what people
  say, which travels imperfectly and can be wrong.
- **Mutually exclusive allegiance** — gains with one faction costing standing
  with another, which forces choice and is where the mechanic earns its keep.
- **Notoriety** — reputation that rises with infamy, opening some doors while
  closing others.
- **Localized versus global renown** — known in one town versus known
  everywhere, which changes what anonymity is worth.

## What it can look like on the page

- A merchant's price changing because of something the protagonist did
  chapters ago in a different city.
- A faction remembering a slight the protagonist has forgotten.
- A character declining a profitable job because of what taking it would cost
  them elsewhere.
- Conversely: standing that rises monotonically with every quest completed,
  so the mechanic never asks anything.

## Common failure modes

- **Reputation as a second experience bar.** Standing that only accumulates,
  with no opposed factions and nothing to lose.
- **Relationships as a farmable resource.** Characters treating people as
  standing dispensers, which the mechanic invites and the prose should
  notice.
- **Rumor that travels perfectly.** Everyone knowing everything instantly,
  which removes the interesting middle where a reputation is contested or
  outdated.
- **Standing without texture.** A number that changes nothing about how
  anyone speaks to the protagonist.
- **Unearned infamy.** A notoriety the story asserts but never dramatizes —
  no one crosses the street, no prices change, no doors close.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

The design question that matters most is whether standing can fall, and how
far. A system with no downward path is a reward track wearing a social
costume. A system where reputation can be destroyed — and where destruction
is not simply repaired by later heroics — makes social consequence real, and
gives a protagonist something they can genuinely lose.

Visibility is unusually rich here because reputation is inherently social.
It can be visible to the character (a displayed value), visible to others but
not to them (everyone knows what is said about them except them), or visible
only to the reader. The second is the most useful and least used: a
protagonist operating on a stale understanding of their own standing is a
reliable source of scenes.

The consequences reach into everything the institutional records describe.
Where standing determines access, someone is selling it — letters of
introduction, sponsorships, cleared records — and someone else is
manufacturing it. A world with formal reputation and no reputation trade has
skipped the obvious.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Numeric standing is among the most gameable mechanics in the genre: repeating
small favours, farming low-value tasks for a faction, buying goodwill,
laundering a bad reputation through an intermediary, or exploiting factions
that do not communicate. Where two factions oppose each other and both track
standing, playing both sides is the obvious move and someone in the world is
doing it professionally. Reputation also interacts with party and guild
structure — a group's standing and an individual's may diverge, which is a
strong source of internal conflict — and with titles, which are usually
reputation crystallized into something portable.

## Questions for the author

- Can this character's standing fall, and has it?
- Which two factions in this world make gains with one cost standing with the
  other?
- Does anyone treat the protagonist differently because of their reputation
  in a scene where it is inconvenient?
- How fast does news travel here, and who benefits from its being slow?
- What does the protagonist believe their reputation is, and are they right?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write the scene where reputation costs something the protagonist wanted,
   and see whether the mechanic starts carrying weight.
2. Name two factions whose regard is genuinely opposed. If none exist,
   reputation is currently a single accumulating track.
3. Give one character a stale or mistaken view of the protagonist's standing
   and let a scene run on the gap.
4. Trace one rumor's route through the world explicitly — who told whom — and
   see whether the setting's information economy holds up.

## When this advice does not apply

- Wandering or episodic structures where the protagonist deliberately leaves
  consequences behind, and continuity of regard would work against the form.
- Stories with no formal standing system, where reputation is ordinary social
  fact and needs no mechanical treatment.
- Early chapters where the protagonist is genuinely unknown and the absence
  of standing is the situation being established.
- Solo and isolated premises where there is no society to be regarded by.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where standing is reported numerically, a coach can extract values, chart
them over the manuscript, and note whether any faction's value ever
decreases — a near-deterministic check, and a revealing one, since
monotonic reputation is the failure mode this record names first.

Beyond that, interpretation. Whether characters *treat* the protagonist
consistently with their stated standing is a semantic judgment requiring the
scenes to be read. The absence of a reputation value is not evidence that a
world lacks social consequence; many settings handle it entirely in prose,
and handle it well.

## Original micro-examples

*Second experience bar:* Reputation with the Ashford Concord: 4,200
(Honored). It had risen after every job. Nothing in the town had changed
except the greeting.

*Standing with a cost:* the Concord raised him to Honored the same week the
river guilds stopped answering his letters, and the river guilds owned the
only boats going north.
