## What it is

Many progression systems assume groups: a party that shares experience, a
guild that holds resources and rank, a raid that requires specific roles. As
soon as the system formalizes a group, the group is a mechanic — it has
composition rules, a way of dividing what it earns, a way of admitting and
expelling people, and a set of obligations members owe each other.

This record treats those structures as system design. It is distinct from
the ensemble-cast trope record, which is about how a story handles a group of
characters on the page, and from the teamwork material inside the encounter
records, which is about how a group functions inside a fight. This one is
about the rules the group runs on.

## Why readers may care

A formalized group converts social life into something with terms, and terms
can be disputed. Who gets the drop? Who was carried? Who is owed a favour?
Research on guilds in online games describes obligation and scheduling
pressure becoming real burdens for real players — play acquiring the texture
of work. Fiction that takes its group structures seriously inherits a rich
source of conflict that requires no antagonist at all.

Groups also solve a structural problem for progression stories: they supply
characters whose competence is legible on the same scale as the
protagonist's, which is one of the few reliable ways to keep a rapidly
advancing protagonist in scenes with genuine peers.

## Common forms and variants

- **The fixed party** — a small group with complementary roles, usually the
  book's core cast, where composition is a character question as much as a
  tactical one.
- **The guild or clan** — a standing institution with rank, resources, and
  politics, which outlives any particular party and can outrank the
  protagonist.
- **Ad hoc grouping** — temporary alliances formed per objective, which
  produces negotiation, mistrust, and short-term incentives.
- **Formal role systems** — where the system itself names roles (tank,
  healer, support) and composition rules become explicit constraints.
- **The solo exception** — a protagonist the system disadvantages for
  working alone, or advantages, either of which is a strong statement about
  what the world rewards.

## What it can look like on the page

- An argument about a split that is really an argument about who matters.
- A character kept in the party for reasons that are social rather than
  tactical, and the cost of that.
- Guild rank determining who may speak in a scene.
- A group whose members never disagree about anything, which usually means
  the group is functioning as one character with several names.

## Common failure modes

- **The frictionless party.** A group with no disagreement about risk,
  reward, or direction — pleasant, and a large source of conflict left
  unused.
- **Roles as labels.** Characters named as tank or healer whose behavior in
  scenes is indistinguishable from each other's.
- **Undesigned distribution.** Loot and experience shared by unstated rules,
  so the reader cannot tell whether a division is generous or a slight.
- **The guild that never asks.** An institution that supplies resources,
  training, and status and never requires anything inconvenient in return.
- **Group as audience.** Companions present to witness and admire the
  protagonist's growth rather than to have any of their own.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

Group structures need failure states as much as individual ones. A party can
be wiped, disbanded, or hollowed out by a single loss; a guild can be
politically captured, bankrupted, or disgraced. Deciding in advance what a
group's failure looks like usually improves it, because a group that cannot
fail is scenery.

Visibility is largely social here. Rank, membership, and reputation are the
most public facts about most characters in these settings — a guild badge is
read at a glance in a way a stat sheet may not be — which makes group
identity the fastest way for a stranger to form an opinion. That in turn
makes expulsion a serious event and forged or bought membership worth doing.

Systemically, formal groups are institutions, and everything the
institutions record says about labor and governance applies: guilds set
prices, restrict entry, license practitioners, and lobby whoever governs.
A world with powerful guilds and no guild politics has left something on
the table.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Shared-reward rules are the classic exploit surface: leeching on a group's
work, inflating contribution, forming a party purely to qualify for a split,
or disbanding immediately before a payout. Where the system grants group
bonuses, characters will assemble to farm the bonus rather than to work
together. Group mechanics also interact sharply with action economy —
numbers are the most direct action-economy advantage available — and with
information asymmetry, since a party is a structure for sharing knowledge
and therefore also a structure someone can withhold within.

## Questions for the author

- What are this group's actual rules about splitting what it earns, and does
  everyone in it agree on them?
- What does the guild want from its members, and what happens to someone who
  will not give it?
- If this party lost its least important member, what would break?
- Does anyone in this group want something the others do not?
- Why is the protagonist in this group rather than a better one — and does
  the group know that?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write the party's distribution rule as one sentence, then find the scene
   where it is tested. If there is no such scene, there may be a good one
   available.
2. Give one companion an objective that conflicts mildly with the party's,
   and follow it for a chapter.
3. Draft the guild's requirements as a short list of obligations. Any
   institution that asks nothing can usually be made more interesting by
   asking for one thing.
4. Remove the protagonist from a group scene and see whether the others have
   anything to do.

## When this advice does not apply

- Deliberately solo stories, where isolation is the promise and a party
  would dilute it.
- Stories where the group is a settled, unexamined comfort — a found-family
  promise that readers arrive for specifically, and which examining too
  hard would spoil.
- Early-series sections where the group is still forming and its rules have
  not yet been established in-world.

## Evidence and detection limits

A coach can observe group composition, how often each member speaks or acts,
which members appear in which scenes, and whether stated distribution rules
recur consistently. Speaking-share across a group is countable and often
revealing.

It cannot determine whether a companion is a character or an audience —
that is a semantic judgment about function. Nor can it conclude from
harmony that a group is underused: some books are deliberately about people
who trust each other, and the coach should ask rather than diagnose.

## Original micro-examples

*Group as audience:* "Nice one," said Bel. "That was incredible," said Roan.
The three of them looked at the crater he had made.

*Group as system:* Bel wanted the core sold and the money split four ways.
Roan wanted it kept for the guild's tithe, because Roan intended to be
promoted. The core sat between them on the table while they worked out which
of them was going to lose.

scheduling pressure, and play acquiring the character of labor; Ernest Adams
([[src.book.adams-fundamentals-of-game-design]]) supplies the design

([[src.book.schell-art-of-game-design]]) supplies the treatment of player
motivation within groups.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the handoff's second sense. Yee describes
real players in real online games — not fictional depictions of them — so the
transfer to how a novel's guild should behave is this drafting pass's
synthesis, and no claim is made that fiction readers respond as players do.
The institutional and economic consequences follow this library's own
systemic-consequences reasoning. No source cited here addresses prose
fiction directly.
