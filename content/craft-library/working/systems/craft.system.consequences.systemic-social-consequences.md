## What it is

If a setting's power system enables something transformative — reliable
resurrection, teleportation, mass communication via magic, or widely
accessible power progression itself — a society that has had access to that
capability for any meaningful length of time should show some trace of
having organized around it: institutions, economies, laws, religions, and
daily habits that would plausibly form in response. A setting where wizards
have existed for centuries but daily life, governance, and economy look
otherwise indistinguishable from a world with no magic at all reads, to
attentive readers, as unexamined rather than deliberately conservative.

## Why readers may care

Worldbuilding that traces a power system's second- and third-order effects
signals a setting that's been thought through, and it also opens genuine
plot material a purely personal-power-focused story leaves untouched:
institutions built around a mechanic can be corrupt, contested, or
collapsing in ways that generate conflict independent of any individual
character's strength. A setting that ignores these effects isn't
automatically bad, but it forecloses an entire category of story the system
itself was implicitly offering.

## Common forms and variants

- **Institutional adaptation**, where governance, law, or religion has
  visibly organized around the power system (licensing for spellcasters, a
  church built around a resurrection mechanic's theology).
- **Economic adaptation**, where markets, labor, and class structures
  reflect who has access to power and who doesn't.
- **Military and political adaptation**, where warfare, diplomacy, and
  borders reflect what the power system makes possible or moot (walls matter
  less if teleportation is common, for instance).
- **Deliberately limited adaptation**, where the story explicitly explains
  why society hasn't organized around a capability as much as one might
  expect (the ability is new, rare, deliberately suppressed, or too
  dangerous to institutionalize) — a legitimate variant provided the
  limiting factor is stated rather than simply absent.
- **Uneven adaptation**, where some sectors of society have reorganized
  around the power system and others conspicuously haven't, which can itself
  be a source of tension (a magical elite alongside an unchanged peasantry).

## What it can look like on the page

- An institution, law, custom, or economic structure explicitly shaped by
  the setting's power system, treated as unremarkable background by
  characters who live with it.
- A character's plan or problem running into a systemic consequence of the
  power system (a licensing law, a guild monopoly, a resurrection-related
  inheritance dispute) rather than only personal-scale obstacles.
- The inverse, as a warning sign: a power capable of reshaping society
  (mass communication, resurrection, cheap teleportation) that the setting
  treats as though it barely exists outside the protagonist's own use of it.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

A power system's social consequences interact directly with its access
rules (see the related resource-lifecycle-design pattern): a universally
accessible power reshapes society very differently than one restricted to a
narrow elite. They also interact with the crafting/economy loop pattern,
since materials, goods, and services tied to the power system imply their
own markets, labor, and scarcity dynamics. Worth considering explicitly:
does the power system change warfare enough that borders, walls, or armies
function differently? Does it change medicine, education, or crime in ways
the story could engage even briefly?

## Common failure modes

- **An unexamined status quo**, where a transformative power exists but
  society looks unchanged from a version of the setting with no magic at
  all, with no explanation for why.
- **Isolated personal use**, where the power system matters only for the
  protagonist's individual advancement and never intersects with any larger
  social structure.
- **Inconsistent institutional logic**, where an institution is shown
  reacting to the power system in one scene and behaving as if it doesn't
  exist in another.

## Questions for the author

- If this world's central power (magic, cultivation, the system itself) has
  existed for generations, what institution, law, or custom would plausibly
  have formed around it?
- Does this power change warfare, medicine, governance, or economy in ways
  the story ever shows, even briefly?
- If society hasn't organized around an available power, is there a stated
  reason (rarity, suppression, danger), or is the absence simply unexamined?
- Is there an institution in this setting whose corruption, contestation, or
  collapse could generate conflict independent of the protagonist's personal
  power level?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Pick one transformative capability in the setting's power system and
   sketch one institution, law, or custom that would plausibly exist because
   of it, even if it never becomes plot-central.
2. Find a scene where the protagonist's plan could plausibly run into a
   systemic consequence of the power system (a law, a guild, a market) and
   test adding that friction.
3. If society has deliberately not organized around an available power,
   make the limiting reason explicit somewhere in the text rather than
   leaving it implicit.
4. Consider one sector of society that would be most disrupted by the
   power system (labor, medicine, the military) and see whether even one
   scene could show that disruption in the background.

## When this advice does not apply

- A young or recently emerged power system (a system apocalypse in its
  opening act, a newly awakened ability) hasn't had time to reshape
  institutions yet, and the absence of systemic adaptation is itself
  accurate rather than an oversight.
- Small-scale, personal-stakes stories may deliberately keep the frame
  narrow enough that broader systemic consequences are outside the story's
  scope, provided the setting doesn't imply a scale it never engages.
- A deliberately isolated or secret power (known only to a handful of
  characters) wouldn't be expected to have reshaped society yet, by design.

## Evidence and detection limits

Whether a setting's social, economic, and political structures plausibly
reflect its power system requires reading and interpreting a manuscript's
worldbuilding as a whole — a judgment no structured data substitutes for. A
coach can prompt the author with the diagnostic questions above but cannot
verify systemic plausibility from text alone.

## Original micro-examples

*Unexamined status quo:* A setting where resurrection has been reliably
available for three hundred years still has grief, funeral customs, and
inheritance law that read identically to a setting with no resurrection
magic at all, with no acknowledgment of the discrepancy.

*Examined consequence:* The same setting instead has an entrenched, wealthy
"Second-Life Guild" controlling resurrection access, inheritance law
explicitly rewritten to account for the possibility of a decedent's return,
and a black market in unauthorized resurrections that the plot can draw on
directly.
