## What it is

Appraisal is any ability that converts a thing into information: a skill that
reads an enemy's level, an identify spell that names an item's properties, an
interface that labels what the character is looking at. It is close to
universal in the genre and is very rarely designed rather than assumed.

The reason it deserves a record is that it decides what scenes are possible.
Every mystery, negotiation, ambush, and bad bargain depends on somebody not
knowing something. An unrestricted appraisal ability removes that condition
across the whole book, usually without the author noticing at the moment they
grant it.

## Why readers may care

Uncertainty is what makes a decision a decision. When a character can see an
opponent's exact statistics before engaging, the fight becomes arithmetic;
when they cannot, it becomes judgment. Neither is wrong — the first supports
tactical, planning-heavy stories and the second supports tense ones — but
they are different books, and the appraisal rules pick which.

Appraisal also determines whether the world can lie. In a setting where
identification is reliable and cheap, deception between characters largely
stops working, and with it a whole family of plots. Deciding what appraisal
*cannot* see is therefore one of the highest-leverage decisions in a system.

## Common forms and variants

- **Level-reading** — seeing an opponent's tier or power. Fast, and the most
  likely to flatten encounters into comparison.
- **Item identification** — revealing properties of objects, often gated by
  cost, skill, or a specialist.
- **Graded appraisal** — information proportional to the appraiser's skill
  relative to the target, so strong things read as unknown. The most useful
  variant, because it preserves uncertainty exactly where the stakes are
  highest.
- **Professional appraisal** — no personal ability; identification is a
  service someone sells, which makes it an economy and a social relationship.
- **Fallible appraisal** — results that can be wrong, spoofed, or resisted,
  which restores deception without removing the mechanic.
- **Contextual limits** — appraisal that requires proximity, time, line of
  sight, or consent.

## What it can look like on the page

- A character choosing not to appraise, because doing so is detectable.
- An appraisal returning "unknown," and the scene reorganizing around that.
- A merchant whose whole living is knowing what appraisal does not show.
- Conversely: every encounter opening with a stat readout, so the reader
  learns the outcome before the fight does.

## Common failure modes

- **Unlimited appraisal.** Granted early, never bounded, quietly deleting
  mystery, negotiation, and ambush for the rest of the series.
- **Appraisal as exposition delivery.** The ability existing so the narration
  can state facts, which makes it a device rather than a mechanic.
- **Selective forgetting.** The protagonist not appraising when the plot needs
  them surprised, which readers notice faster than almost any other
  inconsistency.
- **Information without cost.** No time, mana, risk, or detectability, so the
  ability is never a decision.
- **Nobody else has it.** A world where reliable appraisal exists and its
  economics — insurance, authentication, fraud — are untouched.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

The single most useful design decision is what appraisal returns when it
fails: nothing, partial data, a wrong answer, or a warning that the target
exceeds the appraiser. Each produces different scenes, and "unknown" against
a stronger target is the version that keeps danger legible while preserving
tension.

Visibility runs both ways here, which is unusual and underused. Can the
target tell they are being appraised? If so, appraisal becomes a social act —
rude, aggressive, or revealing of the appraiser's own capability — and
characters will conceal that they are doing it. If not, it is silent
surveillance, and the story should consider what a world of undetectable
mutual assessment feels like to live in.

Consequentially, reliable identification restructures commerce and law.
Contracts need no trust, fraud requires defeating the system rather than a
person, insurers price by appraisal, and someone sells authentication.
Settings usually grant the ability and leave the institutions untouched,
which is a large piece of available worldbuilding.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

The standard exploits are appraisal-spoofing items, suppressing one's own
readable statistics, using a low-level proxy to appraise safely, and mass
appraisal to price a market faster than its participants can. Any resistance
mechanic immediately creates a counter-industry. Appraisal interacts most
sharply with the visible-versus-hidden-systems comparison — appraisal is the
in-world mechanism by which a "hidden" system becomes partly visible — and
with information asymmetry in encounters, since a party with one appraiser
has a knowledge distribution problem of its own.

## Questions for the author

- What can appraisal not see, and was that decided or defaulted?
- What does using it cost, and can the target tell?
- What is the last scene that depended on someone not knowing something? Is
  it still possible under these rules?
- Who sells this service, and to whom?
- If appraisal is fallible, has the reader ever seen it be wrong?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write the rule for what appraisal returns against something stronger than
   the appraiser. That one sentence usually settles the mechanic.
2. Take a scene that turns on a surprise and check whether appraisal should
   have prevented it.
3. Make one appraisal detectable by its target and see what the scene
   becomes.
4. Write the appraiser who does this professionally, and what they know that
   the skill does not tell them.

## When this advice does not apply

- Settings with no appraisal mechanic, where knowledge is ordinary
  observation and this record does not apply.
- Deliberately tactical fiction whose promise is exactly the planning that
  full information enables — a real contract, worth honoring knowingly.
- Stories where the system's omniscience is the subject rather than a tool,
  and its intrusiveness is the point.
- Early chapters where the protagonist's inability to appraise is the
  situation being established.

## Evidence and detection limits

Where appraisal results appear as structured text, a coach can count them,
chart their frequency, and check whether stated limits hold — for instance,
whether something previously described as unreadable is later read without
explanation. That consistency check is close to deterministic and is the most
valuable thing available here.

Whether appraisal has flattened a scene is interpretive. A stat readout
before a fight may be doing legitimate work in a tactical story. The coach
should report where appraisal appears and where stated rules appear to bend,
and ask about intent rather than declaring a mechanic overpowered.

## Original micro-examples

*Unbounded:* "Appraisal: Vashka Deepwarden, Level 61, Weakness: fire." He had
never met her, and now the fight had been decided in one line.

*Bounded:* "Appraisal: — ." The blank was itself the information, and it was
the only thing the skill had ever refused him.
