## What it is

Distance is a constraint, and the rules that govern crossing it — road speed,
mounts, portals, teleport skills, waypoint networks — determine what a
character can attempt in a week. This record treats travel as a system:
what it costs, who controls it, and what a setting looks like once it becomes
cheap.

The decision that matters most is whether fast travel exists. It is usually
granted for convenience, to stop the story spending chapters on roads, and it
is one of the largest worldbuilding decisions a setting can make almost by
accident.

## Why readers may care

Travel time is where a plan acquires a price. If reaching the capital takes
three weeks, then everything happening in the capital happens without the
protagonist, allies elsewhere are genuinely out of reach, and a decision to
go is a decision not to be somewhere else. Remove the time and you remove the
tradeoff.

Distance also produces one of the most reliable sources of tension available:
being far from something that matters. A message that takes ten days is a
plot; an instant one is a phone call.

## Common forms and variants

- **Ordinary travel** — roads, mounts, ships, weather. Slow, textured, and
  the source of most incidental encounter structures.
- **Waypoint networks** — fixed points connected instantly, which preserves
  distance between points while collapsing it along the network. The most
  design-friendly form, because it creates a map of privileged places.
- **Personal teleportation** — a character-level ability, which is the
  version most likely to break plotting, since it removes distance for one
  person only.
- **Gated fast travel** — instant movement at a cost: money, mana, a
  consumable, a cooldown, a licence.
- **One-way or unreliable movement** — travel that cannot be reversed or
  cannot be trusted, which makes each use a commitment.
- **Territory control** — movement restricted by who holds the ground, which
  converts geography into politics.

## What it can look like on the page

- A plan changed because of how long the road takes.
- News arriving late, and a character acting on information that is no longer
  true.
- A waypoint town that exists because the waypoint does.
- Conversely: distances that stretch and compress to fit the chapter, which
  teaches the reader not to hold the map in mind.

## Common failure modes

- **Elastic distance.** Journeys taking however long the plot needs, the
  single most common consistency failure in secondary worlds.
- **Teleportation without consequence.** Instant movement granted to the
  protagonist and to no institution, army, merchant, or courier in the
  setting.
- **Travel as dead time.** Weeks of road narrated because they happened,
  rather than because anything does.
- **The unused map.** Elaborate geography that never constrains a decision.
- **Logistics ignored.** Armies, caravans, and sieges that move as though
  supply were not the hardest part of any of them.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

Travel is one of the few mechanics whose failure states are naturally
interesting: getting lost, arriving late, being cut off, having a route
closed behind you. A system with no way to fail at travel has converted its
geography into a menu.

Visibility matters mostly for fast travel. If waypoints are public, they are
infrastructure — taxed, guarded, queued, and closed in emergencies. If a
character's ability to move instantly is private, it is a weapon, and their
knowing they can leave any room changes how they behave in every room.

The consequences are the largest of any mechanic in this record set, because
cheap movement restructures everything: trade goes long-distance, perishables
become viable, empires become governable at a distance, armies concentrate
faster than they can be met, and local scarcity stops meaning anything.
Settings routinely grant teleportation and keep a medieval economy, which is
the clearest case in the genre of a system's implications going unexamined.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

Cheap movement is an arbitrage engine: buy where a thing is common, sell
where it is rare, and any world with both teleportation and regional prices
has someone doing that at scale. Other standard edges are using fast travel
to escape any losing fight, scouting with impunity, and delivering something
dangerous instantly. Travel interacts directly with the currency and market
record — distance is what makes prices differ in the first place — and with
institutions, since whoever controls the network controls the economy that
runs on it.

## Questions for the author

- How long does the most-used journey in this story take, and has that been
  consistent?
- What can the protagonist reach in a day, and what is out of reach?
- Who else has access to fast travel, and what have they built with it?
- What does it cost to move something heavy, and does anyone in the story
  care?
- If teleportation were removed, which plots would stop working — and are
  those plots better or worse for it?

## Revision or design experiments

1. Write down the three most common journeys and their durations, then check
   the draft against the list. Elastic distance shows up immediately.
2. Take one instant journey and make it take a week. Note what the delay
   generates.
3. Write the merchant who uses the waypoint network professionally, and let
   the setting acquire an economy.
4. Close a route behind the characters and see what the map becomes when it
   is not fully connected.

## When this advice does not apply

- Single-location stories — a city, a school, a tower — where travel is not
  a live mechanic.
- Deliberate convenience, where a story's promise is momentum and readers
  arrive for the scenes rather than the roads.
- Dungeon-centred fiction, where the interesting geography is bounded and
  vertical; the dungeon record governs that case.
- Comedic and picaresque work where impossible logistics are part of the
  register.

## Evidence and detection limits

Stated journey durations are extractable and comparable, which makes elastic
distance one of the more genuinely detectable problems in this library: the
same route taking three days in one chapter and three weeks in another is a
contradiction a coach can surface without interpretation, subject to the
obvious caveats about differing conditions, routes, and unreliable narrators.

What cannot be computed is whether a journey should have been dramatized or
skipped, or whether cheap travel has cost the story more than it saved. Those
are judgments about the book's intent. The coach's useful contribution is the
consistency check and the question about who else uses the network.

## Original micro-examples

*Elastic:* the ride to Kell took most of a month in chapter nine. In chapter
thirty-one they need to be there by the festival, and it takes four days.

*Costed:* the waypoint to Kell cost eleven silver, which was most of what
they had, and it went one way, and they spent the ride to the platform
arguing about whether they would be able to afford to come back.
