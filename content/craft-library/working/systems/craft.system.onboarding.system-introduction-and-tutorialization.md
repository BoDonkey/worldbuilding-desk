## What it is

A story built on a power system has to teach that system to its reader: what
the rules are, what the numbers mean, what is at stake when a value changes.
Onboarding is how and when that teaching happens. It competes for space with
everything else the opening chapters must do, which is what makes it a
design problem rather than a matter of writing a clear explanation.

The genre has a characteristic solution — the tutorial sequence, in which a
character new to the system is taught it in-world — and a characteristic
failure, in which the story stops so the system can be described.

## Why readers may care

Game design has converged on a principle worth borrowing carefully: players
learn a system best while using it, and worst while being told about it
outside of play. Koster's account of fun as the sensation of learning
suggests why — the pleasure is in acquiring a pattern, and being handed the
pattern outright removes it.

Fiction has the same shape and one extra constraint. A reader can put the
book down. A tutorial that pauses the story spends the reader's earliest and
least-secured attention on material that has not yet been made to matter.
Where onboarding works, the reader learns the rules from consequences they
cared about, and the teaching is invisible.

## Common forms and variants

- **The naive protagonist** — a character who does not know the system
  either, so explanation is motivated. Effective and overused; the risk is a
  companion who exists only to answer.
- **In-world instruction** — an academy, a mentor, an orientation: justified
  exposition, at the cost of chapters spent in a classroom.
- **Learning by consequence** — the character misuses the system, something
  goes wrong, and the rule is legible in the wreckage.
- **Progressive disclosure** — introducing one mechanic at a time as the
  story requires it, so the system is never explained wholesale.
- **Deferred explanation** — rules withheld and inferred, trading
  comprehension for mystery; workable only while the stakes stay legible.

## What it can look like on the page

- A first chapter that establishes one rule and its cost, rather than the
  system's full taxonomy.
- An interface panel appearing at a moment when the reader already wants the
  information it contains.
- A character asking the question the reader is currently asking, rather than
  asking one purely so it can be answered.
- Conversely: several pages of nested system description before anything has
  happened; a companion whose dialogue is entirely explanatory.

## Common failure modes

- **The rules dump.** The system explained comprehensively before the reader
  has any reason to care about any part of it.
- **Teaching what is not yet needed.** Mechanics introduced chapters before
  they matter, so the reader must hold them without use.
- **The explaining companion.** A character whose function is exposition,
  who is recognizable as such and rarely survives it as a person.
- **Unmotivated ignorance.** A protagonist who does not know things they
  plainly would, so that someone can tell them.
- **Onboarding that never ends.** New rules arriving at the same rate in
  chapter eighty as in chapter three, so the reader never reaches
  competence.

## Progression, failure behavior, visibility, and consequences

Onboarding has a natural end, and deciding where it falls is a real design
choice: the point at which the reader can predict what the system will do.
Past that point, further explanation reads as padding, and the story should
be spending on consequences instead. A system that keeps adding rules
indefinitely never lets the reader feel competent, which forfeits the
mastery pleasure the genre is largely built on.

Visibility determines what onboarding even means. A system visible to
characters can be taught in-world, and the tutorial is a scene; a system
visible only to the reader must be taught by inference, and the "tutorial"
is a matter of narration. Where the system is hidden from both, onboarding
becomes the mystery plot itself.

Socially, a system old enough to be understood implies teachers, manuals,
apprenticeships, and disputed interpretations. A system that arrived
recently — an apocalypse premise — has none of these, and its onboarding
problem belongs to every character in the world, which such stories usually
underuse.

## Exploits, edge cases, and interaction with other mechanics

The obvious edge case is a protagonist who onboards faster than anyone else
in the world for no stated reason. If the system is teachable, someone has
taught it before, and the story should know why this character learns
differently — a real answer is usually more interesting than the tutorial. Onboarding also interacts directly with stat-block density: the
first appearances of an interface teach the reader how to read it, and a
story that front-loads dense panels is training the reader to skim exactly
the element it will later rely on.

## Questions for the author

- What is the smallest set of rules a reader needs to follow chapter one?
- Is each mechanic introduced at the first moment it matters, or before?
- Does the reader learn this rule from an explanation or from a consequence?
- Who in this world already knows all of this, and why is the protagonist
  being told instead of having learned?
- At what chapter does the reader know enough to predict the system? Is that
  where the story wanted it?

## Revision or design experiments

1. List each mechanic and the chapter where it is first explained and first
   used. Gaps between explanation and use are candidates for deferral.
2. Take one explained rule and replace the explanation with a scene where
   getting it wrong costs something.
3. Cut the first system explanation in half and see whether chapter three
   still reads. Comprehension survives more cutting than drafts assume.
4. If a companion exists mainly to explain, give them one objective of their
   own and see whether the explanations survive the change.

## When this advice does not apply

- Systems-heavy work whose readers arrive for detailed mechanics and treat
  the specification as a pleasure rather than an obstacle. A real readership
  and a real contract.
- Later books in a series, where the audience is fluent and re-onboarding is
  the greater risk.
- Stories where the system's opacity is the point, and a reader's confusion
  is a designed experience rather than a failure of teaching.
- GameLit and adjacent subgenres with no explicit interface, where there is
  little to teach.

## Evidence and detection limits

A coach can locate where system terms first appear, where structured
interface text occurs, how dense those passages are, and the interval
between a term's introduction and its next use. That gap is close to
deterministic where terms are used consistently.

What it cannot determine is whether an explanation was needed, whether a
reader was confused, or whether a passage read as a dump. A long early
explanation may be a genuine problem or a deliberate contract with a
systems-loving readership. The coach should describe the shape of the
teaching and ask what the author intended it to feel like.

## Original micro-examples

*Explained:* "Mana regenerates at a rate determined by your Attunement,
modified by rest quality and ambient density. Attunement rises with..." —
four paragraphs before anyone has wanted mana.

*Learned:* He spent everything he had on the second wolf and stood there
afterward with his hands shaking, unable to make even a spark, while the
third one watched him from the treeline. Nobody had to tell him what
regeneration rate meant after that.

## Sources and confidence notes

Jesse Schell ([[src.book.schell-art-of-game-design]]) supplies the treatment
of teaching a system through play rather than instruction; Raph Koster
([[src.book.koster-theory-of-fun]]) supplies the account of learning as the
source of engagement, which underpins the argument against wholesale
explanation; Ernest Adams
([[src.book.adams-fundamentals-of-game-design]]) supplies the design
vocabulary for progressive disclosure and tutorial structure.

`source_confidence` is `mixed` in the handoff's second sense. All three
sources independently support the game-design principles. Their transfer to
prose is this drafting pass's synthesis, and carries a disanalogy worth
stating: a player must act to proceed, while a reader only reads, so
"learning by doing" becomes "learning by consequence" — a weaker mechanism
than the sources describe. The systemic observations follow this library's
own consequences reasoning, not a cited source.
