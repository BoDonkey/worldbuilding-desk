# Chapter Five — The Hollow Court

They took Brannic on a Thursday, between the Compact hall and his own doorstep, in the hundred quiet paces where the terrace lamps had all, that one evening, gone unaccountably dark.

Tam brought her the news at a dead run, still carrying his lighting-pole. The lamps on Cooper's Walk had been doused with sea-water, he said — all nine of them, wicks drowned in their cups, and no lamplighter in Grayharbor would ever. And on the cobbles halfway along, Warden Halloway's cane, lying in the dark like a dropped word.

The Compact posted search parties along the terraces. Sera went to the Weighing House instead, because she had stopped believing in coincidence somewhere in the middle of Chapter Four of her life, and because Odessa's corner office had a back door and the back door was standing open.

The office had been searched by professionals — nothing broken, everything moved a finger-width. Odessa sat amid it in her chair with a cut on her cheekbone and her lenses snapped clean in half on the desk, and she was, Sera realized with a lurch, laughing. Quietly. Furiously.

"Three of them," she said. "Polite as bailiffs. They knew the cabinet, girl. They knew the seal-wheel, they knew the weight-tag, and they knew to be gone before the Watch bells." She opened her right hand. The little brass tag lay in her palm, its cord cut. "They took the impression of it. Wax. Which means somewhere on these terraces tonight, someone is filing a copy of my tag, and by tomorrow's tide the deep vault is theirs to sign from like honest customers."

"Then the Key—"

"The Key is paper now, same as the Compact's rights. Papers burn." Odessa stood, and for a small round woman she filled the wrecked office like a squall. "They have Bran because the vault needs two signatures and they only troubled to forge one. You they'll come for sweetly — you're the Ledgerbound, you're the map, you're the other name on the ledger. Salt-white hair will make you an offer with your warden's life on the scale."

The offer arrived within the hour, by a boy too frightened to know what he carried: a fine paper, a fine hand.

*The Court convenes below at the night's low tide. Bring your good sense. Your warden vouches for your attendance with his continued comfort. — C. Lash*

---

The tide went out at two bells after midnight, and Sera Kestrel went down with it, alone.

The cliff stair in the dark was a conversation with her own death, one worn bowl of stone at a time. No wardens at the head of it — reassigned, all of them, on orders no one would later own. The sea drew back below her, and the Salt Door stood open at the bottom of the world, and lantern-light that was not wardlight moved in the antechamber like something feverish.

The Hollow Court did not look like broadsheet villains. That was the worst of it. They looked like the Weighing House on a busy day — clerks, appraisers, a surveyor unrolling her own stolen maps across the dais with professional appreciation, all of it lit by lamps burning an oil that smelled of nothing at all. Twelve of them. And Corvo Lash at the dark gate, white hair silvered by lamplight, one gloved hand resting on the shoulder of Brannic Halloway, who sat on a salvage crate with his hands bound and his face a mask of contained fury that broke, for one instant, into naked fear when he saw her come through the Door.

"Sera, no—"

"Ledgerbound." Corvo Lash smiled his exact smile. "Punctual as a tide table. We are all admiration here — truly, the maps are the finest survey work the Court has bought in a century, and we didn't even have to buy them." He gestured, courteous, at the gate. "One service, and everyone climbs home. The lock answers the Key, but the Vault below, we find, answers only the Marked. Open the way to the well, walk down with us, and your warden's account closes in full."

"And if I don't, you'll drown him."

"We're not brutes." He looked genuinely pained. "The tide will drown him. We merely schedule."

Sera came down the antechamber slowly, one hand loose at her side, the other pressed to her coat pocket, and her mind running colder and clearer than it had any right to. Twelve of them. One narrow gate. One warden who could not run. One knife at her left hip that opened things which should stay shut, and one shard of orange glass, warm as a banked fire against her hip, that she had carried since the antechamber and never once let out of arm's reach.

She stopped at the gate. She pressed the Emberglass Key into the lock, and the orange light ran out through the hairline channels, and the little door swung inward on eleven centuries of silence.

"There," she said. "Open. Now the second half of your problem."

Corvo Lash raised one pale eyebrow.

"The Vault answers the Marked," Sera said. She held up her wrist, and in the Court's odorless lamplight the marks shone pewter-bright, and she reached — the way she had reached in the gallery, but wider, the way the well at the avenue's end had breathed — down into all that black cold standing below their feet, and asked it, politely, to notice the twelve unmarked strangers standing at its door.

Far down in the dark, something the size of a city exhaled.

The channels in the antechamber floor lit ember-orange, every one, root to rim. The gray haze came off the walls of the stair below the gate all at once, in a wave, like a held breath released, and the Hollow Court's lamps began — clerkishly, apologetically, one after another — to go out.

"What," said Corvo Lash, in the new dark, and for the first time his voice held no weather at all, "have you done?"

"Scheduled," said Sera Kestrel, and went for Brannic and the knife at the same time.
