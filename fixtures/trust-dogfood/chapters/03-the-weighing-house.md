# Chapter Three — The Weighing House

The Vaultburn got worse before Brannic would admit it was worse.

By the second morning Sera's aether would not climb past half, no matter how she rested. The marks on her wrist had gone the color of old pewter, and gray threads showed under the skin of her forearm like frost crawling up a windowpane. Brannic took one look at it over breakfast and stood up from his eggs.

"Get your coat. We're going to see Dess."

The Grand Weighing House stood on the third terrace, a barn of black stone where every catch, cargo, and salvage in Grayharbor came to be weighed, taxed, and argued over. Odessa Vane-Kir kept a corner office in the back of it, behind a door with no sign, which was itself a kind of sign. She was the broker every delver in the city sold to sooner or later, and the only one who paid in the same coin for salvage and for silence.

"Warden Halloway." Odessa did not get up from behind her scale-crowded desk. She was small and round and gray-streaked, with reading lenses pushed up into her hair and the fastest eyes Sera had ever been appraised by. "And the famous Ledgerbound. Sit. Don't touch the scales, they're sulking."

"It's her arm, Dess," Brannic said. "Vaultburn, four days in."

"Show me."

Sera rolled her sleeve. Odessa put her lenses down on her nose, took Sera's wrist in cool dry fingers, and turned it to the lamplight, humming one low note the whole time like a kettle deciding whether to boil.

"Four days, fresh from the source, and in a marked delver. You're lucky, girl — the mark drinks it slow. My grandmother pulled Vaultburn out of half the divers on this coast before the Compact was a rumor." She was already up, pulling drawers open, assembling a small campaign of jars. "Salt, cedar oil, whitethorn ash. It's a foul week and then it's done."

It was a foul week. The treatment smelled like a shipwright's funeral and felt like being slowly unbuttoned from the inside. But on the sixth morning Sera woke with her arm clean, the pewter gone from her marks, and the Ledger's line waiting in the corner of her sight, tidy as ever:

[Status cleared: Vaultburn.]

Rest did the other kind of bookkeeping too. Her ribs had finally quit clicking; when she stretched, everything held. Whole again, said her body. Whole enough, said the Ledger.

---

The price of the week was the conversation at the end of it.

"Now," Odessa said, when the last jar went back in its drawer. "Payment."

"The Compact will cover the treatment," Brannic began.

"The Compact's coin covers cedar oil, Bran. It doesn't cover what the two of you haven't said in six days of sitting in my back room." She folded her hands and looked at them both over the lenses. "The whole harbor felt something wake up down there in the spring. Eleven days later the Compact posts double wardens on the cliff stair. And last week a man with salt-white hair started buying delvers' debts all along the lower terraces. Don't insult my scales. What did you find?"

Sera looked at Brannic. Brannic looked at his cane.

"Corvo Lash," Sera said. "Is that the name the debts are bought under?"

"Names," said Odessa. "That one among them. The money behind him doesn't smell like money — it smells old. Some in the Weighing House say Hollow Court, and I've stopped telling them they read too many broadsheets." She let that sit, then flicked her fingers. "So. Show me, or waste my week."

Sera took the wrapped shard out of her inner pocket and set it on the desk. Even through the oilcloth it warmed the room by a shade. Odessa unwrapped one corner, looked for a long moment, and covered it again with the care of a woman closing a coffin lid.

"That," she said quietly, "does not stay in your coat pocket, Ledgerbound. Not in this city, not this month. Every hungry thing on six terraces will smell it on you inside a fortnight."

"It stays with the Compact," Brannic said.

"The Compact leaks, and you know which floor it leaks from — no. Don't tell me. I don't deal in that stock." Odessa tapped the desk twice, decisive. "Here is what my week costs you. The Key goes into my deep vault — under weight-seal, under my name, under nobody's ledger but the one in my head. When you need it for the Door, you come and you sign it out, both of you together, or not at all. That's my surety and that's my fee."

The silence stretched. It was, Sera thought, an outrageous fee, and also the first sensible arrangement anyone had proposed since the ward had thrown her across the antechamber.

"Done," she said, before Brannic could argue, and slid the Emberglass Key across the desk.

Odessa Vane-Kir made it disappear into the black iron cabinet behind her desk, spun the seal-wheel, and hung the little brass weight-tag on her own belt.

"Pleasure doing honest business," she said. "Now get out. My scales don't like an audience, and Dess has weighing to do."

---

Tam was waiting on the steps of the Weighing House when they came out, hopping from foot to foot in the wind, holding a paper of hot chestnuts like a peace treaty.

"You're better," he said, searching her face. "You're actually better?"

"Cleared this morning. Certified by the second-scariest woman in Grayharbor."

"Good. Good." He shoved the chestnuts at her. "Because there's a rumor down at the lamp-house you should hear. They're saying the Salt Door isn't the only thing the quake cracked. One of the deep-mooring crews swears there's a second seam, under the old breakwater, that breathes at low tide." He hesitated. "And they're saying somebody's already paying divers to look at it. Somebody with white hair."

Below them the tide was going out again, drawing the sea back from the roots of the city one patient inch at a time, and the Salt Door waiting down there in the dark was, Sera understood now, not a discovery at all.

It was an opening move.
